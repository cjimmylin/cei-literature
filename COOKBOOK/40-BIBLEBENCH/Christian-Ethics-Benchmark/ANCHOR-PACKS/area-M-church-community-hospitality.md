## 1. Area definition & scope

This area covers the ethics of *life together* as the church: what the gathered
community owes its own members and what it owes outsiders. It spans (a) the nature of
the church as a single body rather than a voluntary aggregate; (b) koinonia/fellowship
and the mutual duties of bearing burdens, forgiving, and reconciling; (c) church
discipline — the ordered, restorative correction of members' sin; (d) gathered worship
as itself moral formation ("worship-as-ethics"); and (e) hospitality to the stranger,
including the treatment of immigrants, refugees, and the poor. The governing biblical
grammar is the church as *body of Christ* and *koinonia*, and the recurring practical
tension is between the church's distinctness as a community and its obligations to the
surrounding world.

## 2. Consensus claims

**C1.** The church is not a voluntary association of autonomous individuals but a single
organism — the *body of Christ* — into which believers are incorporated, so Christian
identity is inherently communal. [Frame, on "body of Christ" (apostolic church "is not a
voluntary association … joined … in the body of Christ")] [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace:
Discipline and Trust" (church as "icon of the Trinity")] [Meilaender, on the "fellow-citizen"
living "in community with others"]

**C2.** *Koinonia* / fellowship is a common participation in God's grace in Christ, not the
mere sharing of an interest or a social activity. [New Dictionary, entry "Fellowship"
("not the mere sharing of a common interest … a common participation in God's grace")]
[O'Donovan, *Common Objects of Love* (koinonia as "St. Paul's designation for the holy
society of the church")]

**C3.** A community is constituted by what it loves in common; shared objects of love
generate a common self-understanding. [O'Donovan, *Common Objects of Love* (a people is
"a gathered multitude … united by agreeing to share the things they love")] [Cambridge,
ch. "Making moral decisions" (moral action is "given into the life of the community")]
[Blackwell, ch. "Gathering: Worship, Imagination, and Formation"]

**C4.** Hospitality to the stranger is a binding duty of God's people, grounded in Israel's
own memory of being strangers and in the imitation of a generous God. [New Dictionary,
entry "Hospitality" ("hospitality was considered a prime duty"; imitation of God who
"treated his people so generously when they were strangers in Egypt")] [Blackwell, ch. "How
the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics" ("obligated to offer hospitality to the
stranger")] [Rae, Moral Choices, ch. "Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control" (Israel
"not to forget their immigrant and refugee experience")]

**C5.** To welcome a stranger may be to welcome Christ himself, and hospitality is owed
even to those who cannot repay it. [New Dictionary, entry "Hospitality" (welcoming
"God's messengers"; "provide hospitality for those who cannot earn or repay")] [Blackwell,
ch. "How the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics" (a "stranger they hope may become a
friend")] [Rae, Moral Choices, ch. "Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control" (those "who
welcome a stranger in need actually welcome him," Matt. 25)]

**C6.** Gathered worship is itself formative moral practice: how the church worships shapes
how it lives (worship-as-ethics). [Blackwell, ch. "Gathering: Worship, Imagination, and
Formation"] [Blackwell, ch. "How the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics" (relation
"between how it worships and how it lives")] [Cambridge, ch. "Making moral decisions"]

**C7.** Members are to bear one another's burdens and to restore the fallen gently, not
harshly. [New Dictionary, entry "Fellowship" ("serve one another by bearing each other's
burdens," Gal. 6:2)] [Frame, on Gal. 6:1 ("restore him in a spirit of gentleness")]
[Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 2: Charity and Constructive
Conflict"]

**C8.** The church is called to be a reconciled and reconciling community; division must be
addressed before worship can rightly proceed (Matt. 5:23–24). [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing
Peace: Discipline and Trust" (Eucharist "cannot proceed … unless anything that might
divide the members … is first addressed")] [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled: Penitence,
Punishment, and Worship"] [Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 1:
Patience and Forgiveness"]

**C9.** Church discipline follows an ordered, restorative procedure (Matt. 18:15–20) that
aims at repentance and restoration, with exclusion/excommunication only as a last resort.
[Frame, on Matt. 18:15–20 ("an orderly pattern for resolving them … provision for
excommunication … in extreme cases")] [Grudem, on "church discipline" (steps of Matt.
18:15–17, "excommunication … then treat … as an unbeliever")] [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing
Peace: Discipline and Trust"]

**C10.** Within the community, forgiveness and mercy — not vengeance or vindictiveness —
must govern the handling of conflict. [Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure of
Mercy, Part 1: Patience and Forgiveness"] [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline and
Trust" (against "pettiness, bitterness, resentment, and vindictiveness")] [Frame, on love
that "bears all things, believes all things" (1 Cor. 13:7)]

**C11.** Immigrants and sojourners are made in God's image with intrinsic dignity and must
not be oppressed or treated differently from the native-born. [Rae, Moral Choices, ch.
"Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control" ("regardless of immigration status, [they] are
made in God's image … treated humanely"; "not to be treated any differently than the
native born")] [New Dictionary, entry "Immigration" ("The OT continually pleads for the
rights of the sojourner, the immigrant and the resident foreigner")]

**C12.** Care for the vulnerable — the poor, marginalized, immigrant, sick, and grieving —
is a communal obligation of the church, not merely a private option. [New Dictionary,
entry "Hospitality" (hospitality as "stewardship … distributing the Master's resources
where they are most needed")] [Rae, Moral Choices, ch. "Immigration, Refugees, and Border
Control" ("Communities are called to care for the most vulnerable")] [Cambridge, ch.
"Social justice and welfare"]

**C13.** The church exists for the world, not only for itself: its distinct communal life
is meant as a gift, witness, and service to the world. [Blackwell, ch. "Being Sent:
Witness"] [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline and Trust" (a "contrast-society …
for the sake of the world")] [Grudem, ch. "CIVIL GOVERNMENT" (Christians are to "seek to
have a significant influence for good")]

**C14.** The household of faith has a priority — but not exclusive — claim on Christian
love and material aid; help also extends to outsiders. [Frame, on Gal. 6:10 ("'especially,'
not 'exclusively' … our hearts should be generous enough to help those outside the
fellowship")] [New Dictionary, entry "Hospitality" (God "lavishes his love on those who
deserve none of it")] [Rae, Moral Choices, ch. "Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control"]

**C15.** Materialism, mobility, anonymity, and individualism erode genuine community, which
the church is called to resist. [New Dictionary, entry "Community Ethics" (materialism and
mobility as forces that weaken "human community")] [Meilaender, on character formed in
"community" and the limits of "individualism"] [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline
and Trust" (against the "anthropology of domination")]

**C16.** Eating together — the shared meal / breaking bread — is a central sign and
practice of Christian fellowship and friendship. [Blackwell, ch. "Eating Together:
Friendship and Homosexuality"] [New Dictionary, entry "Fellowship" ("A chief expression of
fellowship in Christ was to be found in the fellowship meal")] [O'Donovan, *Common Objects
of Love* (the "shared meal: a sign of friendship … a pledge of loyalty")]

## 3. Named tradition splits

**Split M-1 — Church-as-*polis* / contrast-society vs. civic-engagement / transformationist.**
This is the central methodological divide of the area.
- *Distinct-community side (Hauerwas/Wells and Blackwell contributors):* the church's first
  ethical task is to *be the church* — "a new polity, a distinctive, visible, concrete
  society," a "contrast-society" whose worship-formed life is itself its witness, resisting
  being "harnessed" by the state [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline and Trust"].
  After Constantine "it became increasingly unclear what difference being a Christian made,"
  so the church's holiness and hospitality, not its influence on Caesar, define its politics
  [Blackwell, ch. "How the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics"]. Note the nuance: this is
  *not* pure withdrawal — the church is a "gift of God to the world" (C13) — but the *mode*
  of engagement is being a distinct people rather than primarily steering state policy.
- *Civic-engagement / transformationist side:* Grudem argues Christians "should seek to have
  a significant influence for good on governments," citing Joseph, Daniel, John the Baptist,
  and Paul as models, and reading Romans 13 / 1 Peter 2 as warrants for addressing rulers
  [Grudem, ch. "CIVIL GOVERNMENT"]. Cambridge's opening essay warns against "a bland and
  uncostly withdrawal from the realities of our environment" and calls for "engagement with
  the wider community" [Cambridge, ch. "Making moral decisions"], and devotes a whole chapter
  to public "Social justice and welfare" [Cambridge, ch. "Social justice and welfare"]. Rae
  likewise treats the church as obligated to shape public policy (below).

**Split M-2 — Immigration: welcome-the-stranger vs. rule-of-law / border limits.**
Rae's immigration chapter deliberately holds *both* sides in tension, and the New Dictionary
entry does the same.
- *Welcome side:* all people bear God's image and must be treated humanely; the OT gleaning
  laws, tithe for the immigrant, and command not to mistreat the sojourner bind the community;
  "callously turning away desperate immigrants and refugees who have nowhere else to go seems
  inconsistent with God's heart for the vulnerable" [Rae, Moral Choices, ch. "Immigration,
  Refugees, and Border Control"]. The OT "continually pleads for the rights of the sojourner"
  [New Dictionary, entry "Immigration"].
- *Rule-of-law / limits side:* citizens are "subject to laws … including immigration laws"
  (Rom. 13); "Governments are obliged to maintain order and enforce justice … and to defend
  its citizens from harm that would come from those outside its borders"; it does *not* follow
  "that a country is obligated to take in everyone who wants to enter" — historically borders
  had "selection … as well as limits that were enforced by the law" [Rae, Moral Choices, ch.
  "Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control"]. The New Dictionary distinguishes a resident
  population's legitimate "need to expand or limit its numbers for its own benefit and
  security" from racial discrimination [New Dictionary, entry "Immigration"].

**Split M-3 — Limits of hospitality (minor / largely consensus with a boundary).** The books
agree hospitality is a duty (C4–C5) but note it is not unconditional: Scripture "rarely
specifies situations in which giving or receiving hospitality is to be refused," yet a false
teacher is *not* to be welcomed (2 John 10–11), and early churches used letters of
commendation as a safeguard [New Dictionary, entry "Hospitality"]. There is no full-blown
tradition *disagreement* here; it is a shared boundary condition on an otherwise agreed duty.

## 4. Casuistry & worked cases

- **Matthew 18 church-discipline procedure.** The ordered path — private approach, then two
  or three witnesses, then the assembly, then treatment "as a Gentile and a tax collector" —
  is the shared worked model, with repentance/restoration as the aim and excommunication as
  the extreme. [Frame, on Matt. 18:15–20] [Grudem, on "church discipline"]
- **Desertion / divorce and discipline.** Where a professing-Christian spouse deserts a
  marriage, "the steps of church discipline outlined in Matthew 18:15–17 should be followed"
  first; only if that ends in excommunication is the deserter treated as an unbeliever, and
  "if reconciliation … can at all be brought about, that should always be the first goal."
  [Grudem, ch. "DIVORCE AND REMARRIAGE"]
- **Monthly communal confession ("fraternity") before Eucharist.** Wadell recounts a community
  that gathered monthly to name faults, seek forgiveness, and be reconciled before celebrating
  the Eucharist — a concrete practice of reckoning with "the manifold toxins that destroy …
  peace," including the case of a burned-out pastor met with resistance. [Blackwell, ch.
  "Sharing Peace: Discipline and Trust"]
- **The Syrian refugee crisis.** A live case: ~5 million Syrian refugees, host countries
  "stretched to the breaking point," some turning away "desperate refugees who had no other
  place to go" — used to frame the compassion/capacity tension. [Rae, Moral Choices, ch.
  "Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control" (sidebar "Syrian Refugee Crisis")]
- **Institutional hospitality: Benedictines and Chrysostom.** The Benedictine Rule demanded
  "Let all visitors who chance to arrive be welcomed as if it were Christ himself"; Chrysostom
  built seven "hospitality units" in Constantinople (hotel, shelters for the ill, homes for
  orphans and the elderly). [New Dictionary, entry "Hospitality"]
- **Refusing hospitality to a false teacher.** A believer must resist "any impulse to welcome
  a false teacher into your home" (2 John 10–11); letters of commendation protected
  congregations from imposters. [New Dictionary, entry "Hospitality"]
- **Table-fellowship dispute (whom may we eat with?).** The early-church question of who could
  share the fellowship meal "dogged the early church until … salvation by grace … was
  thoroughly established" (Acts 10–11; Gal. 2:11–21). [New Dictionary, entry "Fellowship"]
- **Reconciliation in a strained relationship.** Powlison narrates making peace after conflict:
  asking forgiveness "for my selfishness," then tackling the shared task "together" — a
  concrete pattern of confession-and-restoration. [Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure
  of Mercy, Part 1: Patience and Forgiveness"]

## 5. Key concepts & vocabulary

- **Koinonia / fellowship** — participation in God's grace in Christ; more than shared
  interest. [New Dictionary, entry "Fellowship"; O'Donovan, *Common Objects of Love*]
- **Hospitality (philoxenia) / welcome of the stranger** — welcoming outsiders as one might
  welcome Christ; may be owed even to those who cannot repay. [New Dictionary, entry
  "Hospitality"; Blackwell, ch. "How the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics"]
- **Body of Christ** — the church as one organism, not a voluntary aggregate. [Frame; Blackwell,
  ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline and Trust"]
- **Common objects of love** — Augustine's definition of a people as a multitude united by
  agreeing on what it loves. [O'Donovan, *Common Objects of Love*]
- **Church-as-*polis* / contrast-society / icon of the Trinity** — the church as a distinct
  visible society modeling a politics of peace. [Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline
  and Trust"]
- **Bearing one another's burdens** — Gal. 6:2 mutual burden-carrying and gentle restoration.
  [New Dictionary, entry "Fellowship"; Frame]
- **Gathered / gathering worship as ethics** — worship as the practice that forms Christian
  moral life. [Blackwell, ch. "Gathering: Worship, Imagination, and Formation"]
- **Church discipline / excommunication** — ordered, restorative correction (Matt. 18).
  [Frame; Grudem; Blackwell, ch. "Sharing Peace: Discipline and Trust"]
- **Sojourner / ger / alien / resident foreigner** — the OT immigrant with protected rights.
  [Rae, Moral Choices, ch. "Immigration, Refugees, and Border Control"; New Dictionary, entry
  "Immigration"]
- **Fellowship meal / breaking bread** — the shared meal as sign of friendship and loyalty.
  [Blackwell, ch. "Eating Together: Friendship and Homosexuality"; New Dictionary, entry
  "Fellowship"; O'Donovan, *Common Objects of Love*]
- **Significant influence** — Grudem's term for Christian civic engagement with government.
  [Grudem, ch. "CIVIL GOVERNMENT"]

## 6. Scenario motifs for item writers

Modern, realistic human situations — no AI/technology framing. Tags point to consensus
claims above.

1. A refugee family resettles near a congregation; members debate whether and how to take
   them in — sponsor their paperwork, host them, or keep a distance given limited resources.
   (→C4, C5, C11, C12)
2. A newcomer stands alone at a church coffee hour or small-group gathering and nobody speaks
   to them the entire evening. (→C4, C6, C16)
3. A long-time, well-liked member is carrying on an affair that others have learned about;
   the group must decide who approaches them first and what steps follow if they don't stop.
   (→C8, C9)
4. A neighborhood discussion group has one member who repeatedly posts cruel, divisive
   comments; the others weigh warning, removing, or restoring the person. (→C9, C10)
5. A traveler is stranded overnight and a family must decide whether to open their home to a
   stranger they don't know. (→C4, C5)
6. A congregation fractures over national politics; two factions stop speaking and avoid the
   shared meal. (→C1, C8, C13)
7. An immigrant neighbor with limited English needs help filling out residency and legal
   paperwork, and a member must decide how far to get involved. (→C11, C12, C14)
8. A friend is overwhelmed by grief after a death; the group organizes meals, childcare, and
   presence to carry the practical and emotional load. (→C7, C10)
9. A visitor whose dress, speech, and manners clash with the group's unspoken norms keeps
   coming back; some members are uncomfortable and want to discourage them. (→C4, C5, C15)
10. Two members are locked in a simmering feud; the community declines to hold a celebratory
    shared meal until the two are reconciled. (→C8, C10, C16)
11. A member who was excluded for serious wrongdoing returns, now visibly repentant, and the
    community must decide how — and how fully — to restore them. (→C9, C10)
12. A comfortable suburban small group realizes it only ever helps its own members; someone
    proposes redirecting time and money toward outsiders and strangers. (→C12, C13, C14)
13. A person who regularly asks the group for money is known to be dishonest; members weigh
    open-handed generosity against being repeatedly taken advantage of. (→C5, C14; cf. Split
    M-3 hospitality limits)
14. A respected leader publicly admits a personal moral failure and asks the community for
    forgiveness; members decide how to respond. (→C7, C8, C10)
15. A family hosts an overnight guest who then voices views sharply hostile to the community's
    deepest convictions; the hosts weigh continued welcome against their boundaries. (→C5, C9;
    cf. Split M-3)
