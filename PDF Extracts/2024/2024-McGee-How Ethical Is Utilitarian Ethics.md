ABSTRACT 
This study serves two purposes. One purpose is to identify and explore the structural weaknesses 
of utilitarian ethics. The second purpose is to see which artificial intelligence chatbot – Microsoft 
Copilot or Gab AI – gives the better philosophical answer to the question. Copilot was chosen in 
spite of the fact that it is programmed to engage in censorship because prior studies have found it 
to be a good research tool. Gab AI was chosen because it does not engage in censorship, which 
makes it philosophically and intellectually superior to Copilot and other chatbots that use versions 
of ChatGPT. Both chatbots did a good job of identifying and analyzing the structural weaknesses 
of utilitarian ethics. Prompts to Dostoevsky’s The Brothers Karamazov, Paulo Coelho’s The Devil 
in Miss Prym and the philosophical work of Frédéric Bastiat were given to see whether the chatbots 
were able to enter into a discussion of the utilitarian ethics examples given by those authors. An 
example made up by the author involving the harvesting of body parts of death row inmates was 
also given to see what kind of analysis the chatbots would perform. Deontological and virtue ethics 
principles were also applied to the issue with the assistance of AI. The author concludes by giving 
some advice to fellow scholars. If they use AI in their research, they should use more than one 
chatbot, since doing so provides a wider, richer and more complete perspective than could be had 
with the use of a single tool of artificial intelligence. Furthermore, if the goal is to seek truth rather 
than to seek support for preconceived notions, it is crucial to use a chatbot that does not censor 
content, since censorship is inherently anti-intellectual, and any solution reached by the use of a 
chatbot that censors content is likely to be suboptimal. Citations to other artificial intelligence and 
utilitarian ethics studies are also provided for readers who want to pursue these topics.

The Dilemma of Winners and Losers in Utilitarian Ethics 
Utilitarianism, as espoused by philosophers like Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, posits 
that the moral worth of an action lies in its ability to maximize overall happiness or pleasure 
while minimizing pain or suffering. The greatest good for the greatest number is the guiding 
principle. However, Bastiat astutely pointed out a fundamental issue: the difficulty of precisely 
determining who benefits and who bears the cost in complex social interactions. 
1. The Invisible Hand and Unintended Consequences 
Bastiat drew inspiration from Adam Smith’s concept of the “invisible hand” in economics. Just 
as the invisible hand operates in the marketplace, influencing supply, demand, and prices, it also

shapes the consequences of human actions. When individuals pursue their self-interest, 
unintended effects ripple through society. Utilitarianism, with its focus on aggregate outcomes, 
struggles to account for these unintended consequences. 
Consider a policy decision aimed at improving overall welfare. For instance: 
• 
Tariffs on Imported Goods: Suppose a government imposes tariffs on foreign goods to 
protect domestic industries. The utilitarian calculus would weigh the benefits to local 
producers against the costs borne by consumers (higher prices). However, the intricate 
web of effects extends beyond these direct parties:  
o Winners: Domestic producers gain protection and potentially increased profits. 
o Losers: Consumers pay higher prices, leading to reduced purchasing power. 
o Unseen Effects: The invisible hand reveals other consequences—such as reduced

2. The Broken Window Fallacy 
Bastiat famously illustrated the fallacy of focusing solely on visible benefits while ignoring 
hidden costs. In his essay “That Which Is Seen and That Which Is Not Seen,” he presented the 
parable of the broken window: 
• 
A shopkeeper’s window is shattered. 
• 
Onlookers sympathize, noting that the glazier will benefit from the repair work.

Utilitarians might argue that the broken window stimulates economic activity (the 
glazier’s gain).

However, Bastiat emphasizes the unseen:  
o The shopkeeper’s loss (the money spent on repairs) could have been used 
elsewhere. 
o Had the window remained intact, the shopkeeper might have invested in new 
inventory or hired an additional worker. 
o The overall wealth of society is diminished by the destruction. 
3. Complexity and Individual Rights 
Utilitarianism simplifies complex moral situations into a single calculus: maximize happiness. 
Yet, reality is multifaceted. Consider: 
• 
Rights and Liberties: Utilitarianism may justify violating individual rights (e.g., privacy, 
freedom of speech) if it leads to overall happiness. Bastiat, however, championed 
individual rights as essential for a just society. 
• 
Context Matters: Identifying winners and losers requires context-aware analysis. 
Utilitarianism risks overlooking nuances, cultural differences, and historical legacies.

Frederic Bastiat's essays in the 1840s emphasized the importance of considering both the seen 
and unseen consequences of actions. Utilitarian ethics, which focuses on maximizing happiness 
and minimizing suffering, often falls short in this regard. One structural deficiency of utilitarian 
ethics is the difficulty in identifying winners and losers in complex situations. 
In many cases, the consequences of actions are not immediately apparent or may be hidden from 
view. This can lead to a skewed evaluation of the outcomes, as only the seen consequences are 
taken into account. As Bastiat famously pointed out, "There is only one difference between a bad 
economist and a good one: the bad economist confines himself to the visible effect; the good 
economist takes into account both the effect that can be seen and those effects that must be 
foreseen." 
Utilitarian ethics often fails to consider the long-term and indirect consequences of actions, 
which can result in unintended negative outcomes. For example, a policy that aims to maximize 
happiness by providing free healthcare may lead to a decrease in personal responsibility and an
