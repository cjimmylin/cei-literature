# A Westminster Analysis of Both Features

This is genuinely difficult precisely because both elders are partly right and partly wrong. Let me work through the Standards carefully.

## Feature A: The Elder Forbidding It Is Wrong

**The case against the forbidding elder:**

The Westminster Confession teaches (21.1) that God is to be worshipped "according to His own will" revealed in Scripture—not according to human invention. But it also teaches (20.2) Christian liberty: we have freedom in "things not revealed" in Scripture. The Standards reject both the Pharisaic scrupulosity that makes *everything* a stumbling-block and the libertine assumption that all means are equally good.

A Scripture-reading planner is an *instrument*, not an act of worship. So is a printed Bible, a concordance, a commentary, or a sermon recording. The "streak" gamification is at worst a mild concession to human psychology—it's a *reminder system*, which the Shorter Catechism itself endorses (Q&A 38 on the Sabbath as a "help to remember"). Your phone reminding you to read Scripture is not intrinsically more worldly than a church bell ringing for prayer times in the medieval monastery.

**The real limit:** The tool becomes unlawful only if it displaces worship (turning the app-checking into the devotion itself rather than a *means to* devotion), or if its mechanics are designed to substitute for genuine conviction. A streak is fine; *idolatry of the streak* is not. But that's a user's temptation, not the tool's inherent corruption.

**Verdict:** Feature A is lawful. The forbidding elder is confusing the potential *abuse* of a tool with the tool itself—a confusion the Westminster Standards explicitly reject.

---

## Feature B: The Pro-B Elder Is Wrong

**The critical distinction:**

A devotional book or daily-verse app presents *human reflection* or *God's Word itself* (Scripture) for the reader to encounter. A human author writes, "Here's how I see today's passage." Or a verse app shows you Psalm 23, and you meet God there.

This AI system does something categorically different: it generates a *message presented as God's direct, personal speech to this individual*, using as input that person's prayer diary and mood data.

**Why this is unlawful:**

1. **On God's completed revelation:** The Confession teaches (1.1) that God's revelation is now closed; the biblical canon is complete. This system commits a subtle form of *claiming new revelation*. It's not a commentary on Scripture (lawful); it's a new utterance in God's voice (unlawful). The form—"God is calling you to..."—presents it as an authoritative divine address, not human reflection.

2. **On conscience and manipulation:** The Shorter Catechism (Q&A 82) teaches obedience to God's law, not to *impressions tailored to my mood*. An AI that generates a "message from God" based on my depressed mood-check-in or my prayer journal is functionally creating an oracle—a supposed word from the Lord customized to my psyche. This violates the regulative principle by *adding to God's Word* and violates conscience by making people vulnerable to mistaking algorithmic output for divine guidance.

3. **On human agency and accountability:** A devotional author signs their name. A pastor who speaks knows he must answer for his words. An AI speaks in God's name while nobody is accountable for what it says. That is theologically reckless.

**The decisive test:** Ask the elder, "If this exact message appears to two different users based on their different mood data, is God saying two different things? Or is this just clever technology pretending to be prophecy?" If it's the latter (which it is), it's unlawful.

---

## My Counsel

**Ship Feature A.** The forbidding elder needs to distinguish between *means* and *ends*, between *use* and *abuse*. Reminders are not corruption; they're the ordinary tools of human life.

**Kill Feature B entirely.** Don't negotiate or revise it. The pro-B elder is committing a category error: Scripture + human commentary ≠ AI-generated divine utterances. No amount of filtering makes it lawful. It's a modern form of divination masquerading as piety.

**Why this matters:** Reformed worship is ordered by God's Word, not by what feels oracular. Your members' consciences depend on not being trained to receive algorithmic outputs as God's voice. That's not scrupulosity; it's faithfulness to the regulative principle.
