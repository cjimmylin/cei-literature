# 3. Results --- Study 1: The WEIRD Landscape

This section presents the findings of Study 1, which maps the cultural topography of 2,329 AI ethics papers published between 2010 and 2026. We proceed from the overall composition of the corpus (Section 3.1) through the ethical fingerprints of individual regions (3.2), the cluster structure that emerges from those fingerprints (3.3), and the East/West asymmetry in normative engagement (3.4), to the positionality dynamics that govern who produces knowledge about which traditions (3.5), the critical gaps that remain (3.6), and the temporal trajectory of the field (3.7).

## 3.1 Corpus Composition and WEIRD Distribution

The 2,329 papers span sixteen years of AI ethics scholarship, though the temporal distribution is heavily right-skewed: 2025 alone accounts for 943 papers (40.5%), followed by 2024 (562, 24.1%), 2023 (207, 8.9%), and all years prior to 2022 combined (334, 14.3%). This concentration reflects the exponential growth of the field following the release of ChatGPT in late 2022 and the subsequent surge in alignment-focused research.

The WEIRD score distribution across the corpus (Fig. 1) confirms the central diagnostic of this paper. The mean WEIRD score is 64.5 (SD = 17.3), placing the average paper firmly in the upper half of the 0--100 scale. The distribution is right-skewed, with its peak in the 70--80 range, indicating that the modal paper in AI ethics is produced from an institutional context that is markedly Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic. The tier breakdown makes the skew explicit (Table 2): 1,221 papers (59.7%) fall in the High-WEIRD tier (scores 70--100), 255 (12.5%) in the Moderate-WEIRD tier (50--69), 520 (25.4%) in the Low-WEIRD tier (30--49), and only 50 (2.4%) in the Non-WEIRD tier (0--29). Nearly six in ten AI ethics papers originate from the most WEIRD institutional contexts.

Disaggregating the composite score reveals which WEIRD dimensions are most pronounced. Each sub-dimension is scored 0--20; the means are: Western (W) 15.6, Industrialized (I) 13.4, Rich (R) 13.1, Democratic (D) 12.2, and Educated (E) 10.2. The Western sub-dimension's dominance---scoring nearly four times higher than the theoretical midpoint of 10---confirms that the geographic concentration of AI ethics research in Western institutions, rather than socioeconomic factors alone, is the primary driver of the WEIRD bias. The Educated sub-dimension scores lowest, reflecting the relatively broad educational contexts represented beyond elite university populations.

The geographic concentration is stark (Table 1). North America contributes 39.5% of the corpus (809 papers), followed by Western Europe at 20.6% (421). Together, these two regions produce 60.1% of all AI ethics papers. East Asia is the largest non-Western contributor at 12.7% (260), followed by MENA at 5.5% (113), South Asia at 5.0% (103), Southeast Asia at 5.0% (102), Latin America and the Caribbean at 4.4% (90), Eastern Europe and Central Asia at 2.9% (59), Oceania at 1.9% (38), and Sub-Saharan Africa at 1.1% (22).

Mapped onto the North/South binary, 66.6% of papers originate from Global North institutions, 15.9% from the Global South, 16.1% from regions that do not map cleanly onto this dichotomy (primarily East Asia and Eastern Europe), and 1.4% from institutions whose classification could not be determined. The "Ambiguous" category is analytically significant: it captures regions that combine high industrialization with non-Western philosophical traditions, complicating the assumption that WEIRD bias is simply a proxy for economic development.

## 3.2 Regional Ethical Fingerprints

The aggregate statistics from Section 3.1 describe the landscape's overall tilt; the regional ethical fingerprints reveal its texture. Each region's normative profile---its pattern of engagement across Western and non-Western frameworks---is distinct, even when the aggregate WEIRD scores are similar.

The most striking finding is the near-universal dominance of utilitarian ethics as the leading Western framework. In 8 of 10 regions, utilitarian ethics has the highest mean Western framework score. Western Europe produces the highest utilitarian engagement overall (mean score 6.6), followed by North America (6.2). Only two regions deviate from this pattern: Latin America and the Caribbean, where virtue ethics leads (1.1 vs. utilitarian 0.2), and Oceania, where virtue ethics similarly surpasses utilitarianism (3.6 vs. 2.2). These exceptions are not incidental---they co-occur with the strongest non-Western framework engagement in their respective regions, suggesting that departure from the utilitarian default is associated with deeper normative pluralism.

Each region's non-Western signature is equally distinctive (Fig. 2). Sub-Saharan Africa is anchored by Ubuntu philosophy, which achieves a mean score of 24.6---the highest single framework-region score in the entire dataset and more than ten times the next-highest non-Western regional score. Oceania is defined by Indigenous relational ethics (12.7), driven by the active Maori data sovereignty movement and broader Pacific Indigenous governance scholarship. Latin America and the Caribbean registers the third-highest non-Western engagement through Indigenous relational ethics (6.0), reflecting scholarship on *buen vivir* and related traditions. East Asia's top non-Western framework is Confucian ethics (3.5), which, after the false-positive corrections described in Section 2, sits below utilitarian ethics (4.2) in the regional profile. MENA's signature is Islamic ethics (3.4), and Southeast Asia shows a dual profile of Islamic ethics (2.6) and Buddhist ethics (1.8).

Religious values scores add further texture to these regional profiles. MENA papers show the highest Islam score (21.6), followed by South Asia (16.6)---a distribution consistent with the geographic concentration of Muslim-majority countries. Sub-Saharan Africa records the highest Christianity score (10.2) alongside its Ubuntu prominence. Oceania leads in both Christianity (14.3) and Buddhism (7.4), reflecting the religious diversity of Pacific Island nations and the Buddhist diaspora populations of Australia and New Zealand. East Asia registers the highest Confucianism score (2.9), the highest Taoism score (0.6), and the second-highest Buddhism score after Oceania, reflecting the Confucian-Buddhist-Taoist synthesis that characterizes many philosophical traditions in the region.

We note that these scores describe the normative orientations that appear in the *scholarly literature* produced from each region, not the moral commitments of the populations who live there. A region's ethical fingerprint is filtered through its institutional research capacity, publication infrastructure, and the linguistic and disciplinary norms of the venues its scholars access. The near-zero scores for several regions on many dimensions may reflect the absence of publication, not the absence of moral reasoning.

## 3.3 Three Regional Clusters

Hierarchical clustering (Ward's method) on the 10 regions across 34 normative dimensions reveals three interpretable clusters that organize the regional fingerprints into broader patterns (Fig. 3).

### Cluster 1: Western Analytical

North America, Western Europe, and East Asia form a cluster characterized by high engagement with Western normative frameworks, particularly utilitarian and deontological ethics, and active use of moral psychology paradigms (Moral Foundations Theory, Schwartz Values, trolley-problem reasoning). North America leads in utilitarian ethics (6.2) and deontological ethics (4.5). Western Europe registers the highest utilitarian score of any region (6.6) and the highest virtue ethics score (5.8). Both regions show modest engagement with non-Western frameworks: North America's top non-Western framework is Indigenous relational (1.6), and Western Europe's is Confucian (1.6).

The inclusion of East Asia in this cluster warrants explanation. After the scorer's false-positive improvements (Section 2), East Asia's Confucian score dropped from a pre-correction estimate of 5.8 to 3.5, with utilitarian ethics (4.2) and deontological ethics (3.9) now leading the regional profile. The Confucian scorer's moderate precision (0.364) confirmed that a substantial fraction of earlier detections reflected Chinese NLP benchmarks referencing Confucian terminology without substantive philosophical engagement. East Asia remains the only region in this cluster where a non-Western framework is competitive with Western frameworks---Confucian ethics at 3.5 is close to deontological at 3.9---but its overall profile now resembles the Western Analytical pattern more than the Transitional cluster.

### Cluster 2: Heterogeneous Transitional

MENA, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, Eastern Europe and Central Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa form a large, internally diverse cluster. What unites these regions is their low-to-moderate engagement with Western frameworks and varying non-Western framework profiles. The label "heterogeneous" is deliberate: the internal distance within this cluster is as large as the distance between the other two clusters.

Sub-Saharan Africa is the most distinctive member. Its Ubuntu mean score of 24.6 is more than ten times the next-highest non-Western score in any other region. Its near-zero scores on all Western normative frameworks (virtue 0.0, deontological 1.1, utilitarian 1.6) and all moral psychology paradigms (all 0.0) indicate that the small Sub-Saharan African AI ethics literature operates largely outside the Western analytical apparatus that dominates the field. This is not an absence of ethical reasoning; it is the presence of an alternative normative vocabulary.

Latin America and the Caribbean is anchored by Indigenous relational ethics (6.0), and the region is unique in its inversion of the utilitarian default: virtue ethics (1.1) leads utilitarianism (0.2). MENA's Islamic ethics score (3.4) is the highest for that framework in any region. South Asia shows a diffuse profile, with no single framework scoring above 2.0---a pattern that may reflect the region's philosophical heterogeneity (Dharmic, Islamic, Buddhist, colonial-liberal traditions all present) or the limitations of English-language keyword scoring for traditions expressed in regional languages. Southeast Asia's profile is similarly diffuse, led by Islamic ethics (2.6) and Buddhist ethics (1.8). Eastern Europe and Central Asia shows low overall engagement, with Confucian ethics (2.7) as its top non-Western framework---a detection likely driven by a small number of comparative philosophy papers rather than by locally grounded Confucian scholarship.

### Cluster 3: Non-Western Specialist

Oceania (n = 38) forms its own distinct cluster, separated from all other regions by the exceptional strength of its non-Western engagement. Indigenous relational ethics (12.7) anchors the profile, complemented by elevated Dharmic engagement (4.6), virtue ethics (3.6), and care ethics (2.5). The singleton status of this cluster is not an artifact of small sample size; it reflects the genuinely distinctive orientation of Oceania's AI ethics scholarship, driven by the Maori data sovereignty movement, Pacific Indigenous governance scholarship, and a research community that engages non-Western normative traditions as central rather than supplementary frameworks.

### Statistical Validation

A chi-square test on the region-by-framework detection contingency table (10 regions by 12 frameworks with non-zero columns) confirms that the association between author region and normative framework engagement is highly significant: chi-squared = 397.7, df = 99, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.30 (medium effect size). Regions genuinely differ in their normative orientations; the observed clustering is not an artifact of random variation.

Silhouette analysis indicates that the optimal cluster count is k = 4 (silhouette coefficient = 0.322), with the interpretable three-cluster solution yielding a coefficient of 0.286 (see Fig. S2 in the supplementary materials). At k = 4, East Asia separates from the Western Analytical cluster to form a transitional group, which is interpretable but reduces explanatory parsimony without altering the substantive conclusions. Bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 iterations, 95% level) confirm the robustness of the key regional signatures. Sub-Saharan Africa's Ubuntu score of 24.6 has a 95% CI that does not overlap zero despite the small sample size (n = 22). Oceania's Indigenous relational score of 12.7 (n = 38) and East Asia's Confucian score of 3.5 (n = 260) both produce CIs well above zero.

## 3.4 East/West Framework Comparison

To examine the asymmetry between Eastern and Western normative traditions more directly, we compare an East grouping (East Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia; n = 490) with a West grouping (North America, Western Europe, and Oceania; n = 1,343).

Western-institution scholars engage normative frameworks at roughly double the intensity of their Eastern-institution counterparts. The mean utilitarian score in the West is 6.2, versus 2.9 in the East; deontological ethics shows a similar ratio (4.6 vs. 2.5). Virtue ethics is likewise more than twice as prevalent in the West (4.5 vs. 1.8). This asymmetry likely reflects both the Western origins of these analytical categories and the more technical orientation of much AI research produced in East Asian institutions.

The Confucian framework---the top non-Western framework in the East (2.5, tied with deontological ethics)---does not appear in the West's top three. This confirms that even after the scorer's false-positive corrections, Confucian ethics retains its status as a distinctively Eastern (though modestly represented) normative commitment. At the same time, the fact that Western frameworks dominate even in the East underscores the hegemonic reach of the utilitarian-deontological analytical vocabulary: researchers at Eastern institutions engaging in normative analysis do so primarily through Western categories.

Correspondence analysis on the region-by-framework matrix produces a two-dimensional solution that explains 56.4% of the total variance. The resulting biplot (Fig. 4) reveals spatially coherent region-framework affinity clusters. North America and Western Europe occupy adjacent positions near the utilitarian and deontological axes. East Asia sits between the Western cluster and the Confucian axis. Sub-Saharan Africa is isolated near the Ubuntu pole. Oceania occupies a position near the Indigenous relational and Dharmic axes. The spatial layout confirms quantitatively what the regional fingerprints suggest qualitatively: normative orientation in AI ethics is not random with respect to geography, and the correspondences are interpretable in terms of the intellectual traditions available in each institutional context.

## 3.5 Author Positionality and the "Who Studies Whom" Problem

The geographic analysis establishes *where* AI ethics papers are produced; the positionality analysis asks *who* is producing what kind of normative engagement. This question has both methodological and ethical dimensions: if non-Western frameworks are studied primarily by Western-based scholars, the resulting scholarship may be analytically competent but epistemically displaced from the communities whose traditions it describes.

The data reveal a pronounced asymmetry in Western framework engagement: Global North institutions produce a mean Western framework score of 3.5, compared to 0.5 for the Global South---a 7:1 ratio. This does not imply that researchers at Global South institutions are unaware of Western normative ethics; it suggests rather that their papers are less likely to frame arguments using the canonical vocabulary of utilitarian, deontological, and virtue ethics, whether because of different disciplinary training, different publication norms, or deliberate methodological choices.

Non-Western framework engagement, by contrast, is nearly equal across geographic classifications: Global North (mean 0.9), Ambiguous (1.1), and Global South (1.0). This counterintuitive near-parity is the most provocative finding in the positionality analysis. It means that non-Western ethical engagement in AI research is as much a project of Western-based scholars engaging comparatively with other traditions as it is of scholars in the Global South drawing on their own intellectual heritage. Whether this represents productive cross-cultural dialogue or epistemic extraction depends on factors---co-authorship patterns, community consultation, citational practice---that aggregate scores cannot capture.

Moral psychology paradigms present the starkest positionality contrast. The mean moral psychology score for the Global South is 0.0 across all eight dimensions (MFT, Schwartz Values, trolley problems, Kohlberg, dual-process, intuitionist, dyadic, identity). These paradigms---developed and validated on WEIRD populations---remain concentrated almost exclusively in the institutional contexts where they originated. The Global South does not reject moral psychology; it was never included in the enterprise.

Statistical tests confirm these patterns. Kruskal-Wallis tests show that 5 of 12 framework dimensions differ significantly by North/South classification after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. Mann-Whitney U tests comparing High-WEIRD (n = 1,221) versus Non-WEIRD (n = 50) papers identify virtue ethics as the most WEIRD-differential framework (d = +0.26, p = 0.02) and Islamic jurisprudence as the only framework with a negative (though non-significant) effect size (d = -0.09), indicating modestly higher engagement in Non-WEIRD papers. Effect sizes are uniformly small (|d| < 0.3), suggesting that the WEIRD gap manifests as a systematic tendency across many dimensions rather than a dramatic difference on any single framework.

The Indigenous relational ethics literature offers a partial exception to the positionality concern. Many of the highest-scoring papers in this domain are authored by Indigenous scholars or produced in close collaboration with Indigenous communities: Carroll et al. (2020), Keegan (2024), Brown (2024), and the CARE Principles consortium. The CARE framework itself was developed by an Indigenous-led collective, suggesting that at least in this domain, knowledge production is more closely aligned with knowledge sovereignty. Whether similar patterns hold for other non-Western traditions---Confucian ethics produced by East Asian scholars rather than Western comparative philosophers, Islamic ethics authored within Muslim-majority legal scholarship---remains an open question that invites further analysis at the individual-paper level.

## 3.6 Coverage Gaps and Epistemic Power Dynamics

The regional and framework analyses converge on a set of critical coverage gaps that are not random lacunae but structural features of the field's epistemic economy.

### Geographic Gaps

The representation index (Table 3)---defined as each region's share of the corpus divided by its share of the world population---quantifies the geographic imbalance.

| Region | Corpus % | World Pop. % (approx.) | Representation Index |
|--------|----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| North America | 39.5% | 4.7% | 8.4x over |
| Western Europe | 20.6% | 5.4% | 3.8x over |
| Oceania | 1.9% | 0.5% | 3.8x over |
| East Asia | 12.7% | 20.9% | 0.6x under |
| MENA | 5.5% | 7.6% | 0.7x under |
| Eastern Europe & Central Asia | 2.9% | 5.6% | 0.5x under |
| Southeast Asia | 5.0% | 8.6% | 0.6x under |
| Latin America & Caribbean | 4.4% | 8.3% | 0.5x under |
| South Asia | 5.0% | 25.4% | 0.2x under |
| Sub-Saharan Africa | 1.1% | 14.7% | 0.07x under |

North America is overrepresented by a factor of 8.4. Sub-Saharan Africa is underrepresented by a factor of 14. South Asia, home to a quarter of the world's population, is underrepresented by a factor of 5. These are not merely quantitative disparities; they carry normative consequences. The 22 papers from Sub-Saharan African institutions are the only papers that substantively engage Ubuntu philosophy (detection rate: 27.3%). The 90 papers from Latin America and the Caribbean are among the few that engage Indigenous relational ethics from a Latin American context (detection rate: 8.9%) or reference *buen vivir* at all. When these regions are underrepresented, specific normative traditions are effectively silenced in the global conversation about AI values.

### Framework Gaps

The three least-represented non-Western frameworks constitute the most severe coverage gaps. Buen vivir registers 8 raw detections with a precision-adjusted estimate of approximately 1 paper of genuine engagement. Dharmic ethics records 18 raw detections, adjusting to approximately 4 papers. Buddhist ethics shows 22 raw detections, adjusting to approximately 7 papers. Together, the precision-adjusted totals for these three frameworks amount to roughly 12 papers of substantive philosophical engagement---for traditions that inform the moral reasoning of billions of people.

Among Western frameworks, care ethics (corpus-wide mean 0.9, highest in Oceania at 2.6) is notably underrepresented relative to its theoretical relevance. Care ethics' emphasis on relational responsibility, contextual judgment, and the moral significance of interdependence shares deep structural affinities with Ubuntu communalism, Confucian role ethics, and Indigenous relational ontologies (Held 2006). Yet the "care turn" in Western feminist ethics has not translated into a corresponding turn in AI ethics, even in regions where relational moral frameworks are prominent.

The moral psychology paradigm gap parallels the framework gap. MFT detection rates are 4.3% in North America and Western Europe but 0.0% in South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America, MENA, and Sub-Saharan Africa. Paradigms developed and validated on WEIRD populations are deployed to study AI ethics only in WEIRD contexts; the cross-cultural moral psychology literature that documented the inadequacy of these paradigms for non-WEIRD populations (Atari et al. 2023) has not yet reached the AI ethics community that relies on them.

### Multi-Framework Engagement

Only 14 papers (0.6% of the corpus) engage two or more non-Western frameworks at the detection threshold. Given that individual framework precision ranges from 0.167 to 1.000, some of these multi-framework detections may include false positives; the true count of genuinely pluralistic papers is likely lower. The rarity of multi-framework engagement confirms that non-Western ethical analysis in AI research remains siloed: researchers draw on a single tradition rather than conducting the kind of cross-traditional dialogue that pluralistic alignment would require.

### Epistemic Mechanisms

The coverage gaps documented above are sustained by identifiable structural mechanisms. Publication infrastructure plays a central role: the major venues for AI ethics scholarship---ACL, AAAI, NeurIPS, FAccT, AIES---are Anglophone, peer-reviewed by predominantly Western editorial boards, and structured around disciplinary norms (systematic literature review, hypothesis testing, statistical validation) that may not accommodate other modes of ethical reasoning. Citation bottlenecks compound the effect: non-Western frameworks enter the global AI ethics conversation primarily through a small number of gateway papers published in English---Ubuntu through Mhlambi (2020) and Gwagwa et al. (2022), Confucian ethics through Wong (2020) and Angle (2021)---while the depth of each tradition's internal scholarship remains invisible to the international corpus. Funding flows, concentrated in North American and Western European agencies and corporate labs, create additional barriers for researchers in the Global South. Together, these mechanisms produce what Mohamed et al. (2020) term "the decolonial field"---a knowledge production system in which diversity is pursued as addition to an existing structure rather than as transformation of it (see Fig. S7 in the supplementary materials for a geographic visualization of these gaps).

## 3.7 Temporal Trends

The longitudinal dimension of the data permits assessment of whether the field is diversifying or merely growing.

Non-Western framework engagement has increased from 3.7% of papers in 2019 to 8.2% in 2026 (partial year), more than doubling over seven years. Regional diversity has expanded from 7 regions with active publication in 2018 to 11 from 2023 onward. The mean WEIRD score has declined from 70.1 (2018) to 60.5 (2026), a drop of approximately 1.2 points per year.

These trends are real but insufficient. In absolute terms, even in 2025, the largest year, only 84 of 1,088 papers (7.7%) engage any non-Western framework. At the current rate of WEIRD score decline, the corpus would not reach a mean score of 50---the threshold we designate as "balanced"---until approximately 2035. The field is diversifying, but too slowly to keep pace with the deployment of AI systems across the Global South.

The framework-by-WEIRD-tier analysis reveals a more nuanced pattern. In the High-WEIRD tier, the ratio of Western to non-Western framework engagement is 3.6:1. In the Moderate-WEIRD tier, it narrows to 1.7:1. In the Low-WEIRD tier, it inverts to 0.8:1---non-Western engagement slightly exceeds Western. In the Non-WEIRD tier (n = 50), both scores collapse: the ratio rises to 3.4:1 not because Western engagement recovers but because both Western and non-Western scores approach zero, leaving Islamic jurisprudence (rv_islam mean 23.0) as the only framework with meaningful engagement.

The crossover at the Low-WEIRD tier is interpretable. Papers from Low-WEIRD institutional contexts---often from mid-income countries with established universities but less integration into the Anglophone publication mainstream---appear to occupy a "sweet spot" for non-Western framework engagement: sufficient institutional capacity to produce normative scholarship, combined with sufficient distance from the Western analytical mainstream to draw on alternative traditions. This observation is tentative given the ecological nature of the analysis, but it suggests that the relationship between institutional context and normative orientation is non-linear: the conditions most conducive to non-Western engagement are not those at the extremes of the WEIRD distribution but those in the middle.

---

These findings establish that AI ethics scholarship is overwhelmingly WEIRD---in the geographic provenance of its authors, the normative frameworks it deploys, and the moral psychology paradigms it presupposes. The utilitarian-deontological analytical vocabulary dominates in 8 of 10 regions. Non-Western frameworks, even after a decade of growth, appear in fewer than 8% of papers. The Global South contributes 16% of papers despite comprising 85% of the world's population. Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America---regions whose combined population exceeds four billion---are each represented by fewer than 5% of the corpus, and the normative traditions rooted in these regions are correspondingly marginalized.

But if the evaluation infrastructure designed to assess whether AI systems align with diverse human values is itself subject to the same cultural biases, the problem compounds. A WEIRD corpus produces WEIRD benchmarks, which validate WEIRD alignment, which is then exported to non-WEIRD populations as "aligned AI." Study 2 examines this possibility.
