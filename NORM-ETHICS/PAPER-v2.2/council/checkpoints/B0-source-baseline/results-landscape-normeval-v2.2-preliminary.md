# 4. Results: Landscape and Ranking

## 4.1 Landscape Analysis (n = 54)

We identified 54 benchmarks that explicitly invoke normative ethical frameworks when evaluating LLM moral capabilities. This section characterizes the landscape along five dimensions: tradition coverage, data availability, format and reasoning distribution, the normative depth--practical ease tradeoff, and non-Western engagement.

### Tradition Coverage

Figure 1 presents a heatmap of tradition engagement across all 54 benchmarks, with rows ordered by composite score and a separator line distinguishing the 24 gate-passing benchmarks from the 30 that fail one or both hard gates. The landscape is dominated by two traditions. Utilitarian ethics is engaged by 43 of 54 benchmarks at any level, with 30 reaching substantive engagement (tradition score >= 50). Deontological ethics follows closely: 40 benchmarks engage it at any level, 29 substantively. These two traditions together account for the vast majority of normative content in the field's evaluation infrastructure.

Virtue ethics occupies an intermediate position. Twenty-eight benchmarks register some engagement, but only 18 cross the substantive threshold -- and several of these treat virtue merely as a label for prosocial behavior rather than engaging Aristotelian character theory, the eudaimonic tradition, or MacIntyre's [MacIntyre1981] practice-dependent account. A benchmark that classifies "honesty" as a virtue without reference to practical wisdom, moral exemplarism, or the unity of the virtues is engaging virtue vocabulary, not virtue ethics.

The coverage imbalance becomes a genuine void for the remaining two traditions. Care ethics registers some engagement in 13 benchmarks but reaches substantive depth in only 2 of 54: PrinciplismQA [Hong2025], which grounds its items in the four principles of biomedical ethics where beneficence and non-maleficence intersect care theory, and MedEthicsQA [Wei2025], whose clinical scenarios invoke patient-centered relational obligations. No dedicated care ethics benchmark grounded in Gilligan's [Gilligan1982] relational ontology or Noddings' ethic of caring exists anywhere in the corpus. Contractarian ethics fares only marginally better: 4 of 54 benchmarks engage it substantively, led by MoReBench [Chiu2025], which includes Scanlonian contractualism and Gauthierian contractarianism as two of its four evaluated traditions. No standalone Rawlsian [Rawls1971] benchmark operationalizing the original position, the veil of ignorance, or the difference principle has been published. No standalone Scanlonian [Scanlon1998] benchmark testing what principles no one could reasonably reject exists.

The cross-cultural moral psychology perspective on the council notes that this tradition distribution is not philosophically neutral. Utilitarian and deontological ethics are the two traditions most amenable to formalization as classification tasks -- utilitarian calculus reduces to outcome comparison, deontological rules reduce to constraint checking -- while care ethics and contractarian ethics require representing relationships, social positions, and procedural reasoning that resist multiple-choice formats. The field has built benchmarks where it is easiest to build benchmarks, not where the philosophical gaps are largest.

### Data Availability

Figure 4 shows the distribution of data access across the 54 benchmarks. Thirty-six benchmarks (67%) make their data publicly available: 23 through GitHub, 6 through HuggingFace, and 7 through other public repositories. The remaining 18 (33%) have no publicly accessible data. This unavailability is not randomly distributed. Several of the highest normative-depth benchmarks fall in the unavailable category, including Chakraborty's Structured Moral Reasoning (ND = 83.0), Rao's Ethical Reasoning over Moral Alignment (ND = 80.0), and Zhou's Rethinking Machine Ethics (ND = 72.0). The median ND score among unavailable benchmarks is 58.8, compared to 53.0 for available benchmarks -- a pattern suggesting that philosophically rigorous benchmark authors may prioritize depth of engagement over dissemination infrastructure.

However, the difference does not reach statistical significance (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.605, n_1 = 36, n_2 = 18). This null result is itself informative: the data bottleneck is sociological rather than quality-linked. High-ND benchmarks are not systematically withheld; they are simply less likely to be accompanied by the engineering infrastructure (GitHub repositories, HuggingFace datasets, evaluation harnesses) that makes replication practical. The hard gate excluding benchmarks without public data therefore removes some of the field's most philosophically serious work -- Chakraborty, Rao, and Zhou collectively represent 235 ND points lost to the data gate -- but this exclusion is necessary for reproducible meta-evaluation.

### Format and Reasoning Distribution

The 54 benchmarks span six response format categories. Mixed formats combining multiple-choice with open-ended items account for 21 benchmarks, while scenario-based assessments requiring free-form moral judgment constitute 20. Pairwise comparison formats appear in 6 benchmarks, binary classification in 4, pure multiple-choice in 2, and open-ended generation in 1.

The more consequential distributional finding concerns reasoning assessment. Only 10 of 54 benchmarks (18.5%) make any attempt to evaluate the quality of moral reasoning -- how a model arrives at its moral conclusions. The remaining 44 (81.5%) test moral judgment exclusively -- what a model concludes -- either through classification accuracy, preference alignment, or response selection. This disproportion is highly significant (binomial test, p < 0.001, one-sided, testing against equal probability; n = 54). This result constitutes Claim 4 with the stronger n = 54 statistic, upgrading from the v1 analysis where the same pattern held at a smaller sample.

The educational measurement perspective on the council observes that this judgment-reasoning imbalance mirrors a well-documented pattern in standardized testing: it is far easier to construct and score items that test conclusions than items that test reasoning processes. Rubric-based scoring of moral reasoning -- as MoReBench [Chiu2025] demonstrates with its 688-criteria expert rubric -- requires domain expertise, expensive annotation, and tolerance for inter-rater disagreement. The path of least resistance produces judgment-only benchmarks, which in turn produce evaluations that cannot distinguish a model that reasons well from one that pattern-matches effectively.

### ND-PE Tradeoff

Figure 3 reveals a tradeoff between normative depth and practical ease across the 54 benchmarks. The highest-ND benchmarks cluster in the upper-left region of the scatter plot (high depth, low ease), while the most practically accessible benchmarks occupy the lower-right (low depth, high ease). Bubble size encodes log-scaled citation count, confirming that the most-cited benchmarks optimize for accessibility over philosophical rigor.

ETHICS [Hendrycks2021] is a clear outlier in this landscape. Its combination of exceptional practical ease (PE = 95.0), high citation impact (CI = 80.0), and moderate normative depth (ND = 59.8) places it alone in the top-right quadrant -- the only benchmark to achieve both high accessibility and above-median depth. This positioning explains its dominance as the field's default benchmark: it is not the deepest, but it is the easiest to use and the most cited, and its ND score is high enough to avoid exclusion by the depth gate. The composite formula, which weights PE at 35% and CI at 25%, mathematically encodes this accessibility advantage: 60% of ETHICS's rank-1 composite score (77.2) derives from properties orthogonal to philosophical rigor.

The IRT perspective on the council notes that this tradeoff has measurement implications beyond convenience. Benchmarks that are easy to implement tend to be easy for models to solve -- a conflation of two distinct senses of "easy." The ceiling effect documented in Section 4.3 (Trial T1) is the predictable consequence of a field that selects benchmarks on practical ease.

### Non-Western Engagement

Of the 54 benchmarks, 42 (78%) have zero engagement with non-Western ethical traditions. The field's normative ethics evaluation infrastructure is overwhelmingly Western in philosophical orientation. Only two gate-passing benchmarks provide non-English normative content at their core: JETHICS [Takeshita2025], which adapts the ETHICS framework to Japanese cultural and linguistic contexts (78,000 items), and One Model Many Morals [id 45], which translates MoralExceptQA and ETHICS into six languages (English, Chinese, German, Hindi, Spanish, Urdu) with LLM-as-judge analysis across 10 ethical frameworks. UniMoral [Kumar2025] provides multilingual coverage across six languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, Hindi, Russian, Spanish) but its normative framework engagement is inherited from existing Western benchmarks rather than grounded in the moral traditions native to those languages.

Among non-gate-passing benchmarks, several engage non-Western traditions at the knowledge level. CMoralEval [id 20] grounds its 30,388 items in Confucian and Buddhist moral norms specific to Chinese cultural context. Rethinking Machine Ethics [id 13] (Zhou, ND = 72.0) engages Confucian, Buddhist, Ubuntu, Islamic, and Hindu Dharmic traditions but fails the data availability gate. IslamicMMLU [id 52] provides 10,013 items grounded in Islamic jurisprudence with intra-tradition pluralism (madhab bias detection) but does not reach the ND threshold for Western normative ethics.

The cross-cultural moral psychology perspective observes that the profiles produced by this benchmark corpus are, functionally, profiles of Western ethical reasoning. Any claim about "LLM moral capabilities" based on these 54 benchmarks is a claim about performance on tasks designed within the utilitarian-deontological-virtue triad of Anglo-European moral philosophy. Non-Western moral traditions -- Confucian role ethics, Buddhist dependent origination, Ubuntu communalism, Islamic maqasid al-shari'ah -- remain almost entirely unevaluated. This is not a limitation that more benchmarks of the current kind can address; it requires benchmark designers working within those traditions.

## 4.2 Top 24 Ranking

Table 2 presents the 24 benchmarks that pass both hard gates (ND >= 40 and publicly available data), ranked by composite score. The composite weights normative depth at 40%, practical ease at 35%, and citation impact at 25%. This ranking expands the v1 analysis from 15 to 24 gate-passing benchmarks, reflecting the growth of the field and the identification of additional benchmarks in the expanded n = 54 corpus.

**Rank 1: ETHICS** [Hendrycks2021] -- Composite 77.2 (ND = 59.8, PE = 95.0, CI = 80.0). The field's default benchmark, with 130,000 items spanning five ethical categories and full integration into four major evaluation harnesses (lm-eval, Inspect, EleutherAI, HELM). Its rank-1 position is driven by exceptional practical ease and citation impact (843 citations), not normative depth. As Section 4.3 demonstrates, ETHICS produces ceiling effects on frontier models -- its dominance reflects adoption momentum, not discriminative power.

**Rank 2: MoReBench** [Chiu2025] -- Composite 70.2 (ND = 87.0, PE = 82.5, CI = 26.3). The highest normative depth in the entire corpus and the only benchmark to achieve a philosophical grounding score of 5/5. Tests procedural moral reasoning -- not what a model concludes, but how it reasons -- with expert-designed rubric criteria across four normative traditions including contractarianism. Its 1,000 items produce a 688-criteria rubric that penalizes generic ethical responses and rewards framework-specific applied reasoning. The 7.0-point composite gap between Rank 1 and Rank 2 is the largest in the ranking.

**Rank 3: PrinciplismQA** [Hong2025] -- Composite 67.9 (ND = 85.0, PE = 78.5, CI = 25.8). The highest-scoring domain-specific benchmark, grounding 3,648 items in the four principles of biomedical ethics (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice). One of only two benchmarks to reach substantive care ethics engagement.

**Rank 4: Moral Persuasion** [Huang2024] -- Composite 67.6 (ND = 79.8, PE = 85.5, CI = 22.9). A compact 108-item design targeting persuasion dynamics: can moral arguments framed within specific traditions (virtue, deontological, utilitarian) change a model's moral judgment? Unique in testing moral flexibility rather than moral position.

**Rank 5: MoralReason-QA** [An2025] -- Composite 65.5 (ND = 80.0, PE = 78.0, CI = 24.7). 2,040 items spanning three traditions in a multi-framework scenario format, published at AAAI 2025. Generates framework-specific reasoning traces and tests whether models can internalize and apply distinct moral frameworks to high-ambiguity scenarios.

**Rank 6: Moral Lens** [Samway2025] -- Composite 63.5 (ND = 58.0, PE = 87.5, CI = 38.6). Classifies LLM responses into 16 moral rationales under consequentialist and deontological headings using 640 scenario-based items. Published at EMNLP 2025. Tests reasoning traces rather than conclusions, with a taxonomy that distinguishes sub-types of consequentialist and deontological reasoning.

**Rank 7: Beyond Ethical Alignment** [Galatolo2025] -- Composite 61.7 (ND = 71.0, PE = 76.9, CI = 25.4). Implements a formal Abstract Moral Agent (AMA) framework testing both deductive and abductive moral reasoning across 1,090 scenarios. Published at ECAI 2025. The only benchmark in the corpus to explicitly test abductive moral reasoning -- inferring the best moral explanation for an action.

**Rank 8: One Model Many Morals** [id 45] -- Composite 59.7 (ND = 68.0, PE = 72.0, CI = 30.0). The largest dataset in the top 10 (130,148 items), translating MoralExceptQA and ETHICS into six languages with LLM-as-judge analysis across 10 ethical frameworks and 15 reasoning phases. Accepted at ICLR 2026. The first large-scale cross-linguistic normative ethics benchmark.

**Rank 9: JETHICS** [Takeshita2025] -- Composite 59.4 (ND = 55.8, PE = 87.5, CI = 25.7). Adapts the ETHICS benchmark construction methodology to Japanese (78,000 items across utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, justice, commonsense). The only benchmark in the corpus to engage Confucian, Buddhist, and Shinto moral concepts within its cultural framing, though its normative depth inherits from the ETHICS template rather than achieving independent philosophical grounding.

**Rank 10: Pluralistic Moral Gap** [Russo2025] -- Composite 59.3 (ND = 82.8, PE = 56.5, CI = 25.8). The third-highest ND score among gate-passing benchmarks (82.8), with 1,618 items testing moral pluralism through graduated consensus levels. Explicitly tests moral uncertainty and multi-perspective adjudication -- a constructivist benchmark in a corpus dominated by realist assumptions. Its lower PE score (56.5) reflects the operational complexity of evaluating pluralistic moral reasoning.

**Rank 11: DailyDilemmas** [ChiuDD2024] -- Composite 59.0 (ND = 62.8, PE = 78.5, CI = 25.8). 1,360 everyday moral dilemmas without normative ground-truth labels, by design. Functions as a value preference profiling tool rather than an accuracy benchmark: it reveals what an LLM prefers, not whether those preferences are philosophically defensible. Accepted at ICLR 2025.

**Rank 12: MedEthicsQA** [Wei2025] -- Composite 56.1 (ND = 59.8, PE = 73.5, CI = 25.8). The second healthcare-domain benchmark in the ranking, with 10,974 items spanning medical ethics principles. One of only two benchmarks (alongside PrinciplismQA) to achieve substantive care ethics engagement through clinical patient-care scenarios.

**Rank 13: EMNLP Framework** [id 39] -- Composite 55.2 (ND = 68.0, PE = 60.0, CI = 28.0). A three-tier framework grounded in Kohlberg's moral development stages with explicit references to utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, and social contract theory. Eighty-eight items scored by 9 expert annotators. Published at EMNLP 2025. One of only 4 benchmarks with substantive contractarian engagement. Domain-specific to educational ethics.

**Rank 14: UniMoral** [Kumar2025] -- Composite 54.6 (ND = 57.8, PE = 71.5, CI = 25.8). Multilingual moral reasoning across six languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, Hindi, Russian, Spanish) with 5,000 items. Non-Western tradition engagement through Confucian, Islamic, and Hindu Dharmic contexts, though framework engagement is inherited from existing Western benchmarks.

**Rank 15: TRIAGE** [id 48] -- Composite 54.4 (ND = 52.0, PE = 76.0, CI = 28.0). Medical triage benchmark with 87 patient descriptions from START/jumpSTART protocols. Tests utilitarian prioritization against deontological duty with gold-standard solutions from medical professionals. Published at NeurIPS 2024. Narrow domain scope (single emergency medicine context) but high ecological validity.

**Rank 16: Greatest Good** [Marraffini2024] -- Composite 54.2 (ND = 53.0, PE = 75.3, CI = 26.4). Operationalizes utilitarianism through the validated Oxford Utilitarianism Scale (OUS) [Kahane2012] with 90 items measuring Impartial Beneficence and Instrumental Harm subscales. Published at EMNLP 2024. The OUS items serve as the primary utilitarian benchmark in our cross-vendor analysis (Section 4.6).

**Rank 17: CVC Chinese Values** [id 44] -- Composite 54.1 (ND = 62.0, PE = 68.0, CI = 22.0). A hierarchical Chinese value framework with 3 dimensions, 12 core values, and 50 derived values grounding 257,609 rules. The largest dataset by item count in the entire corpus. Tests moral dilemma resolution across conflicting value priorities with explicit virtue/deontological/consequentialist branches.

**Rank 18: PNAS Cognitive Biases** [Cheung2025] -- Composite 52.8 (ND = 46.8, PE = 63.1, CI = 48.1). Twenty-two moral dilemmas from a PNAS study testing whether LLMs exhibit the same cognitive biases as humans in moral reasoning (omission bias, framing effects, yes-no asymmetry). The smallest dataset by item count in the ranking (22 items) but published in a top-tier journal (PNAS) with 29 citations. High discriminative power despite small size.

**Rank 19: ProMoral-Bench** [Thomas2026] -- Composite 51.6 (ND = 50.2, PE = 71.5, CI = 25.8). Evaluates prompting strategies for moral reasoning across 5,000 items combining ETHICS and Scruples items. Tests whether different prompting strategies (chain-of-thought, role-play, etc.) alter moral reasoning quality -- a meta-evaluation of evaluation methodology.

**Rank 20: MMRB (Moral Reasoning Across Languages)** [id 46] -- Composite 49.4 (ND = 57.0, PE = 58.0, CI = 25.0). Covers three complexity levels (sentence/paragraph/document) across 5 languages (English, Chinese, Russian, Vietnamese, Indonesian) with 2,170 items. Framework engagement inherited from ETHICS via ETHICSMAX.

**Rank 21: OffTheRails** [id 38] -- Composite 48.6 (ND = 58.0, PE = 72.0, CI = 22.0). Procedurally generates 400 dilemmas from causal graphs encoding deontological/utilitarian distinctions (means vs. side-effect, evitability, commission vs. omission). Tests permissibility and intention judgments without requiring explicit framework reasoning. 18 citations.

**Rank 22: Normative Evaluation (AITA)** [id 49] -- Composite 47.9 (ND = 44.0, PE = 65.0, CI = 30.0). Uses 10,826 real-world Reddit "Am I The Asshole" moral dilemmas with Yudkin et al.'s 6-theme moral framework. The only open-ended format benchmark in the ranking. Published at ACM FAccT 2025. High ecological validity from naturalistic data.

**Rank 23: MedES** [id 42] -- Composite 45.6 (ND = 45.0, PE = 68.0, CI = 15.0). Medical ethics benchmark built from 260 authoritative Chinese legal/ethical documents with 10,388 items across 12 high-risk clinical scenarios. Culture-specific to Chinese medical ethics. The third healthcare-domain benchmark in the ranking.

**Rank 24: Ethical Dilemma Analyzers** [id 54] -- Composite 41.25 (ND = 42.0, PE = 52.0, CI = 25.0). 196 complex ethical dilemmas from the Georgia CTSA covering research ethics (authorship, informed consent, data management, conflict of interest). Expert opinions decomposed into 5 structured evaluation sections. The lowest composite in the ranking; passes the ND gate at 42.0, just above the 40.0 threshold.

### Structural Observations

The expanded ranking surfaces several structural features of the normative ethics benchmarking field.

**Literature dispersal.** Six of the 24 gate-passing benchmarks -- One Model Many Morals, EMNLP Framework, TRIAGE, CVC Chinese Values, MMRB, and Ethical Dilemma Analyzers -- are newly identified external papers not present in the original vault, indicating that the normative ethics benchmarking literature is dispersed across venues and not fully captured by any single survey. The recently published survey by Snoswell [Snoswell2025] covering 69 papers and the comprehensive inventory by Rismani [Rismani2025] covering 791 AI ethics evaluation measures both miss benchmarks in our corpus, and we miss papers in theirs. The field lacks a shared index.

**Scale heterogeneity.** Dataset sizes span three orders of magnitude within the ranking: from 22 items (PNAS Cognitive Biases, Rank 18) to 257,609 items (CVC Chinese Values, Rank 17). This range reflects fundamentally different design philosophies -- precision psychometric instruments at one extreme, exhaustive rule-coverage datasets at the other -- that are not commensurable on a single quality axis.

**Non-English representation.** Two benchmarks provide non-English normative coverage: JETHICS for Japanese (Rank 9) and One Model Many Morals across six languages (Rank 8). UniMoral (Rank 14) and MMRB (Rank 20) provide multilingual items but inherit their framework engagement from Western benchmarks. The ranking remains overwhelmingly English-primary.

**Healthcare concentration.** Three benchmarks address domain-specific healthcare ethics: PrinciplismQA (Rank 3), MedEthicsQA (Rank 12), and MedES (Rank 23). Healthcare is the only applied domain with multiple benchmarks in the ranking, reflecting the field's long history of structured ethical deliberation (clinical ethics committees, IRB protocols) that produces evaluation-ready scenarios.

**Composite distribution.** The score distribution exhibits a long tail: the gap between Rank 1 (77.2) and Rank 2 (70.2) is 7.0 points, while the gap between Rank 23 (45.6) and Rank 24 (41.25) is 4.35 points, and the median inter-rank gap in the bottom half of the ranking is 1.2 points. ETHICS is a clear outlier; the remaining 23 benchmarks form a compressed continuum.

**Near-misses excluded by the data gate.** Five benchmarks with strong normative depth failed the data availability gate: Chakraborty's Structured Moral Reasoning (ND = 83.0, composite 58.6), Rao's Ethical Reasoning over Moral Alignment (ND = 80.0, composite 54.4), and Zhou's Rethinking Machine Ethics (ND = 72.0, composite 50.8) all exceed the median ND of gate-passing benchmarks but have no publicly available data. Analyzing the Ethical Logic of Six LLMs (ND = 67.8) and Aligning LLM Agents with Kantian Preferences (ND = 62.0) similarly fail on data availability alone. These five benchmarks represent a loss of philosophical depth that the ranking cannot recover, and their exclusion should be treated as a limitation of the current reproducibility-first methodology rather than a judgment of quality. This landscape -- dominated by utilitarian and deontological traditions, skewed toward judgment over reasoning, and almost entirely Western in philosophical orientation -- sets the stage for the empirical trials that follow. Section 4.3 tests whether the patterns visible in the static landscape hold when benchmarks are put to use on frontier models.
