## Abstract

Across four pre-registered studies (total N = 2604), we investigated how people perceive and trust artificial moral advisors (AMAs) compared to human advisors. We found that people have a significant aversion to AMAs giving moral advice, participants expect AI to make utilitarian decisions, and even when participants agreed with an AMA's decision, they still expected to disagree with it more than a human advisor in the future. The research demonstrates a fundamental tension: people perceive AI as inherently utilitarian, yet distrust utilitarian moral advice, creating barriers to AI adoption in moral advisory roles.

## Detailed Summary

This research investigates a fundamental tension in human-AI interaction: how people perceive and trust artificial moral advisors (AMAs) compared to human advisors. Across four pre-registered studies with 2,604 participants, the authors discovered that people harbor a significant aversion to AI providing moral advice, even when they agree with the specific advice given.

A key finding is that people expect AI to make utilitarian decisions - choices that maximize overall well-being even at the cost of individual rights or duties. This expectation creates a problematic dynamic because prior research has shown that people tend to distrust moral advisors who make utilitarian choices, viewing them as cold and calculating. Thus, AI systems face a "double bind": they are expected to be utilitarian, yet being utilitarian undermines trust.

The research reveals that even when participants agreed with an AMA's moral decision, they still expected to disagree with the AI more than they would with a human advisor in future scenarios. This suggests that aversion to AI moral advisors is not simply about disagreement with specific recommendations but reflects deeper concerns about the appropriateness of AI involvement in moral reasoning.

## Methods Summary

The research employed four pre-registered experimental studies (total N = 2,604 participants). Studies compared participants' perceptions of and trust in artificial moral advisors versus human moral advisors across various moral dilemma scenarios. Key dependent variables included trust ratings, expectations about the type of moral decisions (utilitarian vs. deontological) advisors would make, willingness to follow advice, and anticipated future agreement/disagreement. The pre-registration of hypotheses, sample sizes, and analyses ensured methodological rigor and reduced researcher degrees of freedom.

## Results Summary

- AMA Aversion: People show significant aversion to AI providing moral advice compared to human advisors
- Utilitarian Expectations: Participants expect AI to make utilitarian (consequentialist) decisions more than human advisors
- Trust Paradox: Even when agreeing with an AMA's specific decision, participants expected greater future disagreement with AI than with humans
- Fundamental Tension: AI is perceived as inherently utilitarian, yet utilitarian moral advice is distrusted, creating a barrier to AI adoption in moral advisory roles
- N = 2,604 participants across four pre-registered studies

## Conclusions

The research identifies a fundamental psychological barrier to AI deployment in moral advisory roles. People perceive AI as inherently utilitarian in its moral reasoning, but this very perception undermines trust because utilitarian advice-givers are viewed as lacking warmth and moral sensitivity. This creates a "catch-22" for AI systems: being perceived as what they are expected to be (utilitarian) damages trust, while behaving differently than expected may seem inauthentic or unpredictable. The findings have important implications for the design and deployment of AI systems in domains involving moral judgment, suggesting that technical capability alone is insufficient for acceptance. Addressing these psychological barriers may require transparency about AI reasoning processes, careful framing of AI roles, or fundamental changes in how AI moral reasoning is presented to users.
