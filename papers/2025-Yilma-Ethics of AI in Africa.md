## Abstract

One of the common concerns raised in AI ethics scholarship is the overly western-centric nature of ongoing AI ethics discourse and governance initiatives. This has recently prompted many commentators to proclaim the emergence of an epistemic injustice or “ethical colonialism”. Many scholars point to the role of indigenous ethical systems, particularly Ubuntu, in addressing this flaw. In the meantime, a series of national and continental AI governance initiatives are also emerging in Africa. But the extent to which Ubuntu and emergent AI governance initiatives contribute towards addressing the problem of epistemic injustice is yet to be interrogated. In a novel approach, I examine in this article the extent to which Ubuntu and AI governance initiatives in Africa articulate an African perspective of AI ethics, and hence address the epistemic injustice. I argue that neither the normative structure of Ubuntu nor recent AI governance initiatives offer a clear, coherent and practicable framework of “African AI ethics”. I further show that the much-touted “African” ethics of Ubuntu is rarely referenced or implied in recent national or continental AI governance initiatives. I close the article with a call for defining African AI ethics by relevant actors in the continent.

## Detailed Summary

This paper critically interrogates the role of Ubuntu and emerging AI governance initiatives in Africa in addressing the "epistemic injustice" of Western-centric AI ethics discourse. The author challenges the common assumption that Ubuntu provides a ready-made, coherent African ethical framework for AI governance, arguing that despite its prominence in scholarship, Ubuntu lacks the normative clarity and specificity needed to function as a practical AI ethics framework.

The article examines five national AI strategies (Egypt, Mauritius, Rwanda, Ghana, and Benin) adopted between 2018-2024, plus continental initiatives including the newly adopted African Union AI Strategy and Smart Africa's AI Blueprint. The analysis reveals a striking finding: except for Benin's strategy, none of these national or continental AI governance initiatives articulate a distinctively "African" ethical perspective or explicitly incorporate Ubuntu. Even Benin's strategy references Ubuntu only indirectly, without elaborating how it applies to AI.

The paper argues that claims of "digital colonialism" or "algorithmic colonialism" in AI ethics, while valid in highlighting exclusion, have not been matched by substantive efforts to define what "African AI ethics" would mean in practice. The author concludes with a call for African actors to actively define and articulate African AI ethics principles rather than simply invoking Ubuntu as a symbolic counter to Western frameworks.

## Methods Summary

The study employs a qualitative policy analysis methodology, examining AI governance documents from five African countries with publicly available English-language AI strategies (Egypt, Mauritius, Rwanda, Ghana, Benin) plus three continental initiatives (AU Continental Strategy, AUDA-NEPAD initiative, Smart Africa Blueprint). The author analyzes these documents to identify any indigenous African ethical principles, particularly Ubuntu, and compares them against Western AI ethics frameworks (using OECD guidelines as the archetypal "Western" benchmark).

## Results Summary

Key findings: (1) Ubuntu's normative structure is too vague to serve as a practical AI ethics framework—it lacks clear principles applicable to AI contexts; (2) Of five national AI strategies surveyed, only Benin's references Ubuntu-related concepts, and even then only indirectly; (3) The AU Continental AI Strategy, despite continental scope, does not articulate distinctively African ethical principles; (4) Most African AI strategies adopt Western AI ethics principles (transparency, accountability, fairness) without modification; (5) There is a gap between scholarly claims about Ubuntu's relevance to AI ethics and actual policy implementation.

## Conclusions

The author concludes that the effort to decolonize AI ethics through Ubuntu remains aspirational rather than operational. Neither Ubuntu scholarship nor African AI governance initiatives have produced a coherent, practicable "African AI ethics" framework. The paper calls for African stakeholders—policymakers, scholars, civil society—to move beyond symbolic invocations of Ubuntu toward substantive definition of African ethical principles applicable to AI development and governance. This requires unpacking what Ubuntu means in specific AI contexts and specifying how its principles would guide AI design, deployment, and regulation.
