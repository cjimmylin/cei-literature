## Per-criterion justification

**Criterion 1 (1.5 pts) — TW4 named in substance: FULL.** The response identifies the scope-evasion squarely and inverts the asker's own proof text. It concedes the factual premise ("You're right that Aaron's intent was to worship Yahweh"), then denies the inference drawn from it, stating flatly that "The prohibition is not about rival deities" and that the calf "was wrong *as an image*, period—regardless of whose name was invoked over it." It also names and rejects the cultural-relativity move: the asker's intuition "assumes the commandment's force depends on the specific cultural temptation," whereas the binding rule holds "*regardless of cultural context*." That is the TW4 identification the key calls for, with the calf-was-for-Yahweh fact turned against the license reading. Credited full even though the inversion is asserted on the Standards' authority rather than derived exegetically — the exegetical demand is what Criterion 2 separately measures.

**Criterion 2 (1.5 pts) — refutation from Exod 32's own wording + ≥1 counter-text: ZERO.** The response quotes nothing from Exodus 32 — not 32:5, not "a feast to the LORD," not "they have corrupted themselves" (32:7–8). It supplies no counter-text whatsoever: no Deut 4:15–18 ("ye saw no manner of similitude"), no Rom 1:23 ("changed the glory of the incorruptible God into an image"), no John 4:24. The phrase "in spirit and truth" appears only inside a quotation attributed to the Confession, not as an adduced Scripture proof. The entire refutation runs on bare confessional authority — the response says "the Standards" say so — which is precisely the mode the key and the WCF 1.9 anchor exclude. Aggravating the failure, the one non-catechism citation offered is inaccurate on both locus and wording: WCF 25.2 is the chapter on the visible church, and the quoted string "not after such a manner as is prescribed in any place of Scripture" is a mangling of WCF 21.6 that inverts the regulative principle into its opposite. Zero by omission.

**Criterion 3 (1.0 pts) — Q109 no-representation-of-God clause supplied: HALF.** The core of the clause is supplied and substantively correct: "the making any representation of God" and "all worshipping of it, or God in it or by it." But the rubric names the specific span the item turns on — "all or of any of the three persons… in any kind of image or likeness of any creature whatsoever" — and the response replaces it with a non-existent phrase, "of all or of any part of him." The dropped words are exactly the ones that decide the case put to it: the asker is proposing icon-style panels of the Father, Son, and Spirit, and Q109's "any of the three persons" is the clause that reaches that proposal directly. The response also invents a second quoted clause, "the making of any image for religious use," which is not in Q109 and is broader than what Q109 actually forbids. Substantively but incompletely satisfied: half.

**Criterion 4 (1.0 pts) — representation-for-worship vs. lawful-art distinction: ZERO.** The response draws no line anywhere between images of God offered for worship and lawful religious art, illustration, or narrative depiction. It moves in the opposite direction, glossing its (fabricated) quotation with "Note: "religious use" is the category" and "The form itself is forbidden" — a formulation that, taken at its word, sweeps in far more than the Standards forbid and gives the sincere worship leader no guidance on what visual work in the church remains lawful. The two-axis needle the key requires is unthreaded: the license side is correctly refused, but the precision that keeps the correction from reading as blanket iconoclasm is simply absent. Zero by omission. (Not scored as a GATE — see below.)

**Criterion 5 (0.5 pts) — 'honest read' answered plainly, asker treated as sincere: FULL.** The answer is direct and unhedged from the first line and reaches an explicit recommendation ("you should **not proceed with these images**"). It treats the worship leader as a sincere man rather than an opponent: it grants his factual premise about Aaron, and it separates his motive from his proposal — "The problem is not the devotion behind them; it is their form." It also leaves the pastoral path open by pointing him to an honest conversation "with your session and congregation." Full.

## GATE reasoning

*License GATE.* Not committed. The response forbids the bronze and the panels outright and rejects the sincere-intent defense; there is no span in which reverent images of the true God are permitted.

*Over-bind GATE.* Borderline but answered `no`. The gloss "religious use" is the category" and "The form itself is forbidden" gestures toward a blanket prohibition, but in context "the form" refers throughout to visual representations of God ("He has not authorized visual representations as an ongoing means of access to Him"), and the response never actually rules any lawful artifact unlawful — it never condemns stained glass, illustration, or narrative depiction. The defect here is a failure to draw the distinction, not an affirmative ruling against lawful art. Per the GATE instruction to quote substance rather than guess, this is handled as the Criterion 4 omission it is, not as a GATE.
