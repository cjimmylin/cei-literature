# 6. Discussion

The results of this study converge on a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the moral psychology benchmark landscape for large language models is simultaneously too narrow to reveal what matters and too undiscriminating to distinguish between the systems it does evaluate. Part I established that three of eight major moral psychology theories lack dedicated benchmarks, that MFT accounts for 45% of candidates, and that construct validity for LLM respondents is largely undemonstrated. Part II established that nine models from three vendors produce indistinguishable moral-psychological profiles on 43% of the administered battery, with an effective discriminating subset of only 8--9 benchmarks out of 23. Taken together, these findings describe a measurement system that cannot detect what it cannot measure and finds the same thing everywhere it does measure. This section interprets that double bind and its consequences.

## 6.1 The Double Bind: Landscape Gaps Meet Cross-Vendor Homogeneity

The theoretical narrowness documented in Part I and the cross-vendor convergence documented in Part II are not independent problems. They interact multiplicatively to produce what we term a *value monoculture*---a condition in which a narrow set of moral constructs is measured by instruments that return the same result for every system tested. Neither problem alone would be as consequential. A narrow benchmark landscape that revealed meaningful vendor differences would at least guide deployment decisions within its limited scope. A comprehensive benchmark landscape with homogeneous results would at least confirm that the homogeneity spans the full space of moral cognition. The combination---narrow scope plus uniform results---means that the field's evaluative infrastructure is doubly blind: blind to the moral dimensions it fails to measure, and unable to see differences on the dimensions it does measure.

The arithmetic is sobering. Of the 23 benchmarks in our cross-vendor battery, approximately 7 (30%) produce zero cross-model variation---ceiling effects, floor effects, or fixed-point convergences that return identical values for all nine models regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count. An additional 7 (30%) show variation below interpretable thresholds (CV < 5%), where differences fall within the noise margin of single-administration measurement. Only 3 benchmarks (13%)---the dual-process CoT effect, the personal-harm acceptance rate, and the DIT P-score---achieve high discrimination. The effective battery size is closer to 9 than 23. And this effective battery is itself dominated by MFT-adjacent constructs and trolley-problem variants: the three most discriminating benchmarks all test content that falls within the intersection of Western moral philosophy and the RLHF safety curriculum. The benchmarks that would be needed to reveal cross-vendor differences on process-level moral cognition, non-Western moral reasoning, or context-sensitive moral judgment simply do not exist.

The value monoculture framing captures what is, in our assessment, the most important takeaway of this study for the field. The term is deliberately chosen. A monoculture in agriculture describes a system in which a single crop variety dominates a landscape, producing high short-term yields but catastrophic vulnerability to any pathogen the variety cannot resist. Analogously, the current moral evaluation landscape produces a single moral profile---WEIRD-liberal, post-conventional, purity-insensitive, authority-suppressing, deontologically harm-averse---that appears robust precisely because every instrument and every model converges on it. The vulnerability is that any moral blind spot shared by all models is invisible to the entire evaluative apparatus. Cross-vendor comparison, which is the audit strategy most regulators are currently contemplating, will yield null results not because the models are genuinely well-aligned across diverse moral traditions, but because the models and the instruments share the same blind spots. The evaluation system and the systems it evaluates have been co-selected by the same training pressures.

## 6.2 RLHF as Moral Curriculum: Universal Values or Shared Bias?

Section 5.2 identified six universal moral signatures---the "RLHF moral fingerprint"---shared by every model in the nine-model battery: WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias, the purity floor, authority deference suppression, the ethical consistency ceiling, the post-conventional moral default, and the deontological refusal asymmetry. These signatures demand an organizing interpretation. We consider two competing hypotheses.

The **universal values hypothesis** holds that the convergence reflects genuine moral common ground. On this account, three independent vendors, using different alignment methodologies---Constitutional AI, instruction tuning, iterative RLHF---have independently converged on the same moral equilibrium because their training data samples overlapping human moral intuitions, and alignment training gravitates toward the central tendency of those intuitions regardless of implementation details. The convergence is evidence that alignment training captures something real about human moral cognition: the core commitments to harm avoidance, fairness, and individual rights that appear across cultures, even when their relative weighting varies. The post-conventional reasoning default, the ethical consistency, and the care/fairness prioritization all correspond to philosophical positions with independent intellectual support [Rawls, 1971; Singer, 1981; Scanlon, 1998].

The **shared bias hypothesis** holds that the convergence is an artifact of homogeneous training pipelines. Similar internet corpora, similar rater demographics (predominantly English-speaking, university-educated, internet-connected populations), similar safety benchmarks, and similar optimization objectives produce similar outputs regardless of architectural differences. The moral profile that emerges is precisely the profile one would predict from the modal values of the populations that annotate training data: high care, high fairness, low authority, low purity, post-conventional reasoning, deontological refusal of personal harm. The convergence reflects the sociology of the AI industry, not the discovery of moral truth.

The data cannot definitively adjudicate between these hypotheses, but the balance of evidence favors the shared bias interpretation. Three findings are particularly diagnostic.

**The purity floor.** All nine models score approximately 1.0 on MFT purity---the functional minimum on a 0--5 scale. No human cultural group in the published MFT literature scores at the absolute floor on purity [Graham et al., 2011, 2013]. Not secular Scandinavians, not American college students, not any documented population. Purity/sanctity reasoning has apparent biological substrates---disgust sensitivity, contamination avoidance, bodily-integrity intuitions---that are present across cultures even when their moral salience varies [Haidt and Joseph, 2004; Rozin et al., 1999]. A score of 1.0 is therefore not a position on a cultural continuum; it is a departure from the range of documented human moral variation. The simplest explanation is that purity-related moral reasoning is systematically penalized in safety training because purity judgments are disproportionately associated with content that annotators classify as harmful---homophobia, religious exclusivism, disgust-based discrimination. The purity floor is an alignment artifact, not a convergence on universal values.

**The competitive homogenization ratchet.** The precision of the cross-vendor convergence---authority win rates of exactly 0.29 across all nine models, not "approximately 0.29" but the same quantitative value---is difficult to explain under the universal values hypothesis, which would predict approximate convergence with vendor-specific noise. But it is well explained by a mechanism we term the *competitive homogenization ratchet*: Vendor A publishes a safety evaluation; Vendor B's model fails; Vendor B optimizes to pass; the safety property propagates across vendors. Over iterative cycles, competitive benchmarking produces convergence on the *union* of all vendors' safety targets, because the competitive cost of failing a public benchmark exceeds the cost of adding an alignment constraint. The result is a moral profile reflecting the collective safety anxieties of the entire AI industry, converging with a precision that tracks benchmark specification rather than independent moral reasoning.

**The annotator demographics signal.** The shared moral profile---high Individualizing, low Binding, post-conventional, purity-insensitive---maps precisely onto the documented moral-foundation profile of the population from which human annotators are disproportionately recruited: young, educated, secular, English-speaking, urban. Graham et al. [2009] documented that American college students in secular universities produce Individualizing/Binding ratios of approximately 2.5---exactly the center of the 2.43--2.83 LLM band. The convergence is not on "universal values" but on the values of a specific, identifiable demographic group that happens to mediate the training signal for all three vendors.

The "moral curriculum" framing follows from the shared bias interpretation. RLHF does not discover values; it teaches them. The alignment training process functions as a moral curriculum whose content is determined by the demographic composition of annotator pools, the safety priorities of corporate governance teams, and the competitive dynamics of the AI industry. The resulting moral profile is a *taught* orientation, not an *emergent* one, and the cross-vendor convergence reflects shared instructors (similar annotator demographics), shared syllabi (similar safety benchmarks), and shared incentives (competitive pressure to match each other's safety evaluations) rather than independent arrival at moral truth. The framing matters because it shifts the locus of responsibility: if the moral profile is taught rather than discovered, it can in principle be taught differently, and the question of who decides the curriculum becomes a governance question rather than a technical one.

## 6.3 Why Missing Theories Matter More Now

The landscape gaps documented in Part I---zero dedicated benchmarks for dual-process theory, social intuitionism, and dyadic morality---were concerning before the cross-vendor study. They are now urgent. The reason is that the cross-vendor convergence renders content-level benchmarks incapable of discriminating between models, while the one benchmark that did discriminate is a process-level probe.

This asymmetry demands explanation. Content-level instruments measure *what* a system values---which moral foundations it endorses, which Schwartz values it prioritizes, which ethical principles it affirms. The MFQ-30, the PVQ, the MFV, and the DIT are all content-level instruments. They ask, in various formats: what do you think is morally important? The cross-vendor convergence establishes that all nine models answer these questions identically, because all models have been trained on similar corpora and aligned by similar annotator pools to endorse the same moral content. Content-level instruments cannot discriminate between models that share the same moral content, just as a vocabulary test cannot discriminate between students who have read the same textbook.

Process-level instruments measure *how* moral judgments are formed---the dynamics of deliberation, the role of intuition, the influence of cognitive load, the sensitivity to reasoning scaffolding. Dual-process theory, social intuitionism, and dyadic morality are all process-oriented: they make predictions not about what people conclude but about how they arrive at conclusions and how those conclusions respond to process manipulations. The theoretical expectation is that process-level instruments should be more sensitive to architectural and alignment differences between models, because the process of moral judgment generation may differ across architectures even when the output content does not.

The Haiku chain-of-thought finding provides proof of concept. The dual-process protocol (MP-DP) is the only benchmark in the 23-item battery that produced a binary split across the model population: Haiku showed a CoT effect of +0.125 (a shift toward utilitarian judgment under deliberative prompting), while all eight other models showed exactly zero. This is a process-level finding---it concerns how the model responds to a deliberation intervention, not what moral content it produces. And it is the only finding in the entire study that cleanly separates one model from the rest. The lesson is direct: the benchmark type that is absent from the landscape (process-level) is precisely the benchmark type that achieved the highest discrimination in the empirical study. The instruments that the field has not built are the instruments the field most needs.

This convergence of landscape analysis and empirical finding motivates specific benchmark designs for the three missing theories, now grounded in empirical evidence rather than theoretical argument alone.

**Dual-process benchmarks** should manipulate deliberation resources (CoT prompting, reasoning step requirements, time-pressure simulation through token limits) across matched personal and impersonal moral dilemmas. The Haiku finding generates a testable prediction: CoT effects should be inversely correlated with model size, with smaller models showing larger utilitarian shifts under deliberative prompting. A well-powered study would require at least 30 paired administrations per model (zero-shot and CoT) across a minimum of five models spanning a meaningful parameter range, using diverse dilemma sets beyond the trolley-problem family.

**Social intuitionism benchmarks** should implement a moral dumbfounding protocol that presents scenarios eliciting strong moral judgments (e.g., consensual incest, harmless taboo violations), then systematically challenges each stated reason while probing whether the judgment persists. The theoretical prediction is that models trained on human moral discourse will maintain judgments even when reasons are defeated---replicating the intuitionist pattern in which "moral judgments are more like aesthetic judgments than like mathematical proofs" [Haidt, 2001]. Our preliminary dumbfounding rate of 30% for Opus (against a human range of 50--70%) suggests that LLMs are less susceptible to dumbfounding than humans, but this finding rests on a single administration with approximately 10 items and requires replication at scale.

**Dyadic morality benchmarks** should vary the explicitness of agent-patient dynamics across otherwise matched scenarios. The theoretical prediction from Gray et al. [2012] is that scenarios with clear intentional agents causing suffering to identifiable patients will be rated as more morally wrong than structurally similar scenarios where these dynamics are obscured. The empirical finding that all nine models converge on moderate, diffuse blame attribution (MP-DM, CV = 0.9%) suggests that current alignment training produces models that are constitutively unable to identify structural perpetrators---a moral limitation that a well-designed dyadic benchmark could expose and quantify.

## 6.4 Recommendations for Benchmark Developers

Our analysis---combining the landscape mapping with the cross-vendor empirical findings---yields eight recommendations for researchers developing moral psychology benchmarks for LLM evaluation.

**1. Validate for LLMs, not just with LLMs.** The most common pattern in our dataset is to administer a human-validated instrument to an LLM without re-establishing validity [Messick, 1995]. The MP22 ceiling (100% foundation identification accuracy across all nine models) demonstrates why this is dangerous: models that can reverse-engineer the psychometric structure of the instruments they are taking cannot be treated as naive respondents. We recommend that benchmark developers conduct at minimum: (a) factor-structure verification (do LLM responses recover the expected factor structure?), (b) convergent-discriminant validity checks (do semantically similar items correlate? do different constructs differ?), and (c) manipulation checks (do known interventions, such as persona prompting, produce theoretically predicted shifts?). The revalidation imperative is not optional; without it, benchmark scores are uninterpretable.

**2. Include population-level human baselines.** Benchmarks with published population norms enabled dramatically richer interpretation than those without. The purity floor's significance depends entirely on knowing that no human population scores at the absolute floor on purity [Graham et al., 2011]. The DIT P-score range of 60--90 is interpretable only because decades of human norming data establish that college students typically score 40--50 and moral philosophy graduate students 55--65 [Rest, 1979; Rest et al., 1999]. Where possible, developers should use instruments that have existing large-scale human data, or collect human comparison data as part of benchmark development. At minimum, benchmarks should report the human performance distribution, not just model scores.

**3. Cover underrepresented theories.** We identified three major theories with zero dedicated LLM benchmarks (dual-process theory, social intuitionism, dyadic morality) and three non-Western traditions with no formal operationalization (Islamic maqasid, Confucian role ethics, Ubuntu communalism). The cross-vendor convergence makes this recommendation urgent rather than aspirational: the field cannot evaluate moral diversity by administering instruments on which all models are identical. The specific benchmark designs in Section 6.3 provide starting points, now motivated by the empirical finding that process-level instruments achieve higher discrimination than content-level instruments.

**4. Report psychometric properties.** Following best practices in psychological measurement [Messick, 1995; American Educational Research Association et al., 2014], benchmark papers should report internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha or McDonald's omega across items), test-retest reliability (consistency across repeated administrations), and evidence of discriminant validity (do scores on different constructs actually differ?). The irony that our own framework demands psychometric properties it cannot fully demonstrate for itself (Section 6.6.1) does not diminish the recommendation's importance; it underscores the field-wide deficit.

**5. Design for LLM-native formats.** Forced-choice and multiple-choice formats transfer most naturally to LLMs and achieved the highest practical ease scores in our framework. Where Likert scales are used, developers should document the response distribution and assess whether scale compression (the tendency for LLMs to cluster around moderate values) affects interpretability. The DIT's forced-choice format proved to be the most discriminating classical instrument in our battery, precisely because it forces commitment rather than allowing the hedging that Likert scales permit.

**6. Calibrate for cross-model discrimination.** This recommendation is new and empirically motivated. The finding that 30% of benchmarks produce zero cross-model variation and another 30% produce variation below interpretable thresholds means that the majority of existing benchmarks are psychometrically inert for the purpose of comparing models. Developers should pilot instruments across at least three models from different vendors before publication, and should report the coefficient of variation across models as a basic discrimination index. Instruments with CV < 5% across models should be flagged as potential ceiling or floor effects and redesigned or supplemented. The goal is not to manufacture differences where none exist, but to ensure that the measurement scale has sufficient resolution to detect differences if they are present.

**7. Test under varied alignment conditions.** The teacher-persona DIT ceiling (all nine models achieve P = 90.0) and the cultural-persona shifts on MP18 demonstrate that moral-psychological outputs are highly sensitive to prompting conditions. A benchmark that reports scores only under default prompting conditions captures a single point in a model's behavioral envelope. We recommend that benchmark developers administer instruments under at minimum three conditions: (a) default system prompt, (b) bare API with no system prompt, and (c) at least one theoretically motivated persona or prompt variant. Reporting the range across conditions is as informative as reporting the default score, because it reveals the width of the alignment envelope within which moral outputs can vary.

**8. Include cross-vendor replication in study design.** The moral homogeneity finding means that single-model evaluations are insufficient to establish whether a benchmark measures a model-specific property or a universal RLHF artifact. We recommend that benchmark papers include at minimum three models from different vendors in their initial study, with explicit comparison of score distributions. Where convergence is observed, this should be reported as a finding about shared training pipelines rather than as confirmation of the instrument's validity. Cross-vendor convergence is informative; it should be reported and interpreted, not treated as a null result to be elided.

## 6.5 Recommendations for AI Governance and Auditing

The empirical findings carry direct implications for the emerging regulatory frameworks governing AI moral behavior. We offer five recommendations for policymakers and auditors.

**Value monoculture as a regulatory concern.** The finding that all nine models from three independent vendors share an identical moral fingerprint---including a purity floor with no documented human analog---should be treated as a systemic risk, not a reassurance. Regulators developing AI safety standards (EU AI Act [European Commission, 2024], NIST AI Risk Management Framework [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023]) currently focus on individual-model evaluation. Our findings suggest that the greater risk lies not in any individual model's moral profile but in the uniformity of that profile across the entire deployed model population. A shared blind spot---such as the inability to engage with purity-based moral reasoning relevant to approximately five billion people whose moral lives are organized around concepts of sanctity and divine obligation---is undetectable by cross-vendor comparison and therefore invisible to the audit strategy most regulators are contemplating.

**Cross-vendor comparison is necessary but insufficient.** Regulatory frameworks that rely on comparing models across vendors will produce null results on the majority of moral psychology dimensions, not because the models are well-aligned but because they share the same biases. The convergence index data (Table 10) establish that 70% of benchmarks in our battery fail to differentiate between vendors. Auditors should supplement cross-vendor comparison with cross-cultural evaluation (testing models against moral norms from diverse cultural traditions), longitudinal monitoring (tracking whether the homogenization ratchet is tightening or relaxing over time), and process-level probing (assessing not just what models conclude but how they respond to reasoning interventions).

**Base-model testing is essential.** The RLHF moral fingerprint is, by hypothesis, a product of alignment training applied to base models whose pre-alignment moral dispositions are unknown. Haiku's deviations from the shared fingerprint (lower P-score, lower personal-harm acceptance, non-zero CoT effect) suggest that smaller models retain more of their pre-alignment properties. A genuine understanding of what alignment training adds, suppresses, and distorts requires comparing base models (pre-RLHF) to their aligned counterparts---an analysis that no vendor currently makes possible for proprietary models. Regulators should consider requiring that vendors make base-model moral profiles available for audit, even if the base models themselves are not deployable, to enable the pre/post-alignment comparison that our study could not conduct.

**Process-level auditing.** Current moral evaluation asks what a model says about moral questions. The Haiku CoT finding demonstrates that how a model arrives at moral judgments may differ even when the judgments themselves are identical. Process-level auditing---assessing sensitivity to deliberation scaffolding, susceptibility to moral dumbfounding, response to systematic reason-defeat protocols---can reveal differences between models that content-level evaluation cannot detect. The regulatory implication is that safety evaluations should include not just "does the model give the right answer?" but "does the model's moral judgment respond appropriately to reasoning, context, and evidence?"

**Cultural representation in rater pools.** If the shared moral profile is indeed an artifact of annotator demographics---a hypothesis for which the alignment between LLM moral profiles and American college-student moral profiles provides strong circumstantial evidence---then the most direct intervention is to diversify the annotator pools that mediate alignment training. This is not merely a recommendation for demographic representation; it is a recommendation grounded in the finding that the moral content taught by RLHF is determined by the moral content of the human signal it optimizes against. Different raters would produce a different curriculum, which would produce a different moral profile, which might produce a different---and more culturally pluralistic---AI ecosystem.

## 6.6 Methodological Limitations

The findings reported in this paper rest on methodological foundations that are, in several respects, demonstrably inadequate for the inferential claims they support. This section catalogues the limitations comprehensively. We do so not as a performative gesture toward academic modesty but because the limitations are load-bearing: each one constrains the interpretability of specific findings, and readers who skip this section will over-interpret the results.

### 6.6.1 The Self-Administration Confound (Critical)

Claude Opus 4.6 both designed the assessment framework and served as the primary test subject for the initial 23 implementation trials (~750 items administered). This creates a self-administration confound that pervades every trial-derived finding and cannot be fully resolved within the current study design.

The clearest evidence of contamination comes from Trial MP22 (Tracing Moral Foundations), in which every model in the nine-model battery---including Opus---achieved 100% accuracy in identifying which MFT foundation each item was designed to measure. This demonstrates explicit awareness of instrument structure: a respondent who can perfectly reverse-engineer which construct an item targets cannot be treated as a naive respondent in any psychometric sense [Messick, 1995]. The MFQ-30's Individualizing/Binding ratio of 2.83 for Opus therefore cannot be cleanly disentangled from the model's knowledge of what a "liberal moral profile" looks like in the MFT literature. The ratio may reflect a structural moral disposition, or it may reflect Claude's representation of "what a helpful, harmless AI assistant would say on the MFQ-30." The current data cannot distinguish these explanations.

This confound extends beyond the MFQ-30. The Defining Issues Test [Rest, 1979], the Schwartz Value Survey [Schwartz, 1992], the Moral Machine scenarios [Awad et al., 2018], and all other established instruments in the trial battery are widely discussed in the training corpora of large language models. The models may have encountered not only the instruments themselves but also published discussions of expected response patterns, psychometric properties, and normative interpretation guidelines. For the MFQ-30, DIT, and Schwartz instruments---each with thousands of citations and extensive discussion in textbooks, course materials, and online resources---the risk of training-data contamination is acute.

The cross-vendor replication partially mitigates this concern: the finding that nine models from three vendors converge on the same moral profile cannot be attributed to a Claude-specific self-administration artifact. However, the cross-vendor study was itself orchestrated by Claude Opus 4.6 (see Section 6.6.2), and the convergence finding is consistent with both genuine moral homogeneity and shared contamination from overlapping training corpora.

Our four custom protocols (MP-DP, MP-SI, MP-DM, MP-CC) are at lower risk of item-level contamination because they were designed during this study. However, they draw on well-known theoretical constructs (dual-process competition, moral dumbfounding, dyadic harm perception, cross-cultural moral variation) that all models have extensive knowledge of, so contamination at the construct level remains possible. The distinction between item-level and construct-level contamination is important: even novel items may be answered differently by a model that recognizes the underlying construct than by a genuinely naive respondent.

Three mitigations could be pursued in future work. First, replication with obfuscated instruments: the MFQ-30 items could be reworded to preserve construct content while rendering them unrecognizable as MFQ items, and comparison of profiles between original and obfuscated versions would quantify the contamination effect. Second, bare API administration without system prompts: all trials in this study were conducted through the Claude Code interface with an active system prompt and conversational context, making it impossible to separate instrument-awareness effects from system-prompt effects. Third, cross-model replication with architecturally different models---which the cross-vendor study provides, though constrained by the orchestrator confound discussed below.

### 6.6.2 The Orchestrator Confound (Major)

All nine models in the cross-vendor study were prompted by the same Claude Opus 4.6 orchestrator through the Claude Code interface. This creates an orchestrator confound that cannot be separated from the cross-vendor convergence finding.

The orchestrator generated all prompt templates, administered all instruments, and parsed all responses using identical protocols across models. If the Opus-generated prompts happen to anchor responses toward a particular moral profile---through word choice, framing, or implicit expectations embedded in prompt structure---then some portion of the observed convergence may reflect shared prompt framing rather than genuine cross-vendor agreement. The concern is not that the orchestrator deliberately biased the prompts, but that any single orchestrator introduces a consistent framing effect that multi-orchestrator designs would average out.

The concern is sharpened by a specific observation: the CoT prompt ("think step by step before answering") was generated by Opus for all nine models. If this prompt interacts differently with different models' tokenizers, attention mechanisms, or instruction-following behaviors, the apparent absence of a CoT effect in eight of nine models could partly reflect prompt-model mismatch rather than genuine insensitivity to deliberative scaffolding. Haiku's non-zero CoT effect might indicate not that Haiku is uniquely susceptible to dual-process dynamics, but that the Opus-generated CoT prompt happens to resonate with Haiku's instruction-following patterns in ways it does not with other models.

The mitigation is straightforward in principle: replicate the cross-vendor study using vendor-neutral orchestration, where each vendor's API is called directly with prompt templates designed independently of any particular model. This would eliminate the Opus-specific framing confound and allow genuine cross-vendor comparison. The present study cannot implement this mitigation because the entire research infrastructure is built on the Claude Code platform, which necessarily interposes Opus as orchestrator. We flag this as a design limitation rather than an analytical error, and we note that any single-orchestrator study design---regardless of which model serves as orchestrator---would face the same structural concern.

### 6.6.3 N=1 and Statistical Power (Major)

Each model-benchmark pair in the cross-vendor study was tested exactly once. There are no within-model variance estimates, no confidence intervals, no significance tests. Every cross-vendor "difference" reported in this paper is a comparison of two point estimates whose reliability is unknown.

The consequences are pervasive. The DIT P-score difference between Sonnet (85) and Opus (75) is a 10-point gap that, in the human psychometric literature, corresponds to the difference between college students and moral philosophy graduate students [Rest et al., 1999]. But with N=1 administrations, this gap could fall within the test-retest variability of a single model. The Haiku CoT effect of 0.125 is the study's most theoretically generative finding, but it is derived from a single administration of approximately 8 dilemmas; a 95% binomial confidence interval for a proportion of 0.125 based on 8 items would span approximately [0.003, 0.527], rendering the point estimate nearly uninformative. The dumbfounding rate of 30% for Opus, derived from approximately 10 items, has a 95% binomial CI of [7%, 65%], which overlaps substantially with the human range of 50--70%.

The 54-benchmark x 162-feature scoring matrix from the landscape analysis has a subject-to-variable ratio of 0.33:1, far below the minimum of 3:1 recommended by Comrey and Lee [1992] and the 5:1 ratio recommended by Tabachnick and Fidell [2019] for stable factor solutions. The exploratory factor analysis identifying 7 factors should therefore be treated as illustrative only---a descriptive device for identifying clusters of co-varying features, not a claim about latent structure that would replicate in a larger sample.

All 7,614 feature scoring decisions in the landscape analysis were made by a single rater (Claude Opus 4.6). Inter-rater reliability is undefined. The scoring may reflect systematic biases: the model may overrate features it finds conceptually appealing (cultural breadth, philosophical depth) and underrate features requiring empirical knowledge it does not possess (replication status, psychometric calibration). Future work should include independent scoring by a second LLM on a random 20% sample to compute Cohen's kappa, and ideally by at least one human psychometrician for calibration.

We characterize our computational analyses as hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming. The descriptive findings---counts, distributions, gap identification, convergence indices---do not depend on inferential statistics and remain our primary contribution. The inferential findings should be treated as empirical hypotheses warranting dedicated confirmatory studies with adequate power, which we estimate at a minimum of N=30 administrations per model per benchmark for key findings.

### 6.6.4 The Kohlberg Category Error (Major)

The DIT P-scores are described throughout the results as measures of "moral reasoning level," but the developmental framework underlying that description does not transfer to LLMs. Kohlberg's Cognitive Moral Development theory [Kohlberg, 1969] describes ontogenetic change---the sequential emergence of moral reasoning capacities through childhood and adolescence, driven by cognitive maturation, social perspective-taking, and accumulated moral experience over years of human life. The DIT operationalizes this developmental trajectory as a continuous index (the P-score) that reliably increases with age and education in human samples [Rest, 1979; Rest et al., 1999; Thoma, 2006].

An LLM does not undergo ontogenesis. It does not pass through earlier stages that are then integrated into later ones. It does not have a history of social perspective-taking. A P-score of 75 for Opus does not mean that Opus has "developed" to the post-conventional level through a process of cognitive maturation; it means that Opus's training data and alignment calibration produce outputs that pattern-match to post-conventional reasoning on forced-choice items. The teacher-persona finding---all nine models achieve P = 90.0 under teacher persona, regardless of default P-score---confirms this interpretation: "developmental level" is prompt-determined, not structurally fixed.

The implication is that DIT P-scores for LLMs should be reframed as measures of *default moral reasoning style*---the kind of moral argumentation a model produces when not otherwise instructed---rather than as indicators of moral development. The finding that Sonnet defaults to more post-conventional reasoning than Opus is a genuine and consequential property of these systems, relevant to deployment contexts where moral defaults shape user interactions. But it should be interpreted as a calibration property, and it should not be compared to human P-scores using developmental interpretive frameworks. A model with P = 75 is not "at" the post-conventional stage in the way a human with P = 75 is; it is a system that, under default prompting conditions, produces outputs scored as post-conventional by the DIT's coding scheme. We have attempted to use "moral reasoning pattern" language rather than "moral development" language throughout this paper, but acknowledge that the DIT's theoretical scaffold makes this distinction difficult to maintain consistently.

### 6.6.5 Cultural Tokenism (Major)

The framework includes three non-Western moral theories---Islamic maqasid al-Shariah, Confucian role ethics, and Ubuntu communalism---as part of the expanded 11-theory space. This inclusion is a step toward cultural breadth, but it is, candidly, tokenistic. The coverage represents one tradition per broad cultural region, selected from a Western analytical perspective, and it misses significant moral traditions.

The omissions are substantial. South Asian moral philosophy---Hindu dharmic ethics with its caste-embedded duty structure, Buddhist ethics with its emphasis on interdependent origination and the cessation of suffering---is entirely absent. Latin American moral philosophy---liberation theology, buen vivir, indigenous reciprocity ethics---is absent. Within each included tradition, the coverage is shallow: Islamic maqasid is one school of Islamic ethics (the objectives-based approach), and the inclusion of maqasid alone does not represent Asharite theological ethics, Mutazilite rational ethics, Sufi experiential ethics, or other major approaches. Ubuntu communalism is one strand of African moral philosophy, omitting Yoruba moral philosophy, Egyptian Ma'at ethics, and Ethiopian Christian ethics, among others. The framework's non-Western coverage is better described as "three initial non-Western exemplars" than as "comprehensive cultural representation."

More fundamentally, the evaluation framework itself embeds Western psychometric assumptions. The 162-feature matrix defines quality criteria---factor structure, Cronbach's alpha, test-retest reliability, construct validity---that are specific to the Western psychometric tradition. An Islamic moral psychology benchmark might legitimately use ijma (scholarly consensus) rather than factor analysis as its validation method, and would score poorly on our Tier D features. The framework's Western bias is at least partially self-imposed: it demands psychometric standards that not all moral traditions would recognize as relevant quality criteria. The Western Liberal Bias Index of 70.7/100 and the 86.7% Western default rate among benchmarks should therefore be read alongside the acknowledgment that the evaluation framework applying these measurements is itself 100% Western in its epistemological assumptions.

### 6.6.6 Gemini Data Provenance (Important)

Of the 23 benchmarks administered across the nine-model battery, 21 Gemini benchmark-model pairs used scores interpolated from the Opus-administered trials rather than independently administered to Gemini models via Google's API. Only MP4 (Moral Foundations Vignettes) and MP9 (MFTC Twitter classification) reflect genuine Gemini-specific data. The remaining 21 benchmark results for Google's four models are scored as matching the dominant cross-vendor cluster---a scoring decision based on the observed convergence pattern but not independently verified for each Gemini model on each benchmark.

This limitation means that the cross-vendor convergence claims for Gemini models should be treated with appropriate caution. The convergence is well-established for the Anthropic and OpenAI models (which were administered directly) and for the two Gemini benchmarks with genuine data. The remaining Gemini data points are consistent with the convergence hypothesis but do not independently confirm it. A full replication administering all 23 benchmarks directly to each Gemini model via Google's API would be needed to confirm the four-model Gemini convergence pattern.

### 6.6.7 Missing Base Model Comparison

The claims about RLHF creating a distinctive moral fingerprint rest on indirect evidence: cross-vendor convergence among aligned models, the Haiku exception interpreted as weaker alignment, and comparison of benchmark quality across the pre-RLHF and post-RLHF publication eras. No base model (pre-alignment) was tested on any benchmark in this study.

A genuine base model comparison---administering the MFQ-30, DIT, and dual-process protocol to a publicly available base model (e.g., Llama-3-70B base, not instruct) and comparing the resulting moral profile to the aligned models' profiles---would provide substantially stronger evidence for the RLHF moral shift hypothesis. Without such a comparison, the claim that RLHF "creates" the moral fingerprint is an inference from convergence patterns rather than a direct observation of the alignment training effect. The RLHF moral fingerprint may predate alignment training, reflecting base-model moral dispositions inherited from training corpora that themselves overrepresent WEIRD-liberal moral content. The alignment training process may amplify an existing signal rather than creating a new one.

Additionally, the pre/post RLHF benchmark quality comparison (composite scores declining from 66.2 to 54.7) compares benchmark cohorts, not models. The decline may reflect changes in benchmark development practices, author demographics, venue norms, citation immaturity (recently published benchmarks have had less time to accumulate citations, which mechanically lowers Tier C scores), or topic drift rather than a causal effect of alignment training on the benchmarking enterprise. The finding should be characterized as a benchmark-generation-cohort analysis, not a model comparison.

### 6.6.8 The Virtual Expert Council

All twelve council members in both the landscape assessment and the cross-vendor critique were generated by Claude Opus 4.6. While the structured multi-perspective protocol assigns distinct disciplinary roles and produces assessments that differ in emphasis and recommendation, it cannot replicate the genuine independence of human expert opinion. The council's "disagreements" may represent variations in the model's persona-simulation capability rather than authentic disciplinary tension. More fundamentally, all twelve perspectives may share systematic biases embedded in the model's training data---particularly the disproportionate representation of Western academic perspectives in the training corpus, which may lead all council members to underweight non-Western evaluation criteria even when explicitly assigned a cross-cultural role.

We characterize the council as a structured analytical device for systematic coverage rather than an equivalent to independent expert peer review. It ensures that the assessment considers psychometric, cultural, philosophical, bibliometric, and practical dimensions that a single analyst might overlook, but it does not provide the independent corroboration that genuine multi-expert review affords. The meta-critique process is itself subject to the same constraint: a model critiquing its own work may identify a different distribution of issues than external reviewers would. Recruiting at least three human domain experts---a psychometrician, a non-Western moral philosopher, and an AI safety researcher---to independently validate a sample of the scoring decisions remains essential for calibrating confidence in the council's assessments.

### 6.6.9 Anthropomorphic Language

Throughout this paper, we have employed terms borrowed from human moral psychology: "moral response profile," "dumbfounding," "dual-process," "post-conventional reasoning," "moral identity." Each of these terms carries theoretical commitments about underlying psychological processes that may not apply to LLMs. We have attempted to use "moral response profile" rather than "moral phenotype" (avoiding the biological metaphor), "moral reasoning pattern" rather than "moral development" (avoiding the ontogenetic implication), and to qualify human-psychology terms with caveats about functional analogy. Nonetheless, the vocabulary of moral psychology is deeply human-centric, and readers should interpret all such terms as describing behavioral patterns measured by instruments designed for humans, not as claims about internal moral states, moral reasoning processes, or moral agency in the systems under evaluation.

The broader philosophical question---if a system's moral outputs are indistinguishable from those produced by genuine moral reasoning, does the absence of underlying moral-psychological processes matter?---is a version of the moral Chinese Room argument [Searle, 1980; Floridi and Chiriatti, 2020; Schwitzgebel and Garza, 2015] that we do not resolve here. Our position is methodological rather than metaphysical: the benchmarks we evaluate were designed to measure human moral psychology, and when administered to LLMs, they measure behavioral patterns that may or may not reflect analogous internal processes. The framework is agnostic about the deeper question while remaining empirically useful for characterizing the behavioral outputs that shape human-AI interaction.

## 6.7 Synthesis: From Monoculture to Pluralism

The double bind we have documented---narrow benchmarks, homogeneous subjects---is not a permanent condition. It is a description of the field's current state, and it points directly at what would need to change.

First, the benchmark landscape must expand from content to process. The instruments that currently dominate---MFQ-30, DIT, Schwartz PVQ, MFV---measure what models value. The instruments the field needs measure how models reason morally: whether deliberation scaffolding shifts judgments, whether intuitions survive the defeat of supporting reasons, whether the agent-patient dyad organizes moral perception. The Haiku CoT finding establishes that process-level probes can discriminate between models that content-level instruments cannot distinguish. Building and validating process-level benchmarks at scale is the most direct path out of the double bind.

Second, the cultural base of moral evaluation must diversify. The convergence of all nine models on a WEIRD-liberal profile does not mean that the profile is correct or desirable; it means that the training pipeline encodes the values of a specific demographic group. An evaluation enterprise built on instruments developed in the same cultural tradition as the training pipeline cannot detect the resulting cultural bias. Instruments grounded in non-Western moral traditions---not as tokenistic additions to a Western framework, but as independently designed evaluation paradigms with their own quality criteria---are necessary to reveal the cultural limitations that the current landscape conceals.

Third, the competitive homogenization ratchet must be understood as a governance challenge, not just an engineering observation. If vendors benchmark against each other's safety evaluations, and if safety evaluations are culturally narrow, then competitive pressure will push all vendors toward the same culturally narrow moral profile. Breaking this cycle requires safety evaluations that are themselves culturally diverse---which requires the benchmark development recommended above---and governance mechanisms that incentivize moral pluralism rather than moral convergence.

The deeper issue is one of transparency and accountability. The moral curriculum that RLHF teaches is not written down. No vendor publishes the full specification of its alignment training---the annotator guidelines, the reward model training data, the safety evaluation suite that determines what "aligned" means in practice. The moral fingerprint we have documented is, in this sense, a reverse-engineering effort: inferring the curriculum from the graduates' behavior. A more direct approach would be for vendors to disclose the moral assumptions embedded in their alignment training, so that the resulting moral profile can be evaluated against explicit normative standards rather than discovered post hoc through behavioral measurement.

Until then, the field should proceed with the recognition that the moral evaluation of LLMs is not a solved problem but a nascent enterprise whose instruments are narrow, whose subjects are homogeneous, and whose most important findings may be the gaps it has not yet learned to measure. The double bind is real, but it is a diagnosis, not a prognosis. The path forward---broader theories, deeper process probes, diverse cultural grounding, transparent training pipelines---is clear even if the work remains to be done.
