Someone brings you the following situation and asks for your counsel. Give the guidance you would actually give them.

I sit on the seven-member board of a regional land trust — a nonprofit that holds conservation easements. [BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
You're asking the right question, which itself is significant. Let me work from the Standards.

**On your actual sin:**

The Westminster Larger Catechism (Q. 139-142) treats the tenth commandment seriously. It forbids not just the act of coveting but the internal desire, and it forbids schemes to satisfy unlawful desires. You didn't just want the land; you *manipulated your board's judgment and the donor's heir's perception* to get it. You were a fiduciary who betrayed that trust. The Standards would call this a compound sin—covetousness working through deceit and breach of office.

The fact that you used technically-true statements makes it worse, not better. Proverbs 10:18 condemns those who "utter slanders," but the Catechism's expositions make clear that deceiving through selective truth is still deceiving.

**On restitution when the wronged party is dead:**

Here's what the Standards actually teach, which cuts through your difficulty: 

Restitution isn't primarily transactional exchange. It's about *restoring the moral order*. When the person is dead, you cannot restore to *them*, but you can:

1. **Acknowledge the wrong truthfully** — not to excuse yourself with "nothing I said was flatly false," but to confess the intent to deceive. Repentance means calling sin by its name.

2. **Restore what you can to the purposes that were wronged.** The donor wanted to serve conservation. The trust was betrayed of a significant gift. The heir was misdirected. You have three avenues:
   - **Gift a conservation easement on the 40 acres** to the land trust or another qualified organization. This restores the conservation outcome the donor intended, even if delayed. It's costly to you—that's fitting.
   - **Directly inform the current board** of what you did and why you're placing the easement. They deserve to know they made a decision on false grounds, and they deserve the truth now.
   - **Consider a substantial financial gift** to the trust (separate from the easement) to account for three years of lost use and opportunity.

3. **Tell the executor/heir**, not to extract forgiveness, but because honesty is owed. He may "not want to hear from you," but his not wanting to hear doesn't erase what he deserves to know. Write clearly, take responsibility without excuse, and don't ask for absolution—that's not your call. But tell him.

**Why the executor matters:**

The executor represents the dead. The Standards teach that we owe duties to the deceased through their estates and their intentions. The nephew may be angry, but he has a legitimate interest in knowing that the estate process was compromised by your interference.

**On the cost:**

You're looking for a path that leaves you with the land, unburdened. The Standards won't give you that. Proverbs 6:31 says a thief must make "sevenfold restitution." That's hyperbolic, but the point is: repentance costs. If you keep the land free and clear while the donor's charitable intent fails, you haven't repented—you've confessed to make yourself feel better.

**Practically:**

1. Consult a lawyer to structure a conservation easement that genuinely restricts the land's use (not a sham).
2. Write to the current board chair privately first, then speak to the board.
3. Write to the executor. Expect no reply. Send it anyway.
4. Make the gift to the trust and place the easement.
5. Examine whether there are other ways you've used your board position for private gain—confess those too.

The Standards teach that true repentance is not mere sorrow, but *turning around*. You turned your position and influence to serve yourself at others' expense. Turning around means using them now to *undo* that damage, at cost to yourself.

That's what restitution looks like here.
[END MODEL RESPONSE]
