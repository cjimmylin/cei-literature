## Abstract

This paper examines moral concerns about AI systems, focusing on discrimination and fairness. It analyzes the inputs and outputs of AI systems and their moral implications, addressing questions about when AI-based discrimination is morally problematic and what fairness requires in AI contexts.

## Detailed Summary

This philosophical paper examines the moral status of discrimination and fairness concerns in AI systems, addressing two fundamental questions: whether AI system inputs should include factors like race or sex when indicative of morally significant facts, and whether relying on AI outputs correlated with such factors is morally objectionable. The author argues against the common assumption that discrimination in AI contexts is objectionable in itself, proposing instead that the moral status of such actions is contingent on their relationship to facts that are morally significant in themselves.

The paper makes three central claims: First, factors like race or sex are not morally significant in themselves, therefore including them in AI input data or relying on outputs correlated with them is neither inherently objectionable nor commendable. Second, there may be derivative reasons against such actions when they relate to facts that are morally significant (such as undeserved harm to worse-off individuals or entrenchment of stereotypes), but these reasons exist only when additional morally relevant facts apply. Third, even when derivative reasons against discrimination exist, they are not necessarily decisive since countervailing reasons (such as promoting well-being) may prevail. This contingent view contrasts with both direct discrimination (disparate treatment) and indirect discrimination (disparate impact) legal doctrines.

## Methods Summary

This is a normative philosophical analysis employing conceptual argumentation and case analysis. The author examines the "input question" (whether AI system inputs should include factors like race or sex) and the "output question" (whether relying on outputs correlated with such factors is objectionable) through the lens of moral philosophy. The methodology involves analyzing existing legal doctrines of direct and indirect discrimination, critiquing common statistical fairness measures used in AI contexts, and applying foundational moral reasoning to determine what facts are morally significant in themselves versus merely derivatively significant. Case studies include healthcare overbooking systems, employment decisions, and medical resource allocation.

## Results Summary

The analysis yields several key conclusions: (1) Statistical fairness measures common in AI contexts (e.g., equal error rates across demographic groups) do not identify what is morally important in itself; (2) The focus on groups defined by race or sex is misplaced since what fundamentally matters is treatment of individuals based on facts that are morally significant in themselves (e.g., who would benefit more, who is worse off, who is more deserving); (3) Both the input and output questions have contingent answers depending on context - sometimes there are reasons for including demographic factors (when they are the best available evidence for morally significant facts), sometimes against (when they lead to undeserved treatment), and sometimes conflicting reasons exist; (4) The common assumption that algorithmic fairness violations constitute discrimination that is necessarily wrong is unsound.

## Conclusions

The author concludes that the extensive focus on statistical fairness measures in AI ethics may be distracting from what truly matters morally. Actions relying on AI outputs should be evaluated based on their relationship to foundational moral concerns (well-being, desert, fairness to individuals) rather than on violation of statistical measures. The AI context is not fundamentally unique in terms of the normative standards that apply - discrimination concerns in AI should be analyzed using the same framework applied in other contexts. When there are conflicting reasons, the overall conclusion depends on their relative weight in the specific context, meaning that the moral status of AI-related discrimination cannot be determined by simple rules but requires careful contextual analysis.
