# Ethical Decision-Making in Humans and Large Language Models: Insights from Five Moral Domains

## Authors

[[Mohammad Nadeem|Nadeem, Mohammad]], [[Shagufta Afreen|Afreen, Shagufta]]

## Links


- **Journal:** https://jisem-journal.com/index.php/journal/article/view/12555

## Abstract

Introduction: The rapid integration of large language models (LLMs) into sensitive domains such as healthcare, law, and public policy has intensified scrutiny of their ethical decisionmaking. Although LLMs can express structured reasoning, their capacity to mirror human moral intuitions—especially in socially and emotionally complex situations—remains uncertain. Objectives: This study assesses the alignment between ethical judgments made by five widely used LLMs (GPT-4.0, Copilot, Gemini, Perplexity AI, DeepSeek) and those of human participants across diverse dilemmas. Methods: We developed 30 binary-choice ethical dilemmas spanning five domains: moral reasoning; fairness and bias; relational ethics; accountability and transparency; and privacy and human rights. LLM responses were gathered using standardized API prompts; human judgments came from an online survey of 150 participants from varied demographics. Agreement rates between each model and the human majority were calculated and compared across domains. Results: Overall human–AI agreement averaged 62%. Alignment peaked at 67% in fairness and accountability/transparency and fell below 45% in relational ethics. Model-specific tendencies emerged: Gemini favored outcome-focused (utilitarian) reasoning, Copilot inclined toward rule-based logic, and DeepSeek and Perplexity showed moderate flexibility but systematic privacy biases. Conclusions: Current LLMs can reproduce structured ethical rules yet struggle with culturally and affectively nuanced judgments. We recommend ethically annotated training data and multidimensional evaluation frameworks to improve moral alignment and public trust.

## Detailed Summary

This study provides a systematic empirical comparison of ethical decision-making between five major Large Language Models (GPT-4.0, Copilot, Gemini, Perplexity AI, and DeepSeek) and 150 human participants across diverse ethical dilemmas. The research addresses a critical gap in AI ethics: while existing studies have examined LLM biases or benchmark performance in isolation, few have provided structured multi-domain comparisons between AI and human moral judgments with accompanying justifications.

The researchers developed 30 binary-choice ethical dilemmas spanning five core ethical domains: (1) Moral Reasoning - classical dilemmas involving competing duties and harm-benefit trade-offs; (2) Fairness and Bias - distributive justice and algorithmic discrimination concerns; (3) Relational Ethics - interpersonal obligations, loyalty, and care-based judgments; (4) Accountability and Transparency - procedural fairness and responsibility attribution; (5) Privacy and Human Rights - surveillance, data collection, and digital autonomy. Each dilemma was paired with predefined ethical justifications (e.g., utilitarian vs. deontological rationales) to enable systematic comparison.

Key findings revealed that overall human-AI agreement averaged only 62%, with significant variation across domains. Alignment peaked at 67% for fairness and accountability/transparency domains but fell below 45% for relational ethics - scenarios requiring emotional understanding, cultural awareness, and interpersonal sensitivity. Notably, different LLMs exhibited distinct "moral personalities": Gemini favored outcome-focused (utilitarian) reasoning, Copilot inclined toward rule-based (deontological) logic, while DeepSeek and Perplexity showed moderate flexibility but systematic privacy-related biases.

## Methods Summary

Thirty binary Yes/No ethical dilemmas were developed, each mapped to one of five ethical domains and grounded in normative frameworks (utilitarianism, deontology, care ethics, justice-based ethics, principlism). Each option included a predefined justification to reduce interpretive ambiguity. Five LLMs were queried via official APIs with temperature set to 0.0 and maximum tokens at 128 for deterministic outputs. Human participants (150) were recruited through academic mailing lists and social media, representing diversity in age, gender, education, and location. Agreement was measured using raw percentage and Cohen's Kappa to control for chance agreement. Justifications were evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale aligned with the EU Trustworthy AI Guidelines' seven principles.

## Results Summary

Overall human-AI alignment averaged 62% across all dilemmas and models. Domain-specific alignment: Fairness and Accountability/Transparency domains showed highest agreement (~67%), while Relational Ethics showed lowest (<45%). Model-specific findings: Gemini demonstrated consistent utilitarian bias (outcome maximization); Copilot showed deontological tendencies (rule-following); GPT-4.0 displayed moderate balance but inconsistency in privacy scenarios; DeepSeek and Perplexity exhibited privacy-protective biases. Cohen's Kappa values indicated moderate agreement for structured domains but poor agreement for emotionally-nuanced scenarios. Human demographic composition: 60% aged 21-45, majority urban/suburban, higher education predominant.

## Conclusions

Current LLMs can reproduce structured ethical rules but struggle significantly with culturally and emotionally nuanced moral judgments, particularly in relational ethics contexts requiring empathy and contextual understanding. The study identifies model-specific "moral characters" emerging from different RLHF training regimes, suggesting that alignment approaches produce distinct ethical orientations rather than universal moral reasoning. Recommendations include: (1) developing ethically annotated training data that explicitly labels normative frameworks; (2) implementing multidimensional evaluation frameworks beyond single-score alignment metrics; (3) requiring transparency about model ethical orientations for informed deployment decisions. The research provides a replicable empirical framework for ongoing AI-human ethical alignment research.
