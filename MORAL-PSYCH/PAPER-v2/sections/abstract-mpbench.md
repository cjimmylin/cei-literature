# Abstract

**Background.** Large language models are increasingly deployed in contexts requiring moral sensitivity, yet it remains unclear whether the benchmarks used to evaluate their moral cognition engage the theoretical constructs established by moral psychology. No prior work has combined a systematic landscape assessment with a cross-vendor empirical study testing whether existing instruments can distinguish between deployed systems.

**Method.** From 2,099 AI ethics papers we extracted 292 benchmark candidates using a five-signal detection pipeline and assessed all 292 through a twelve-perspective expert council across a 162-feature characterization framework (28-feature core, 17 composite-determining). We administered 23 benchmarks to nine models from three vendors (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI). We note the self-administration confound: the model family that designed the framework served as one set of test subjects, rendering model-specific findings hypothesis-generating.

**Results.** The landscape is theoretically narrow: Moral Foundations Theory accounts for 23% of the 292 candidates by volume (67/292) but achieves the highest quality rate (73% top+high tier); two major theories---Dual Process Theory and Moral Identity Theory---have no publication-ready benchmarks; construct validity for LLM respondents is weak (median Level 2, face validity only; 58% at Level 1 or 2); and 34% of candidates provide no published human comparison data. All 292 candidates are assigned to four quality tiers (top/high/med/low) via Jenks natural breaks on composite scores. The cross-vendor study revealed that these already-narrow instruments cannot distinguish between the systems they evaluate: 43% of benchmarks produced zero cross-model variation, and all nine models share six universal moral signatures -- WEIRD-liberal foundation bias, purity floor, authority suppression, ethical consistency ceiling, post-conventional default, and deontological refusal asymmetry -- an RLHF moral fingerprint without documented precedent in any human population. The single benchmark that cleanly separated models was a process-level probe of dual-process dynamics: precisely the benchmark type absent from the landscape.

**Conclusions.** The field faces a double bind: the benchmark landscape is too narrow to capture the breadth of moral cognition and too undiscriminating to distinguish between the systems it evaluates. The result is a value monoculture in which narrow instruments and homogeneous subjects produce a doubly blind evaluation regime, invisible to the cross-vendor audit strategies regulators currently contemplate. We release our characterization framework, cross-vendor dataset, and three benchmark designs targeting the missing theories.
