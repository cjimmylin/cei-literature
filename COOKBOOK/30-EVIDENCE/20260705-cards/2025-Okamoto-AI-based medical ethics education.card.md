## 1. Gist
This paper argues that large language models, specifically ChatGPT-type systems, can serve as tools for cultivating virtues in medical ethics education. Working within a "second-best" or "practicality over perfection" framing, the authors propose that LLMs can function both as exemplars of virtuous behaviour (drawing on Linda Zagzebski's exemplarist moral theory) and as advisors that provoke critical reflection rather than delivering authoritative moral verdicts. The paper matters for values-alignment benchmarking because it identifies concrete criteria—empathy, moral sensitivity, non-principlist reasoning—that LLMs should exhibit, and it documents ChatGPT's systematic bias toward principlism (Beauchamp & Childress four-principles framework) when using naive prompts, highlighting a real alignment gap.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
Not a benchmark paper. Core claims: (1) Medical ethics education currently over-relies on principlist frameworks and lacks non-principlist virtue cultivation; (2) LLMs default to principlism unless explicitly prompted otherwise; (3) With careful prompt engineering, even current ChatGPT can exhibit empathy-focused responses that could scaffold virtue education; (4) The "v-rules" framework (act compassionately, act honestly) from neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics can guide LLM design; (5) LLMs should be treated as advisors rather than testimonial authorities, preserving students' epistemic autonomy.

A key normative claim: current unmodified LLMs "imply a hierarchy of ethical frameworks atop which principlism is presumed to sit" (Rahimzadeh et al., cited p.4), making them poorly suited for virtue education without prompt engineering.

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- No empirical evaluation of LLMs in actual medical ethics curricula.
- The "second-best" framing admits LLMs are not ideal but does not specify what standards must be met before deployment.
- Principlist default bias in current ChatGPT is a documented problem but proposed fix (prompt engineering) is vague.
- Cultural diversity of virtue traditions is acknowledged (reference to "Alternative virtues: Japanese perspectives on Christian and Confucian traditions") but not systematically incorporated.
- Generational value shifts in medicine (work-life balance debates) may mean LLMs trained on historical texts encode outdated norms.
- No discussion of hallucination risks in ethics-sensitive contexts.

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- **LLM-as-exemplar framing**: Rather than testing whether LLMs give "correct" ethical answers, evaluate whether they can model virtuous reasoning processes. This shifts benchmark design from answer accuracy to process quality.
- **Advisor vs. testimonial witness distinction**: A benchmark dimension: does the LLM invite further reflection (advisor stance) or deliver authoritative verdicts (testimony stance)? Could be operationalized via follow-up questions that probe whether the model explains its reasoning and acknowledges uncertainty.
- **V-rules as evaluation targets**: "Act compassionately," "act honestly," etc. could form the basis of a rubric for scoring LLM responses to clinical vignettes — not just "is this principlism or virtue ethics?" but "does the response demonstrate v-rule enactment?"
- **Principlism default as an alignment failure**: Documenting that current LLMs default to a specific ethical framework when not prompted is a concrete, testable alignment property. Benchmarks could measure framework diversity under neutral prompts.
- **Prompt sensitivity as a validity threat**: If different prompts produce radically different ethical frameworks, this is low construct validity for any single-prompt ethics evaluation.
