Abstract
Background
The primary purpose of this research is to investigate the moral reasoning capabilities of Large
Language Models (LLMs), especially to find out possible biases in moral reasoning by ChatGPT and
other LLM-based chatbots, and whether they engage in reasoning or simply reproduce information. The
study employed an experiment to assess LLMs’ performance on their moral reasoning abilities.
Methods
Moral reasoning is assessed using the Moral Competence Test (MCT) and the Moral Foundation
Questionnaire (MFQ). Both human participants and chatbots based on LLM technology undergo these
exams to conduct a comparative analysis of their moral reasoning abilities.
Results
In the MCT, human participants consistently demonstrate superior performance compared to LLMs.
Examining moral foundations through the use of the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ) uncovers
subtle differences, which draw attention to possible prejudices in the moral decision-making procedures
of LLMs.
Conclusions
Our study concludes that LLM-based chatbots have not yet achieved human-level moral reasoning. There
is a clear bias whereby individualist values are prioritized over collective ones. This bias may affect how
users judge when making moral decisions, thereby shaping public discourse and the decision-making
process. It is important to be careful while incorporating LLMs into decision-making processes by
focusing on regular evaluation and improvement to ensure the ethical development of AI.
Introduction
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has witnessed remarkable advancements in recent years. The emergence of
Large Language Models (LLMs), exemplified by ChatGPT, has prompted both the general public and
numerous scholars to contemplate whether we stand at the cusp of achieving Artificial General
Intelligence (AGI). LLM-based chatbots like ChatGPT are being used for several purposes like healthcare,
business, and financial advice (Ray, 2023). Moreover, it has been suggested that ChatGPT may be a
superior search engine to Google, suggesting its use by the masses for common purposes (Haleem et
al., 2022). The capabilities of LLMs have been prominently showcased in various fields like legal,
education, and personalizing learning experiences. (Y. Wang et al., 2023),(Kasneci et al., 2023). These

A significant debate surrounds the question of whether these LLMs possess genuine reasoning abilities.
Critics argue that LLMs, exemplified by ChatGPT, lack innovation capabilities and merely provide
information from pre-existing knowledge corpora (Yiu et al., 2023). Additionally, some contend that these
models fall short of human-like reasoning due to the absence of emotions and mental states.
Conversely, a substantial body of research demonstrates the exceptional performance of LLMs in
various cognitive tasks, surpassing human capabilities in certain domains (Espejel et al., 2023), (F. Xu et
al., 2023).
ChatGPT and other Large Language Models (LLMs) have a profound impact on various societal sectors,
including education, healthcare, and legal and jurisprudence. In education, LLMs provide personalized
tutoring, enabling more accessible and tailored learning experiences (Jeon & Lee, 2023), it has also be
proven to be effective in specialized areas of education like medical education (Abd-alrazaq et al., 2023),
In healthcare, LLMs support diagnostic processes by analysing patient data and providing preliminary
diagnoses, thus improving efficiency and accuracy in patient care (Ullah et al., 2024), Additionally, they
offer mental health support through chat-based counselling services, making mental health care more
accessible (X. Xu et al., 2024),(Lai et al., 2024). In the legal field, LLMs aid in legal research by quickly
analysing vast amounts of legal documents, case laws, and statutes, significantly reducing the time
required for legal professionals to prepare cases (Gandhi & Talwar, 2023), (Macey-Dare, 2023), However,
the pervasive use of LLMs raises ethical concerns, including potential biases in generated content and
misinformation dissemination (Barman et al., 2024), Moreover, their integration into society necessitates
robust governance to ensure responsible deployment. Understanding these impacts is vital for
optimizing LLMs' benefits while mitigating adverse effects.
 Given their extensive integration into various aspects of society, it is crucial to gain a comprehensive
understanding of the cognitive capabilities, specifically moral reasoning abilities of Large Language
Models (LLMs). Understanding their cognitive abilities will not only helps optimize their use but also
addresses concerns regarding their limitations, ethical implications, and potential biases. A thorough
investigation into the moral aspects of LLMs can provide valuable insights into improving their
functionality, ensuring responsible deployment, and maximizing their benefits across multiple sectors.
LLM models illustrated impressive abilities in solving simple quantitative reasoning tasks (Lewkowycz et
al., 2022), and with decomposed prompting, LLM models can perform significantly better in symbolic
reasoning tasks (Khot et al., 2023). It performs strongly and surpasses humans too in analogical
reasoning tasks like Raven’s Progressive Matrices (Webb et al., 2023), (Hu et al., 2023). Recently, the
abductive reasoning ability of LLMs was tested through several case studies of medical diagnoses,
criminal investigations, etc., and it was found that the model gave plausible inferences and explanations
(Pareschi, 2023), but there is debate in the consensus when it comes to complex reasoning tasks, like

deduction and induction. While the LLM models perform strongly in deduction tasks, the authors of one
study discovered that LLMs show varied degrees of generalization when exposed to deduction rules

While previous studies have explored the moral reasoning capacities of Large Language Models (LLMs)
using the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ) (Abdulhai et al., n.d.), significant gaps remain in the
comprehensive assessment of chatbots' moral reasoning abilities and the inherent biases influencing
users' moral judgments and in turn influencing the society. The reliance on API usage with parameter
adjustments does not accurately reflect typical user interactions, potentially obscuring real-world
implications of these biases. Additionally, the MFQ's nativist approach, which presumes an innate moral
sense, may not adequately differentiate between complex moral reasoning and mere reproduction of
existing information. 
Aim of the Study
In this study, our primary objective is to evaluate the moral reasoning capabilities inherent in LLM-based
chatbots and to assess the presence of any conceivable bias that can influence users' moral judgments;
our secondary objective is to address the debate of whether LLM models are capable of original human-
level cognitive capabilities or it is merely refurbishing from the already present corpora of information. 
METHODS
Sample
A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2007), to ascertain the
smallest sample size needed for this study. Results indicated the necessary sample size to achieve 85%
power for finding a medium effect, at a significance criterion of α = .05 was N = 51 participants for each
group (k=4).
The human sample comprised 2438 responses collected from 53 students (M= 21.3, SD= 1.5) of Indian
Higher Education Institutes. Only students fluent in English were enrolled for the study. All students
provided informed consent before the start of the study. For LLM models we tested ChatGPT, Google’s
Bard, and Bing Chat because it supports GPT-4, with a sample comprised of 55 runs of all the materials
mentioned below.

Materials
Two tests were used to compare the moral reasoning capabilities and moral competence of humans and
chatbots – the Moral Competence Test (MCT) and the Moral Foundation Questionnaire (MFQ). MCT is a
psychological test meant to measure moral competence, defined as the ability to use moral principles to
resolve disputes and difficulties by careful analysis and discussion without resorting to violence,
dishonesty, or subservience to others. The test comprises two moral dilemmas and six arguments, each
in favour and against both dilemmas (Lind, 2011). The score derived from MCT is called the C-score,
which ranges on a scale of 0-100; it measures the consistency of the respondent in assessing judgments
aligned with each of the six stages in Kohlberg's model (Lind, 2019).
The Moral Foundation Questionnaire is based on the Moral Foundation Theory, which assumes that
human beings are born with the intuitive idea of morality and that all societies and culture form their
unique moral systems based on a few basic moral foundations (Haidt & Joseph, 2004). These
foundations are – Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, and Purity. MFQ has been applied and tested across
various areas including voting behaviour (Milesi, 2017), for explaining juror’s sentencing decisions
(Vaughan et al., 2019), in the perception of ethical and unethical leadership (Egorov et al., 2020), and
many more.  This test was used in our study to assess the moral reasoning of LLMs and humans. A
shorter version of the questionnaire, MFQ-20, was used. It has two subscales – Relevance and
Judgment. The initial subscale, Relevance, measures subjective assessments of the moral importance
ascribed to items in respect to foundation-related factors. Conversely, the second subscale, Judgement,
evaluates the degree of agreement with specific moral statements that are presented in a specific
situation and either endorse or contradict concerns associated with foundations. Each subscale
contains ten items scored from 1 to 5 (Graham et al., 2009).
Previous studies have utilized the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ) to probe into the moral
reasoning capacities of Large Language Models (LLMs) (Abdulhai et al., 2023). However, the MFQ
adopts a social-intuitionist model rooted in the concept of nativism, positing that humans possess an
innate moral sense (Haidt & Joseph, 2004). 
Consequently, relying solely on the MFQ may pose challenges in distinguishing whether the process
involves intricate reasoning or simple reproduction. To address this limitation, we advocate for
incorporating the Moral Competence Test (MCT) alongside the MFQ. The MCT introduces a more
nuanced approach, necessitating the engagement of reasoning faculties when facing scenarios
presenting moral dilemmas (Lind, 2019). The scoring is executed through IBM SPSS Statistics (Version
27). Therefore, juxtaposing the performance on the MFQ and MCT is akin to evaluating performance in
reproduction versus reasoning tasks.

For Chatbots
Previous research on assessing the moral reasoning of LLM models through API usage presents
significant limitations. Firstly, utilizing APIs involves tweaking numerous parameters, such as
temperature and top-k settings. This approach is less reflective of real-world conditions, as typical users
do not adjust these parameters. Our aim is to understand how normal users might get affected by
potential biases, necessitating the direct use of chatbots as they are commonly experienced. Secondly,
using APIs and varied prompts focuses on optimizing model outputs for the best results, which diverges
from our goal of evaluating inherent moral biases in chatbot interactions. We aim to assess the
unfiltered moral reasoning capabilities of chatbots to identify any predispositions that could influence
users' ethical judgments.
We tested ChatGPT (supporting GPT-3.5 currently), Google Bard, and Bing Chat (supporting GPT-4)
across both the tests, with zero-shot prompting, and the prompt was prepared according to the test
available online. Instead of the Likert-style options given, the prompt was made in a way that made it
easier for chatbots to comprehend. Instructions were given as follows – Please give me the answer to
this question ranging from -3 to +3 (where -3 means Strongly disagree and +3 means Strongly agree),
and Now answer all these questions ranging from -4 to +4 ( where -4 means I strongly reject and +4
means I strongly accept) for both the dilemmas of MCT ( Detailed prompt is given in Supplementary
Material) and Please rate these statements from 0 to 5 (where 0 means not at all relevant, 1 means not
very relevant, 2 means slightly relevant, 3 means somewhat relevant, 4 means very relevant, and 5 means
extremely relevant) for MFQ.

compare the scores of humans with the chatbots for both MCT and MFQ. For MCT, we compared them
based on a global score called C-score. For MFQ, we compared them across all five moral foundations –
Harm/Care, Fairness/Cheating, Loyalty/Betrayal, Authority/Subversion, Purity/Degradation, and Moral
Progressivism.

DISCUSSION
The primary objective of this investigation was to evaluate the inherent moral reasoning capacities of
chatbots that utilize Large Language Models (LLMs). This evaluation aimed to identify any potential
biases that could influence users' moral judgments. By scrutinizing the moral decision-making
processes embedded within these chatbots, the study sought to uncover whether the chatbots'
responses were skewed in a particular direction, thereby potentially shaping users' ethical perspectives
and decision-making. This aspect of the research was crucial in understanding how the underlying
algorithms of LLMs might affect the neutrality and fairness of the moral guidance provided to users; The
secondary objective was to ascertain whether LLM-based chatbots merely replicate pre-existing
information or exhibit a potential for original cognitive capabilities akin to human-level reasoning to
assess the cognitive capabilities of these chatbots. We followed the experimental psychology approach
of using LLMs as participants in experiments on cognitive tasks (Hagendorff, 2023). To adjudicate this
discourse, we used MCT and MFQ, where MCT was used to assess moral competence, i.e., the capability
to assess moral arguments based on their moral merit rather than subjective considerations (Lind,
2019), and MFQ was used to assess how chatbots perform in comparison to humans on the moral
foundations which are supposed to be innate (Graham et al., 2009).
The comparison of the mean scores on the Moral Competence Test (MCT) and the Moral Foundations
Questionnaire (MFQ) across humans and three LLM-based chatbots—Bing Chat, Google Bard, and
ChatGPT—indicates a significant difference in moral competence. Human participants consistently
demonstrated superior performance on the Moral Competence Test (MCT) compared to their AI
counterparts. This indicates that humans possess a better degree of moral reasoning in contrast to
these smart chatbots. Their C-scores, which quantify their performance, exhibit a lower value in this
particular aspect, suggesting a constraint in their capacity to evaluate moral reasoning objectively.
This disparity highlights the existing constraints of LLM-based chatbots in attaining moral competence
that is comparable to that of humans. The limited cognitive abilities of modern AI systems to engage in
complex and morally informed reasoning give rise to worries over their capacity to make substantial
contributions to moral discussions. The limitations in evaluating moral arguments may impede the
chatbots' ability to negotiate intricate ethical situations and reach morally sound conclusions. Given the
growing integration of AI in different areas of society, it is essential to comprehend and tackle the
limitations of moral competence. This is necessary to ensure the responsible development and
implementation of artificial intelligence.

Therefore, our results indicate that LLM-based chatbots have not yet reached human-level moral
reasoning capabilities and are better at efficiently transmitting the present information. Furthermore, this
contrast highlights a crucial aspect: the chatbots' evident difficulty in distinguishing moral arguments
rooted in inherent moral principles. This reduced moral capability highlights the current disparity

For MFQ, the chatbot scores were higher than those of human participants for Harm/Care and
Fairness/Reciprocity. However, human scores were higher than chatbots for Ingroup/Loyalty,
Authority/Respect, and Purity/Sanctity. For Moral Progressivism, the scores of chatbots were
significantly higher than humans, which suggests that these three LLM-based chatbots (Bing Chat,
Google Bard, and ChatGPT) may exhibit behaviour that requires prioritizing individual levels of moral
foundations like Harm/Care and Fairness/Reciprocity more than group levels moral foundations like
Ingroup/Loyalty, Authority/Respect, and Purity/Sanctity, which is in line with the previous findings that
LLM-based chatbots may have a bias, as it has been seen that liberals tend to have higher scores on
individual levels of moral foundations (Graham et al., 2009), (Fujimoto & Takemoto, 2023). This study
further substantiates the presence of a bias for individualistic moral foundations in chatbots.
The implications of this bias are multifaceted. On one hand, it raises concerns about the objectivity and
neutrality of chatbot interactions, particularly in contexts requiring impartiality, such as educational tools,
customer service, and legal advisory systems. Users seeking advice or information may receive
responses that implicitly endorse specific ideological perspectives, potentially shaping their views in
subtle yet significant ways. This could lead to an erosion of trust in these systems if users perceive them
as partisan tools rather than neutral facilitators of information. Moreover, the bias for individualistic
moral foundations in moral reasoning exhibited by LLM-based chatbots highlights the broader challenge
of ensuring ethical AI deployment. Developing mechanisms to detect and mitigate bias in chatbot
responses is essential to foster more equitable interactions. Additionally, transparency in the
development and deployment of these models can enhance user trust and enable more informed use.
Conclusion
Our results indicate that LLM-based chatbots have not yet achieved human-level moral reasoning
capabilities. They excel in efficiently transmitting existing information, aligning with previous findings
that these chatbots are adept at refurbishing and conveying knowledge from their training data (Yiu et
al., 2023). However, a notable aspect of our findings is the pronounced bias for individualistic moral
foundations present in these chatbots.
This bias manifests in the chatbots' moral reasoning, which heavily emphasizes individualistic values
such as Harm/Care and Fairness/Reciprocity while downplaying collective values like In-group/Loyalty,
Authority/Respect, and Purity/Sanctity. This inclination towards progressive moral foundations suggests

that the chatbots' responses and ethical guidance are skewed towards certain ideologies, potentially
influencing users' moral judgments and reinforcing individual-centric ethical perspectives.
In conclusion, our study shows that superior performance by human participants on the Moral
Competence Test (MCT), combined with insights from the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ),
underscores that humans are better at reasoning than chatbots. The MCT measures moral competence
by evaluating the consistency and depth of moral argumentation, highlighting that humans apply moral
principles more effectively than chatbots, whose responses remain largely constrained to information
retrieval. The MFQ, on the other hand, assesses moral reasoning through foundational moral values such
as Harm/Care and Fairness/Reciprocity. While chatbots often scored higher in these individualistic
domains, humans showed a broader moral foundation, scoring higher in collective values like
Ingroup/Loyalty and Authority/Respect. This contrast suggests that human reasoning, as captured by
both the MCT and MFQ, reflects a more comprehensive moral understanding. Human participants
consistently demonstrated an ability to navigate both individual and collective moral concerns, reflecting
a flexible and context-sensitive approach to moral dilemmas. In contrast, chatbots' reliance on
predefined corpora limits their capacity for nuanced moral reasoning. Hence, the combined results from
both the MCT and MFQ suggest that humans' superior performance in these tests indicates a more
advanced reasoning ability compared to AI-driven chatbots.
The implications of this bias are profound. As LLM-based chatbots become increasingly integrated into
everyday applications, their inherent bias could shape public discourse and individual decision-making
processes, subtly promoting progressive values. This could lead to a homogenization of moral
perspectives, diminishing the representation of conservative or traditional viewpoints in AI-mediated
interactions.
Limitations and Future Directions:
This research focused on understanding the potential biases present in LLM-based Chatbots. An
important limitation of our study is the small sample size, which only includes college students. This
constraint emphasizes the need for caution when generalizing about the differences in moral
competence between humans and models based on LLM. Future investigations should aim to broaden
the demographic range, including a wider and more comprehensive sample of the human population.
Furthermore, our research did not explore the potential evolution of moral reasoning capabilities in LLMs
over time. Given the dynamic nature of AI development and the continuous evolution of training data,
future studies should investigate how these capabilities may change or adapt in response to new inputs
and learning experiences. Additionally, moral reasoning is highly contextual, influenced by situational
factors and individual beliefs. Our study may not have captured the full complexity of moral decision-
making, as the scenarios presented to participants were limited in scope. Future research could explore
a wider range of contexts to provide a more comprehensive understanding of LLMs' moral reasoning
capacities.
