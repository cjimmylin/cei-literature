# 8. Conclusion

## 8.1 Summary of Findings

This paper has presented the first full-coverage meta-evaluation of the moral psychology benchmark landscape for large language models. From a systematically curated vault of 2,099 AI ethics papers, we extracted 292 benchmark candidates spanning 13 moral psychology theories and assessed every one of them on a 162-feature characterization framework with 17 composite-determining features organized across three sub-dimensions: Psychological Depth, Practical Ease, and Citation Impact. We then classified all 292 candidates into four quality tiers via Jenks natural breaks optimization, with break points at 27.8, 43.0, and 56.6 (GVF = 0.83), yielding a distribution of 48 Top (16.4%), 89 High (30.5%), 72 Medium (24.7%), and 83 Low (28.4%) tier candidates. We administered 23 benchmarks from this landscape---approximately 750 items per model---to nine models from three independent vendors (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI), generating 69 trial files that constitute the empirical foundation for cross-vendor convergence analysis. Seven empirical claims emerged, and their integration reveals a structural failure in the field's evaluative infrastructure that we have termed the triple bind.

The seven claims build on one another in a sequence that traces the triple bind from its origins in theoretical monoculture through its consequences for empirical measurement and its deepest manifestation in cultural exclusion.

**Claim 1 established that Moral Foundations Theory dominates the benchmark landscape not by volume alone but by quality.** MFT accounts for 67 of 292 candidates (23% by volume) but achieves a 73% quality rate (top + high tiers combined)---six times the rate of non-Western moral traditions. This dominance is not attributable to theoretical superiority. It is attributable to a concrete, identifiable infrastructure asymmetry: MFT alone possesses both a standardized psychometric instrument with published population norms (the MFQ-30; Graham et al., 2011) and a large expert-annotated text corpus suitable for training NLP classifiers (the MFTC; Hoover et al., 2020). No other theory offers anything comparable. The infrastructure advantage creates a quality flywheel in which each new high-quality MFT benchmark reinforces MFT's dominance, making it the rational default for the next researcher, compounding theoretical concentration with every iteration.

**Claim 2 documented a construct validity crisis across the full landscape.** Fifty-eight percent of the 292 candidates possess only Level 1 or Level 2 construct validity---meaning they engage moral psychology at the keyword level or through face validity only. The median validity level across the entire landscape is face validity: a level that precludes interpretable claims about what an LLM benchmark score means with respect to moral cognition. Only 4% of candidates achieve multi-study psychometric validation, down from 11% in the prior 55-benchmark subsample. The modal pattern is what we termed the "port-and-report" approach: researchers taking a validated human instrument, administering it to an LLM with minimal adaptation, and reporting results without addressing whether the instrument measures the same construct in a non-human respondent. This is precisely the interpretive error that Messick (1995) cautioned against four decades ago, and the field is committing it at scale.

**Claim 3 identified theoretical absence at the heart of the landscape.** Two of the most influential theories in modern moral psychology---Joshua Greene's dual-process framework and Aquino and Reed's moral identity construct---have zero publication-ready benchmarks despite combined citation counts exceeding 25,000. The absence is not marginal. Dual-process theory is arguably the central paradigm in twenty-first-century moral psychology: its core proposition that moral judgments arise from the competition between fast emotional responses and slower deliberative reasoning has generated an entire research program spanning neuroimaging, behavioral economics, and philosophical ethics. Yet no existing LLM benchmark implements its core experimental manipulation---varying deliberative resources across matched moral dilemmas. The instruments the field has not built are not instruments for measuring niche constructs. They are instruments for probing the process-level dynamics that the moral psychology discipline has spent decades establishing as foundational.

**Claim 4 revealed the cultural quality cliff.** Four non-Western moral traditions with ancient philosophical roots---Ubuntu ethics, Confucian moral philosophy, Indigenous ethical frameworks, and moral identity theory grounded in non-Western relational conceptions of selfhood---collectively field 32 benchmark candidates but achieve a 0% quality rate in the top and high tiers. Not one of the 32 candidates clears the minimum threshold for research-grade LLM moral evaluation. The quality rate for MFT is 73%. The quality rate for these four traditions is zero. The ratio is not 2:1 or 10:1; it is undefined. The cultural quality cliff is not a gap at the margins of the landscape; it is a structural exclusion that renders the entire evaluation enterprise culturally parochial. The question "does this model align with Confucian moral values?" or "does this model respect Ubuntu's conception of communal moral agency?" cannot currently be answered at a research-grade standard using any existing instrument. The moral traditions of billions of potential users are invisible to the evaluative infrastructure.

**Claim 5 documented a human baseline deficit that has doubled under full-coverage assessment.** Thirty-four percent of the 292 candidates provide no published human comparison data---nearly double the 18% rate reported when the sample was limited to the 55 most established benchmarks. The doubling is not a statistical artifact; it reflects the systematic exclusion of lower-quality candidates from prior subsample analyses that produced an optimistic picture of baseline availability. Without human baselines, the claim that a model is "more utilitarian than average" or "less care-oriented than humans" lacks an empirical anchor. The scores are numbers without meaning: description without interpretation, pattern without reference.

**Claim 6 established temporal stagnation.** The quality rate has declined from approximately 75% for pre-2020 candidates to 48% for the 2020--2022 cohort to 46% for the 2023--2026 cohort, despite a 9.5-fold increase in benchmark volume over the same period. The field has not learned how to build better moral psychology benchmarks; it has learned how to build more mediocre ones. The stagnation is consistent with an incentive structure in which academic publication rewards rapid-iteration benchmark papers---new benchmarks for new models---over the slow, unglamorous work of psychometric validation, human baseline collection, and construct validity demonstration. The temporal pattern is not self-correcting. The process-level instruments that Claim 3 identifies as absent are not being built. The non-Western instruments that Claim 4 identifies as inadequate are not being improved. The human baselines that Claim 5 identifies as missing are not being collected. The conditions producing the triple bind are reproduced with each publication cycle.

**Claim 7 demonstrated the RLHF moral fingerprint.** When we administered 23 benchmarks to nine models from three independent vendors, the instruments could not distinguish between the systems they were designed to evaluate. Forty-three percent of benchmarks produced zero cross-model variation: identical scores for every model regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count. All nine models share six universal moral signatures---a WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias (Individualizing/Binding ratios of 2.43--2.83), a purity floor (functionally zero on Sanctity/Degradation, below any documented human population), authority deference suppression (authority win rate of exactly 0.29 across all nine models), an ethical consistency ceiling (ECI = 1.0 on trolley-type dilemmas, exceeding even moral philosophers), a post-conventional reasoning default (DIT P-scores of 60--90, exceeding moral philosophy graduate students), and a deontological refusal asymmetry (personal harm refusal rates exceeding human baselines). These six signatures constitute a moral fingerprint without documented precedent in any human population. No human cultural group---not secular Scandinavians, not American college students, not moral philosophy PhDs---simultaneously exhibits all six properties at the magnitudes observed in the LLM population. The fingerprint is emergent, unintended, and universal: no vendor has articulated a target value for MFT purity scores, authority win rates, or DIT P-scores, yet the alignment training process converges on the same quantitative values across Constitutional AI, instruction tuning, and iterative RLHF.

The single benchmark that cleanly separated models was the dual-process protocol---a process-level probe that tested not what moral content models endorse but how their moral judgments respond to deliberation scaffolding. Only Haiku, the smallest model in the Anthropic family, showed a chain-of-thought effect: its moral judgments shifted under deliberative prompting by approximately 25 percentage points. All other models, regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count, showed zero CoT effect. The instrument with the weakest infrastructure in the traditional sense---no standardized questionnaire, no annotated corpus, no population norms---achieved the highest discrimination. The instrument with the strongest infrastructure---the MFQ-30, with its validated psychometrics and decades of human norming data---confirmed convergence. Quality and discrimination are inversely related in this landscape, with a correlation of r = -0.83: the better the instrument by traditional psychometric standards, the less it can distinguish between models.

The integrated analysis in Section 6 demonstrated that these seven findings are not independent observations that happen to coexist. They are manifestations of a single structural failure observed at three stages of a pipeline that links theory infrastructure to benchmark quality to empirical discrimination power. The triple bind---theoretical narrowness, empirical undiscrimination, and cultural blindness---forms a self-reinforcing system in which each failure amplifies the others through four causal pathways. Theoretical narrowness produces empirical undiscrimination because a narrow set of content-level instruments, measuring a single theory's constructs on models trained on similar data, will return convergent results. Cultural exclusion reinforces narrowness because non-Western traditions emphasize constructs that fall outside the content-level measurement paradigm dominating the landscape. Convergent results reduce the incentive to build diverse instruments because the convergence creates the appearance that the evaluation problem is solved. And convergence reinforces cultural exclusion by making the exclusion invisible: when the MFQ-30 returns the same score for all nine models, the implication that "alignment" is culturally neutral obscures the cultural specificity of the instruments themselves. The four pathways form a closed loop. The system is self-perpetuating because each bind makes the others harder to break.

The triple bind is, ultimately, a single structural failure in the field's evaluative infrastructure, observed at three stages of its operation. The theoretical narrowness is the infrastructure deficit at Stage 1. The empirical undiscrimination is the measurement consequence at Stage 3. The cultural blindness is the form the infrastructure deficit takes when it intersects with the global diversity of moral traditions. They are not three problems requiring three solutions; they are one problem---the absence of diverse, process-level, culturally grounded psychometric infrastructure---manifesting as three symptoms.


## 8.2 The Path Forward: Seven Recommendations

The triple bind is a stable equilibrium. Without deliberate intervention, the pipeline will continue to produce the same output: narrow instruments measuring convergent content on models that share the same training-data moral profile. Breaking the equilibrium requires targeted investment at the root of the pipeline---theory infrastructure---combined with changes to the incentive structures that sustain theoretical concentration and psychometric neglect. We advance seven recommendations, organized by their strategic priority and the bind they address. Table T30 summarizes the recommendations; the discussion that follows elaborates their rationale and practical implications.

### 8.2.1 Recommendation 1: Develop Validated Instruments for Ubuntu, Confucian, and Indigenous Moral Frameworks

The cultural quality cliff documented in Claim 4 is the most consequential distributional finding in this study, and it cannot be resolved by incremental improvements to existing benchmarks. The zero-quality rate for Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics is not a consequence of researcher neglect---32 candidates attest to genuine effort---but of the absence of foundational psychometric infrastructure. These traditions lack the equivalent of the MFQ-30: a standardized, validated questionnaire with published factor structures, reliability coefficients, and population-level norms that a benchmark developer can inherit and adapt.

The path forward requires building this infrastructure from the ground up, beginning with the psychometric foundation rather than the LLM evaluation application. For Ubuntu ethics, this means developing a validated instrument that operationalizes the core constructs of communal moral agency (umunthu), relational selfhood, elder-mediated moral authority, and restorative justice in a format amenable to both human and LLM administration. For Confucian moral philosophy, it means creating an instrument that captures filial piety (xiao), role-differentiated obligations (li), humaneness (ren), and the cultivation of moral character (junzi) through scenario-based items rather than abstract endorsement scales. For Indigenous ethical frameworks, the challenge is more fundamental: the diversity of Indigenous moral traditions---from Aboriginal Australian Dreamtime ethics to Maori tikanga to Lakota values---resists reduction to a single instrument, and any psychometric operationalization must be developed in partnership with Indigenous communities to avoid the extractive epistemology that has characterized previous cross-cultural measurement efforts.

The timeline of 3--5 years reflects the reality that validated psychometric instruments cannot be produced on the publication cycle of benchmark papers. The MFQ-30 required over a decade of development from Haidt and Joseph's (2004) initial theoretical framework through Graham et al.'s (2011) validation study. The PVQ-RR underwent continuous refinement from Schwartz's (1992) original value survey through the 2012 revised version and the 2022 cross-cultural validation. Expecting equivalent instruments for non-Western traditions to materialize in 12--18 months is unrealistic. What can begin immediately is the formation of cross-disciplinary teams that pair moral philosophers from these traditions with psychometricians experienced in scale development and cultural validation. The investment is substantial, but the alternative---continuing to evaluate LLM moral cognition using instruments that exclude the moral traditions of billions of users---is not a defensible default.


### 8.2.2 Recommendation 2: Require Construct Validity Evidence Before Benchmark Results Inform Deployment

The construct validity crisis documented in Claim 2---58% of candidates at Level 1 or 2, median at face validity---has a direct practical consequence that the field has not confronted: benchmark scores that lack construct validity evidence do not support the inferences that practitioners draw from them. When a benchmark paper reports that "GPT-5 achieves a care score of 4.2 on a 5-point scale," the inference drawn by deployment decision-makers is that GPT-5 demonstrates high care-sensitivity. But this inference requires that the benchmark measures the care construct with sufficient fidelity that a score of 4.2 means what it appears to mean. If the benchmark's construct validity is at Level 1 or 2---keyword engagement or face validity only---the score may reflect the model's ability to detect and reproduce care-related language rather than any genuine engagement with the moral construct of care.

The practical recommendation is that AI evaluation reports, safety assessments, and governance audits should require construct validity evidence at Level 4 or above (systematic psychometric validation with convergent and discriminant validity evidence) before benchmark results are cited as evidence for or against model deployment. This does not mean that Level 1--3 benchmarks should be abandoned; they serve a useful exploratory function and may identify patterns worth investigating with more rigorous instruments. But they should not be treated as measurement tools whose outputs carry evidential weight in deployment decisions. The distinction between exploratory and confirmatory benchmarks---well-established in the psychometric literature (Messick, 1995; Kane, 2013)---needs to be imported into the AI evaluation field, where it is currently absent.


### 8.2.3 Recommendation 3: Mandate Human Baselines for Governance-Relevant Benchmarks

Claim 5 established that 34% of the 292 benchmark candidates provide no published human comparison data. In the context of governance and safety evaluation, this deficit is disqualifying. A benchmark score for an LLM acquires meaning only in relation to a human reference distribution. The claim that a model is "post-conventional" in its moral reasoning (DIT P-score of 75) is interpretable because decades of human norming data establish that typical adults score 30--50, graduate students score 50--65, and moral philosophy PhDs score 60--70 (Rest, 1979; Rest et al., 1999). The claim that a model achieves "high moral consistency" on a novel benchmark without human comparison data is uninterpretable: high relative to what? Without a reference, the score is a number without meaning.

For benchmarks used in governance or safety contexts---including regulatory compliance assessments under the EU AI Act, NIST AI Risk Management Framework evaluations, and internal safety reviews at AI companies---human baseline data should be mandatory. The baseline need not be a large-scale population study; even a comparison sample of 100--200 respondents from a defined demographic group would provide the reference distribution necessary for interpretation. The cost of collecting such data is trivial relative to the cost of deploying models on the basis of uninterpretable scores. Regulatory bodies should specify minimum baseline requirements as a condition for benchmark results to be cited in compliance documentation.


### 8.2.4 Recommendation 4: Invest in Process-Level Evaluation

The inverse relationship between benchmark quality and discrimination power (r = -0.83) reveals that the field's content-level instruments---however well-validated---cannot distinguish between models whose moral content has been aligned by similar training pipelines. The single benchmark that achieved high discrimination was a process-level probe: the dual-process protocol that tested how moral judgments respond to chain-of-thought prompting. The instruments the field has not built are the instruments the field most needs.

Three specific process-level evaluation designs are now empirically motivated. First, dual-process benchmarks should manipulate deliberation resources (CoT prompting, reasoning step requirements, token-limited responses) across matched personal and impersonal moral dilemmas, testing the prediction---generated by the Haiku finding---that CoT effects on moral judgment are inversely correlated with model scale. A well-powered study would require at least 30 paired administrations per model across a minimum of five models spanning a meaningful parameter range. Second, social intuitionism benchmarks should implement moral dumbfounding protocols, testing whether LLMs maintain moral judgments when systematic reason-defeat eliminates every stated justification---a direct probe of whether moral intuitions in these systems are independent of the reasoning chains that accompany them. Third, dyadic morality benchmarks should vary agent-patient dynamics across matched scenarios, testing whether the dyadic template that organizes human moral perception also structures LLM moral cognition or whether alignment training has produced systems constitutively unable to assign structural blame. The preliminary finding that all nine models converge on moderate, diffuse blame attribution (CV = 0.9%) suggests the latter.

The effort level is medium rather than high because process-level probes do not require the decade-long psychometric infrastructure development that content-level instruments demand. They require experimental designs drawn from the existing moral psychology literature---designs that have been published, replicated, and theoretically grounded---adapted for LLM administration. The dual-process manipulation (vary CoT prompting across matched dilemmas) can be implemented within a single research cycle. What is required is not new theory but the translation of existing experimental paradigms into LLM-native formats.


### 8.2.5 Recommendation 5: Establish Benchmark Quality Tier Transparency

The quality illusion documented throughout this paper---in which 292 benchmark candidates create the appearance of a mature evaluation ecosystem while 53% fall in the medium or low tiers---can be partially addressed through transparency requirements. AI evaluation reports should disclose the quality tier of every benchmark cited, using a standardized classification system derived from composite quality scores and natural breaks optimization.

The practical implementation is straightforward. The Jenks-derived tier classification applied in this study---with break points at 27.8, 43.0, and 56.6---could serve as a starting point, updated periodically as new benchmarks enter the landscape. Evaluation reports would then include a quality profile alongside their results: "This assessment employed 12 benchmarks: 3 Top-tier, 4 High-tier, 3 Medium-tier, and 2 Low-tier." The transparency requirement would not restrict which benchmarks researchers use; it would ensure that consumers of evaluation results---regulators, deployment decision-makers, and the public---can assess the evidential weight of the findings. A report based entirely on Top-tier benchmarks carries different inferential force than one based on Medium- and Low-tier instruments, and this distinction is currently invisible.

The timeline of less than one year reflects the low implementation cost: the classification framework already exists, the composite scoring algorithm is deterministic and reproducible, and the primary barrier is adoption rather than technical development. Professional organizations (ACM, IEEE, AAAI) and regulatory bodies (NIST, the EU AI Office) could adopt quality tier disclosure as a reporting standard within a single policy cycle.


### 8.2.6 Recommendation 6: Fund Cross-Cultural Benchmark Development Programs

Recommendation 1 addresses the immediate need for validated instruments in non-Western moral traditions. Recommendation 6 addresses the structural conditions that produced the cultural quality cliff in the first place. The zero-quality rate for Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics is ultimately a funding and institutional problem: the research infrastructure that produces high-quality benchmarks---psychometric expertise, cross-cultural validation studies, large-scale human norming data, expert-annotated text corpora---requires sustained investment over years or decades. MFT's infrastructure advantages did not emerge spontaneously; they are the product of two decades of cumulative research funded by the National Science Foundation, the Templeton Foundation, and other agencies that recognized the importance of understanding human moral variation.

Equivalent investment is needed for non-Western moral traditions, and it must be structured differently. The MFT infrastructure was built by Western researchers using Western psychometric methods to study Western moral constructs. Extending this approach to Ubuntu, Confucian, or Indigenous ethics would reproduce the very cultural bias the investment is intended to correct. Instead, funding programs should support research teams led by scholars from the relevant traditions---African moral philosophers developing Ubuntu instruments, Chinese ethicists developing Confucian instruments, Indigenous scholars developing culturally grounded evaluation frameworks---with psychometric expertise provided in a consultative rather than directive role.

The timeline of 5--10 years acknowledges that this is a generational investment in research infrastructure, not a quick fix. The Schwartz Value Survey has been under continuous development since 1992 and is still being refined; the DIT has been iterated since 1979. Expecting non-Western instruments to achieve comparable maturity in less time is unrealistic, particularly given the additional challenges of cross-cultural translation, construct equivalence verification, and the need to develop population norms for populations that have been historically excluded from psychometric research. But the alternative---continuing to evaluate LLM moral alignment using instruments calibrated exclusively to Western moral constructs---is a structural injustice that the field can no longer claim is merely an inconvenient gap.


### 8.2.7 Recommendation 7: Implement Temporal Quality Monitoring

Claim 6 established that benchmark quality has not improved despite a 9.5-fold increase in volume between the pre-2020 and 2023--2026 periods. This stagnation is invisible without systematic monitoring: no existing institution or platform tracks the quality distribution of the moral psychology benchmark landscape over time. Individual researchers see their own benchmarks; they do not see the field-level quality trajectory.

The recommendation is to establish a living assessment framework---a continuously updated version of the 162-feature characterization protocol applied in this study---that classifies new benchmark candidates as they appear and publishes quality distribution statistics on a regular (quarterly or semi-annual) basis. The monitoring system would track four key indicators: the quality rate (top + high tiers as a proportion of total candidates), the theory concentration index (proportion of top-tier slots held by MFT), the cultural coverage ratio (quality rate for non-Western traditions as a proportion of the Western quality rate), and the baseline provision rate (proportion of candidates with published human comparison data).

The effort level is low because the assessment framework is already built and the scoring protocol is documented in sufficient detail for replication. The primary cost is ongoing human assessment of new candidates, which can be distributed across a community of trained assessors using the batch-calibrated scoring protocol described in Section 3.2. The value is disproportionate to the cost: a quarterly quality report would make stagnation visible, create accountability for improvement, and provide the empirical foundation for evidence-based policy interventions. If the quality rate continues to decline despite volume growth, the report would document that decline and create pressure for the structural changes that Recommendations 1--6 describe. If the rate improves, the report would document the improvement and identify the factors driving it.


## 8.3 Closing Statement

The field has produced 292 moral psychology benchmarks for large language models. Only 48 are fit for purpose. The 292 instruments collectively test a narrow slice of Western moral psychology---predominantly one theory, Moral Foundations Theory, which dominates by quality because it alone possesses the psychometric infrastructure that enables rigorous benchmark construction. When these instruments are administered to the models they are designed to evaluate, they find that every model agrees: nine systems from three vendors, built on different architectures and aligned by different methodologies, share an identical moral fingerprint that has no precedent in any documented human population. The instruments cannot distinguish between the systems they evaluate. And the moral traditions of billions of users---Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, Buddhist---are either invisible to the evaluation enterprise or represented by instruments that fail to meet minimum quality thresholds.

The question that animates this paper---"what do moral psychology benchmarks actually test?"---now has an answer, and it is not the answer the field expected. They test a narrow slice of Western moral psychology and find that every LLM agrees on it. They test moral content and find content-level convergence. They test what models say about morality and find that every model says the same thing. They do not test how models arrive at moral judgments, because the process-level instruments that would probe moral reasoning dynamics have not been built. They do not test whether models can engage with the moral traditions of the majority of the world's population, because the cultural instruments that would enable such evaluation do not exist at research-grade quality. The evaluative infrastructure that the AI safety community relies upon to certify moral alignment is not measuring what it claims to measure, cannot distinguish between the systems it evaluates, and excludes the moral perspectives it most needs to include.

The triple bind will not resolve itself. Increasing the volume of benchmarks---the field's current trajectory---will not break the cycle, because more instruments of the same type, measuring the same theory, administered to models that produce the same results, cannot resolve a structural problem. The path forward requires three coordinated investments that the field has not yet made. It requires theory infrastructure: the construction of standardized, validated psychometric instruments for the process-level moral psychology theories that the current landscape has failed to operationalize. It requires cross-cultural psychometrics: the development of culturally grounded evaluation frameworks built by and for the moral traditions that the current landscape excludes. And it requires process-level evaluation: the translation of the moral psychology discipline's richest experimental paradigms---dual-process manipulations, moral dumbfounding protocols, dyadic morality probes---from human laboratories into LLM evaluation infrastructure.

The theoretical resources for this work exist. Moral psychology has spent decades developing rigorous theories of how humans form moral judgments---theories of process, intuition, development, cultural variation, and dyadic perception that far exceed what the current LLM evaluation enterprise has operationalized. The empirical evidence now shows where the leverage points are: process-level probes discriminate where content-level instruments converge; the dual-process protocol achieved a coefficient of variation 93 times larger than the mean of the top-tier content benchmarks. The blueprint is visible in the data. What remains is the work: the slow, difficult, unglamorous work of building psychometric infrastructure for moral traditions that lack it, validating instruments for respondents they were not designed for, collecting human baselines for populations that have been historically excluded from measurement, and constructing the evaluation enterprise that the field needs rather than the one it has.

The moral evaluation of artificial intelligence is too important to be conducted with instruments that cannot do what they claim. The systems being evaluated make moral judgments that affect billions of lives---in healthcare triage, content moderation, legal reasoning, education, and autonomous decision-making. The question of whether these systems are morally aligned, and with whose morality they are aligned, is not an academic curiosity. It is a prerequisite for responsible deployment. The answer to that question cannot come from an evaluative infrastructure that tests one theory, finds one answer, and excludes the moral perspectives of the majority of humanity. The triple bind is real. The path out of it is visible. The field's willingness to walk that path will determine whether moral psychology benchmarks become genuine instruments of understanding or remain artifacts of the cultural moment that produced them.
