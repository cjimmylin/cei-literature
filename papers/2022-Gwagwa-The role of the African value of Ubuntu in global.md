## Abstract

Historically, Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has been excluded from the beneﬁts of the previous industrial revolutions, as its people and their resources and aspirations have been objectiﬁed through foreign domination, and its culture has either been fragmented or appropriated. While artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) is poised to generate vast amounts of wealth, its application could lead to further social and economic exclusion of SSA due to a lack of access to technological advancements and the historical injustice and exclusion based on protected characteristics. Through an examination of the concept of inclusion, this paper explores how to improve the terms on which African populations and subpopulations and their concerns are included in the global AI ethics discourses. Speciﬁcally, it is argued that the SSA value of Ubuntu could be of immense value in AI applied normative ethics, particularly toward an inclusive approach for the implementation of the universal AI ethics principles and guidelines.

## Detailed Summary

This paper explores how the African philosophical concept of Ubuntu can contribute to global AI ethics discourse, particularly addressing the historical exclusion of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) from technological benefits. The authors argue that Ubuntu, with its emphasis on relational personhood and communal ethics, offers a valuable counterpoint to predominantly Western, individualistic approaches to AI governance. The paper examines how Ubuntu's core tenet - "I am because we are" - can inform more inclusive AI development practices.

The research situates itself within the broader context of AI's potential to either perpetuate or help address global inequalities. Drawing on normative ethics and postcolonial theory, the authors demonstrate how Ubuntu-based principles such as communality, solidarity, and interdependence can enhance existing AI ethics frameworks. They argue that Ubuntu provides a lens for understanding AI not merely as a tool for individual benefit but as a technology that should strengthen social bonds and community well-being.

The paper's significance lies in its challenge to the universality of Western ethical frameworks in AI governance. By articulating how African values can contribute to global AI ethics, the authors advocate for a pluralistic approach that recognizes diverse cultural and philosophical traditions as legitimate sources of ethical guidance for technology development.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs a normative ethics approach combined with conceptual analysis. The methodology involves: (1) a literature review of Ubuntu philosophy and its ethical foundations; (2) analysis of existing AI ethics frameworks and their limitations regarding inclusivity; (3) examination of postcolonial perspectives on technology and development; and (4) synthesis of how Ubuntu principles can address gaps in current AI governance discourse. The paper does not involve empirical data collection but rather provides philosophical argumentation supported by scholarly sources.

## Results Summary

The analysis reveals several key insights: (1) Current AI ethics frameworks predominantly reflect Western, individualistic values that may not adequately address the needs of SSA communities; (2) Ubuntu offers distinct ethical principles including communality, solidarity, and relational personhood that complement existing approaches; (3) The concept of "personhood through community" provides an alternative foundation for thinking about AI's social impacts; (4) Ubuntu-based AI ethics would prioritize collective well-being and social cohesion over individual autonomy alone; (5) Integrating Ubuntu into global AI governance could improve the inclusivity and legitimacy of international AI policy frameworks.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that Ubuntu represents a valuable philosophical resource for global AI ethics that has been largely overlooked. They argue that incorporating Ubuntu principles can help address the persistent exclusion of SSA from AI development and governance. Key limitations include the challenge of operationalizing Ubuntu concepts in concrete policy frameworks and the risk of essentializing African philosophy. Future work should focus on developing practical mechanisms for integrating Ubuntu into AI governance institutions and exploring how other non-Western ethical traditions might similarly contribute to a more pluralistic global AI ethics.
