# Putting the AI in Eudaimonia

## Authors

[[Jesse Velay-Vitow|Velay-Vitow, Jesse]], [[John Grant|Grant, John]]

## Links


- **Article:** https://therepublicjournal.com/putting-the-ai-in-eudaimonia/

## Abstract

This essay explores the intersection of artificial intelligence and the concept of eudaimonia, or human flourishing. The authors contend that technologists should focus on making military work as ethical as possible, adopting Aristotle's view that the fundamental objective of human existence is to live a good life. They suggest that technology, including cutting-edge AI, serves eudaimonia in two ways: by enabling efficiencies that grant individuals more time to pursue their "good life," and by fostering a secure and stable society in which that life can be lived. The essay argues that AI can profoundly redefine what it means to live a flourishing life and proposes that AI development across commercial, civil, and military contexts should be unified by the mission to protect the space and time necessary for human eudaimonia.

## Detailed Summary

This essay from The Republic (The Palantir Foundation) explores the intersection of artificial intelligence and Aristotle's concept of eudaimonia (human flourishing). The authors, both Palantir employees, argue that technologists should embrace a unified moral framework centered on protecting the conditions necessary for humans to live good lives. They contend that AI development across commercial, civil, and military contexts should be guided by this teleological goal rather than being viewed through separate ethical lenses.

The paper makes two key claims about how technology serves eudaimonia: first, by enabling efficiencies that grant individuals more time to pursue their conception of the "good life"; and second, by fostering a secure and stable society in which that life can be lived. The authors invoke Just War theory to argue that defensive military applications of AI can be ethical when they protect the conditions enabling human flourishing. This represents an attempt to bridge the gap between mission-driven tech culture and traditional virtue ethics.

The essay targets computer science and engineering graduates, encouraging them to view their work through an Aristotelian lens rather than dismissing military or dual-use applications as inherently unethical.

## Methods Summary

The paper is a philosophical essay drawing on Aristotelian virtue ethics and Just War theory. It uses conceptual analysis and historical examples (the wheel, modern appliances, computing systems) to argue that technology has progressively freed humans from basic survival tasks, enabling pursuit of higher-order goals. The methodology is normative rather than empirical.

## Results Summary

This is a position paper without empirical results. The key argument is that AI development should be unified by the teleological goal of protecting human eudaimonia, whether in commercial, civil, or military contexts. The authors argue this framing can help technologists view their work as ethical even when it involves defense applications.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that technologists should adopt Aristotle's view that the fundamental objective of human existence is to live a good life, and that AI can profoundly support this goal. They propose embedding eudaimonia as a foundational parameter in AI development to address alignment problems. Limitations include the Western-centric focus on Aristotelian ethics and the potential for this framing to rationalize ethically questionable applications.
