Through Eudaimonia’s Lenses: EudAImonic Well-Being Variables As Guiding
Principles For AI Design
JULIAN MARVIN JÖRS and ERNESTO WILLIAM DE LUCA, Otto-von-Guericke University, Germany
Psychological-philosophical perspectives on well-being have recently reached the research field of human-centered artificial intelligence
(HCAI). One of the challenges of HCAI is the analysis of the effects of AI on well-being or more specifically on eudaimonic well-being
(EWB). We discuss the extent to which eudaimonic orientations can be mapped in AI interaction and how human-AI interaction
generally affects EWB variables (autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, purpose in life, positive relationship with others,
self-acceptance). In this position paper, we propose these variables as useful reflection dimensions for AI technology developments
and at the same time discuss the challenges that the eudaimonic AI development community will have to face in the future.
CCS Concepts: • Human-centered computing →HCI design and evaluation methods; Interaction paradigms; HCI theory, concepts
and models; Interaction design; Human computer interaction (HCI).
Additional Key Words and Phrases: Eudaimonia User Experience (UX), Eudaimonic Technology, Self-Determination Theory, Measure-
ment Methods, Well-Being in HCI
1
INTRODUCTION
Well-being has emerged as one of the key challenges of human-computer interaction (HCI) and human-centered
artificial intelligence (HCAI) [20, 63]. The discussion of possible benefits and negative consequences of AI on well-being
has led to initiatives such as HCAI, human-centered design (HCD) [11], or Human-AI Interaction (HAII) [68]. As
a broad multi-dimensional construct well-being seems to extend beyond simply felt happiness or satisfaction [65].
The conceptualization of well-being raises the fundamental question that already occupied Hellenic philosophers and
emerged in deontological concepts such as eudaimonia from the work of Aristotle or the hedonic way of thinking
of Aristippus of Cyrene: What is well-being and how does the individual achieve it? Previous HCI research has
already shared some work-in-progress, theoretical work, and empirical studies embedding the idea of well-being and
psychological needs in HCI [3, 9, 14, 17, 30, 36, 40, 45–47, 50, 52, 60, 62, 64, 67].
In this position paper, we argue for a critical reflection on designing HCAI through the lens of EWB, with a particular
focus on the six dimensions of EWB [56]. HCI has already shifted towards a more contemporary approach, which
also calls for the development of purposeful technology that creates a long-term impact on well-being and supports
higher-level human needs. We share the current view that both industry and individuals can benefit from a focus
on dimensions of EWB [50]. We hope to contribute to HCAI and HCD by deriving research questions and directions
regarding EWB and AI.

EUDAIMONIC WELL-BEING VARIABLES AS GUIDING PRINCIPLES
Beyond the "hedonic reflection of our times" [53], Aristotle already described in Nicomachean Ethics the gain of well-
being not only from feeling good or satisfaction of appetites but living by the daimon or "true self". This gave rise to
the idea of eudaimonia, which could be explored in specific behavior, experiences, motives for action, values, personal
priorities, and satisfaction variables [24, 25, 27]. An experiment shows that a eudaimonic person strives for excellence
or personal ideals, wants to develop new skills, determines personal motives, and focuses on autotelic activities, by
contrast, hedonists strive for pleasure, enjoyment, relaxation, comfort [28]. According to the findings of Ryff [56], EWB
is strongly linked to the dimensions of psychological well-being (PWB): autonomy, environmental mastery, personal

2.1
Autonomy
The question of a conceptual definition of autonomy was already posed by the HCI community in the 90s [19] and is
also being debated in more recent workshops [10]. However, autonomy is still a vague construct according to a recent
literature review that specifically examined the concept of autonomy and agency in the context of HCI [5]. In this
context, the following questions may arise: Do we feel autonomous in using AI technology directly? Does the use of AI
positively impact our autonomy in general? Which tasks might be affected by AI technology and in which way? In the
following, we discuss feelings of autonomy for the level of life, tasks, and behavior, as well as in direct contact with AI
(i.e. interface):
(1) Consideration of autonomy in the social and life sphere: From our perspective, AI, on the one hand, can help
prevent fraud (phishing) or attacks on society (identity theft) and, on the other hand, counter diminished
autonomy caused by disability and diseases such as dementia. Examples are the detection and prevention of
fraudulent activities on credit transactions to protect public institutions, industry, and people [2]. On a personal
level, we see physical health-promoting applications with AI contributing to a general feeling of autonomy in
life [1, 48, 49]. AI can be also used to help elderly people act more safely and autonomously in the digital world
[38].
(2) Autonomy in the task and behavior sphere: The use of AI is particularly sensitive at the level of tasks and the
sum of tasks, which is summarized under the term behavior. The specific behavior of a eudaimonic-oriented
person is the need for struggle and challenge [51, 66], reflectiveness and reasoning [54], or learning new things
[51]. It remains one of the key challenges to stabilizing eudaimonic behavior quality under the consequences
of cognitive offloading of processes to AI, the reduced need for memorization, and thus changing learning
behavior. We see two research directions: First, it is necessary to analyze which components of tasks contribute
mainly to the eudaimonic quality of one’s behavior. Secondly, how these components can be preserved and
even promoted by AI instead of damaging them, because they seem to be essential for (eudaimonic) well-being.
(3) Aspects of autonomy in the interface sphere: Agency, as we defined it as a form of autonomy in HCI, includes
according to previous findings, how one can perform with technology in direct interaction, e.g. different input
methods such as on-skin input, touchpads, or keyboard [6, 7, 12, 34, 35]. The decisive factor for us is which
control mechanisms, functional scopes and causality mechanisms must be guaranteed to achieve feelings of
autonomy in direct contact with AI. Additionally, research on HCAI should also highlight the interaction with
assistive technology, which intends to proactively support people’s everyday lives, such as elderly people
[15, 23]. It seems that regarding the interaction with assistive technology, different reactions to feelings of
autonomy emerge (some feel under pressure, others see their self-responsibility strengthened). Adoption in
direct contact with AI therefore requires a sensitive analysis in our opinion.

effective use of surrounding opportunities, and selecting the appropriate means for one’s personal needs and values
[55]. Regarding environmental mastery at the interface level, we see strong similarities with questions of autonomy at

the same research efforts here: Focusing on the analysis of autonomy and environmental mastery feelings in direct

“A well-functioning human being uses reason well, to modulate wants and desires” [18]. AI will reduce our capability
of making judgments as we externalize judgment capabilities as long as we don’t offer sufficient explanations of
decision-making of AI that learning processes are possible (I understand what the AI has done and why). Nevertheless,
we try to be a bit provocative here: in some cases, AI decision-making might be more efficient and could lead to even
more environmental mastery meaning that sitting in an autonomous car in a fully automated traffic network could
lead to a higher feeling of environmental mastery than in situations of being in a human-driven car which carries
the risk of being involved in an accident. This example could be an argument that precarity and deficiency of human
judgment (faulty, unfairness, distinct moral concepts, misbehavior) could be prevented and efficiently done by statistical
predictive control mechanisms of environmental structures - shortly by AI. The question becomes even more complex,
as experiments confirm that people do indeed show preferences towards algorithmic judgment, but make less accurate
decisions in situations where they do not [33]. As Eisikovits and Feldmann [18] put it correctly from our perspective: We
might lose step by step the ability to make judgments not only in single situations but more and more, in general, relying
on external structures and thus losing an overall capacity for judgment. We therefore call for a serious assessment of
the extent to which judgment and the associated perceptions of environmental mastery are influenced by AI.
2.3
Personal Growth
Personal growth is achieved by the individual through continuous self-improvement, the feeling of realizing one’s
potential, and above all, increasing self-knowledge and effectiveness [55]. From our perspective, AI should support
people in their personal growth despite advanced automation [41], the doctrine of simplicity [61], and the shift of
practical judgment tasks to AI [18]. AI-empowered systems should establish an interactive relationship with the user
contributing to a form of activity instead of passivity of the user [22]. Several studies have provided evidence that more
self-determined types of motivation (e.g. intrinsic motivation) lead to the most positive behavioral (greater persistence),
cognitive (enhanced concentration), and emotional (greater well-being) outcomes [8]. We are convinced that it may well
be a question of design to continue to promote this form of intrinsic motivation and to enable flow experiences [13].
Following Peters’ [45] heuristics for developing technology regarding well-being, we recommend practical heuristics for
the design of HCAI and HCD such as providing proximal and multi-level feedback, informing a pathway to improvement,
providing adaptation and leveling, i.e. ensuring that a level of complexity is appropriate for the user. The influence of
AI in learning processes is particularly important [43]. The early use of AI technology in schools can have different
effects if, for example, it is used as a substitution tool for learning processes (AI, write my homework) or simply as an
assistance tool for correcting expression errors in a foreign language (AI, show me my expression errors). In our opinion,
it may make sense to emphasize personal growth-oriented AI design (learning-friendly AIs), which promotes forms
of intrinsic motivation, moments of concentration, and, above all, cognitive load, because it contributes strongly to
eudaimonic well-being.

emotional integration in later life [42]. The eudaimonic feeling of a purpose in life can reduce pathological symptoms in
old age and promote engagement in preventive health behaviors [58]. Applications in HCI, for example, mainly concern

as altruism, contemplation, and academic learning [69]? Can meaning be somehow experienced, so can we design
interaction with AI in a way that is valuable and important? How can AI technology help humans achieve a realistic
conceptual framework for sense-making in life [37]? Can AI be used somehow to evoke nostalgic feelings and thus
eudaimonic emotions [32]?
2.5
Positive Relationship With Others
Aspects of positive relationships with others include qualities such as warm, satisfying, trusting relationships, empathy
development, feelings of intimacy, and understanding give and take of human relationships [55]. More aspects of
positive relationships are [31]: social integration, social coherence, social actualization, social acceptance, and social
contribution. In our opinion, all aspects of social life must be respected concerning the use of AI in social networks
and similar virtual spaces, because these are social communication spaces that should above all emphasize positive
communication between people. We assign a more supportive and mediative role to AI [59] to ensure healthy exchange
between people, as we don’t support the direct replacement of human-to-human communication by human-to-AI in the
long term. However, AI platforms such as Replika1 are useful in some ways as a first aid against loneliness and anxiety.
In the long term, it should help people return to real social life. Nevertheless, as social beings, we need direct interaction
to learn empathic skills and trust. Furthermore, AI can ensure safe interaction in virtual spaces, e.g. to protect women
from inappropriate behavior in dating apps2.
2.6
Self-Acceptance
Self-acceptance is understood as the internalization of a positive attitude towards oneself, i.e. the perception and
acceptance of various aspects of one’s personality, as well as a positive perception of the present and past self [55].
Associated with self-acceptance are well-being outcomes such as self-actualization, maturity, optimal functioning, and
mental health benefits [57]. For self-acceptance, space and time must be created for self-reflection [24]. We want to
emphasize the usefulness of AI in this regard, because it may help identify mental health issues by behavioral pattern
recognition and thus contribute to self-reflection, promote mindfulness [16], and spiritual activities [67, 69]. More
recent app developments such as Meditopia3, for example, are trying to combine AI technology and meditation by
offering an AI-assisted chatbot Soul based on ChatGPT4.

3
CONCLUSION
The dimensions of EWB can be used as guiding principles in all forms of HCAI development. In our view, two important
challenges can be declared: first, ensuring personal growth while externalizing cognitive resources and practical
judgment capabilities, and second, sharing tasks with AI, insofar as psychological variables such as personal growth
and purpose in life do not fall below an acceptance level.
