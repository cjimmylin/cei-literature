## Abstract

Abstract             A considerable body of research cross-culturally examines the evolution of religious traditions, beliefs and behaviors. The bulk of this research, however, draws from coded qualitative ethnographies rather than from standardized methods specifically designed to measure religious beliefs and behaviors. Psychological data sets that examine religious thought and behavior in controlled conditions tend to be disproportionately sampled from student populations. Some cross-national databases employ standardized methods at the individual level, but are primarily focused on fully market integrated, state-level societies. The Evolution of Religion and Morality Project sought to generate a data set that systematically probed individual level measures sampling across a wider range of human populations. The set includes data from behavioral economic experiments and detailed surveys of demographics, religious beliefs and practices, material security, and intergroup perceptions. This paper describes the methods and variables, briefly introduces the sites and sampling techniques, notes inconsistencies across sites, and provides some basic reporting for the data set.

## Detailed Summary

This paper describes the cross-cultural dataset for the Evolution of Religion and Morality Project, which sought to systematically probe individual-level measures of religious beliefs and moral behavior across a wider range of human populations than typically studied. The dataset addresses a significant gap in existing research: most cross-cultural studies of religion draw from coded qualitative ethnographies rather than standardized methods, psychological datasets are disproportionately sampled from WEIRD student populations, and cross-national databases focus primarily on market-integrated state-level societies.

The project collected data from multiple field sites representing diverse societies including: Hadza foragers (Tanzania), Yasawa Fijians, Tanna Island (Vanuatu), inland and coastal Tyva (Siberia), Marajó Island (Brazil), and Mauritius. The dataset includes behavioral economic experiments (specifically variants of the dictator game designed to test prosocial behavior toward in-group vs. out-group members) alongside detailed surveys covering demographics, religious beliefs and practices, material security, and intergroup perceptions.

This data descriptor paper introduces the methods, variables, sampling techniques, and basic reporting for the dataset. It notes inconsistencies across sites that researchers should be aware of and provides guidance for using the data. The project represents a significant contribution to the empirical study of religion and morality by providing standardized measures from non-WEIRD populations, enabling tests of theories about the co-evolution of religion and cooperation.

## Methods Summary

The study employed: (1) Behavioral economic experiments including modified dictator games where participants allocated real monetary resources between themselves and recipients (either co-religionists, geographically distant members, or known others), (2) Standardized surveys measuring demographics, religious beliefs (including questions about moralistic gods, afterlife beliefs, punishment for norm violations), religious practices (frequency, rituals), material security (food, economic resources), and intergroup attitudes, (3) Sampling from diverse societies including hunter-gatherers, horticulturalists, and mixed-subsistence communities, (4) Local adaptations of protocols to ensure cultural appropriateness while maintaining cross-site comparability.

## Results Summary

The paper describes the dataset rather than testing specific hypotheses. Key descriptive information: (1) Data collected from 8 diverse field sites across 5 continents, (2) Sample sizes vary by site from approximately 90 to 500 participants, (3) Dictator game allocations and survey responses enable analysis of prosocial behavior, religious beliefs, and their relationships, (4) Variables include belief in moralistic supernatural punishment, frequency of religious participation, allocations to various recipient types, and demographic covariates. The dataset supports testing theories about whether belief in moralistic gods promotes cooperation with strangers.

## Conclusions

The authors present this as a data descriptor rather than drawing theoretical conclusions. They emphasize: (1) The dataset enables tests of theories about religion and cooperation using standardized measures from diverse societies, (2) Users should attend to site-specific inconsistencies documented in the paper, (3) The behavioral economic experiments provide objective measures of prosocial behavior complementing survey responses, (4) Future analyses can examine relationships between religious beliefs, practices, and prosocial behavior across cultural contexts. The data are designed to be reusable for multiple research questions about the evolution of religion and morality.

## Data Description

This paper describes the cross-cultural dataset from the Evolution of Religion and Morality Project, containing behavioral economic experiment data and survey responses from 591 participants across eight field sites in seven countries. The dataset includes variables on demographics, religious beliefs and practices, supernatural agents' characteristics, intergroup relations, and coin allocation outcomes from a modified Random Allocation Game. Data are hosted on Harvard Dataverse in CSV format with accompanying R scripts and codebooks.
