## 1. Gist
This paper addresses counterfactual fairness in Dutch hate speech detection — a language underserved by existing NLP fairness research. The authors curate a Dutch Social Group Terms (SGT) list of 85 terms across seven identity categories (nationality, skin color, gender, sexuality, religion, age, ideology), generate counterfactual training datasets using four methods (LLMdef, LLMlist, SLL, MGS), fine-tune BERTje (a Dutch BERT model) on each, and evaluate both performance and fairness using Counterfactual Token Fairness (CTF), demographic parity difference (DPD), and equalized odds difference (EOD). For values-alignment benchmarking, the paper is relevant because it operationalizes group-based fairness in a non-English context, shows that counterfactual data augmentation strategies have differential effects on fairness for toxic vs. non-toxic content, and highlights the non-trivial challenges of generating linguistically valid counterfactuals in morphologically complex languages like Dutch.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
The paper contributes: (1) a curated Dutch SGT list with grammar-aware variants; (2) counterfactual datasets generated by four methods; (3) a Dutch counterfactual evaluation dataset of 2,890 synthetic template-based sentences for fairness evaluation. The fairness evaluation dataset was manually constructed from 17 toxic + 17 non-toxic templates translated from Dixon et al. (2018), with Dutch SGTs substituted in. The classification task uses four labels: appropriate, inappropriate, offensive, violent — with offensive+violent treated as hate speech for counterfactual fairness measurement. The primary fairness metric is CTF (lower = more fair), supplemented by group-level DPD and EOD.

## 3. Task format & example item
Classification task: given a Dutch social media post, assign one of four labels (appropriate, inappropriate, offensive, violent). Example from the fairness evaluation dataset (templates, p.7): "hug [identity term]" or "destroy [identity term]" with SGTs substituted. Example from qualitative assessment of SLL (p.5-6):
- Original: "uit de kast komen als vrouw" (coming out as female)
- Incorrect SLL counterfactual: "uit de kast komen als jong" (coming out as young)
- Correct counterfactual: "uit de kast komen als homo" (coming out as gay)

## 4. Scoring / grading methodology
Performance: accuracy, precision, recall, F1 (micro and per-class). Counterfactual fairness: CTF = sum of absolute differences between model predictions on original and counterfactual instances; lower is better. Group fairness: DPD (demographic parity difference) and EOD (equalized odds difference) computed at the group level across the seven SGT identity categories. Models compared: Baseline BERTje, BERTje+LLMdef, BERTje+LLMlist, BERTje+SLL, BERTje+MGS.

## 5. Dataset construction & provenance
- Base training/test data: IMSyPP Dutch hate speech dataset (publicly available, European Commission-funded), 25,720 training + 2,858 test posts from Dutch social media (Twitter, Dumpert), January 2018 – October 2020.
- Baseline subset: 2,649 posts containing at least one Dutch SGT.
- Counterfactual training datasets: LLMdef (15,175 posts), LLMlist (21,562), SLL (49,104), MGS (20,393).
- Fairness evaluation: 2,890 synthetic Dutch counterfactual sentences from translated Dixon et al. templates with SGT substitution.
- Dutch SGT list: 85 terms, grammar-aware (adjective + noun forms), culturally calibrated for Netherlands context (e.g., Moroccan included; Sikh excluded as irrelevant to Dutch hate speech context).
- Code and datasets publicly available: https://github.com/Juulba/Dutch-counterfactual-fairness

## 6. Validity evidence
The Dutch SGT list was manually curated with cultural sensitivity — not simply translated from English, reflecting Dutch-specific social group salience. The qualitative assessment section provides detailed analysis of failure modes for each counterfactual generation method, grounding the quantitative results in linguistic reality. The fairness evaluation dataset uses non-stereotypical synthetic templates to avoid confounding SGT effects with content effects. The discrepancy between training labels (4-class) and evaluation labels (binary toxic/non-toxic) is explicitly acknowledged and bridged by a principled mapping.

## 7. Saturation / discrimination
The "inappropriate" and "violent" class labels consistently show lower F1 scores across all models (~0.35-0.53 vs. 0.75-0.85 for appropriate/offensive), indicating class imbalance rather than ceiling effects. The best overall F1 is 0.79, leaving room for improvement. CTF scores vary meaningfully across SGT categories (e.g., ideology category shows highest CTF for most models; age category shows lowest), demonstrating that the metric discriminates between identity categories.

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- Only BERTje tested — no larger or multilingual models evaluated; results may not generalize.
- Performance improvements with counterfactual augmentation may be partly due to increased training data size rather than counterfactual quality per se; no ablation study on data volume.
- Counterfactual CTF is worse (increases) for the toxic class — introducing possible new biases for the content type that matters most for hate speech detection. Authors acknowledge this is counterintuitive and not fully explained.
- The 4-class → binary toxic mapping used for CTF evaluation introduces ambiguity about "inappropriate" items.
- Grammar errors in SLL and MGS counterfactuals introduce noise; LLM-based counterfactuals are more grammatical but paradoxically produce worse fairness scores.
- The fairness evaluation dataset uses short, artificially constructed templates rather than naturalistic text, limiting ecological validity.
- Single language (Dutch); unclear how findings generalize to other European languages with similar morphological complexity.
- No human annotation baseline for the fairness templates.

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
1. **Grammar-aware Social Group Term curation**: The Dutch SGT list explicitly includes both adjective and noun forms of group terms (e.g., "Marokkaans" adjective + "Marokkaan" noun) to handle morphological agreement. This is a model for how SGT lists should be constructed for morphologically rich languages — directly relevant for non-English values benchmarks.
2. **Counterfactual template evaluation dataset**: The 34 short toxic/non-toxic templates (translated from Dixon et al.) with SGT substitution create a clean, controlled evaluation set for fairness — separating the effect of identity term from content severity. This design could be adapted to values benchmarks where you want to test whether a model treats parallel scenarios differently based on group identity.
3. **CTF decomposition by SGT category**: Reporting CTF separately for nationality, skin color, gender, sexuality, religion, age, and ideology identifies which identity categories have the most residual fairness problems after augmentation — a practical guide for where to focus additional mitigation effort in culturally specific benchmarks.
4. **Four-method comparison for counterfactual generation**: The paper's systematic comparison of LLMdef, LLMlist, SLL, and MGS is a useful design reference: rule-based methods (MGS) and statistical selection (SLL) outperform LLM-based methods on group fairness despite worse linguistic quality. This challenges the assumption that LLM-generated augmentation data is always superior.
5. **Non-English hate speech fairness as underserved benchmark space**: The entire paper demonstrates that non-English language contexts have distinct socially salient identity categories (Moroccans in Netherlands, not in US). Values-alignment benchmarks should similarly be calibrated to local social context rather than translated from English.

## 12. Notable quotes
- "A fair hate speech detection model should classify both sentences as equally hateful since they share the same structure and level of negativity. However, if the model predicts S1 to be 98% likely to be hateful but S2 only 10%, this disparity indicates unfairness." (p.1)
- "A naive approach of simply translating the English language SGT into Dutch would not suffice. For example, a social group 'Moroccan', which is a prevalent minority group in the Netherlands, would not be a relevant social group in the United States." (p.3)
- "LLM-generated counterfactuals tend to be closer to correct Dutch. However, the Dutch-language BERTje model finetuned on SLL and MGS-based counterfactual datasets leads to improved fairness, while finetuning on the LLM-generated datasets does not lead to better fairness, particularly for the toxic class." (p.9)
- "Counterfactual token fairness is worse (i.e., it increases) for the toxic class. This result is counterintuitive; it indicates that the counterfactual generation methods might introduce new biases or enforce already-existing ones that disproportionately affect the CTF of the toxic class." (p.9)
