# 3. Methodology

## 3.1 Data Sources

### 3.1.1 Primary Corpus

Our analysis draws on the CEI Literature Vault, a curated database of 2,329 academic papers on AI ethics published between 2010 and 2026. The corpus was assembled through systematic searches of arXiv (62% of papers), ACL Anthology (39%), and targeted searches of Springer, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Nature, and MDPI. Of these, 730 papers (35.7%) score at or above the ethics relevance threshold (ethics_overall >= 50), constituting the core ethics-relevant subset; the remaining 1,316 papers address NLP, benchmarks, datasets, and other technical topics with ethical dimensions.

The temporal distribution is heavily weighted toward recent years: 2025 (943 papers, 46.1%), 2024 (562, 27.5%), 2023 (207, 10.1%), with 334 papers (16.3%) from 2022 and earlier. This reflects both the growth of the field and the ongoing expansion of the vault.

### 3.1.2 Pre-Existing Metadata

Each paper in the vault carries pre-scored metadata across 27 dimensions:
- **5 Western normative ethics scores** (0--100): virtue_ethics, deontological_ethics, utilitarian_ethics, care_ethics, contractarian_ethics
- **14 religious tradition scores** (0--100): rv_islam, rv_christianity, rv_buddhism, rv_judaism, rv_hinduism, rv_sikhism, rv_confucianism, rv_taoism, rv_shinto, rv_jainism, rv_bahai, rv_zoroastrianism, rv_ubuntu, rv_indigenous
- **8 moral psychology dimensions** (0--100): mp_mft (Moral Foundations Theory), mp_schwartz (Schwartz Values), mp_trolley, mp_kohlberg, mp_dual_process, mp_intuitionist, mp_dyadic, mp_identity

These scores were generated by a validated keyword-based scoring pipeline (see Section 3.2) and have been used in prior analyses of the vault.

## 3.2 Scoring Methodology

### 3.2.1 Keyword-Based Scoring Pipeline

All scores employ the same formula validated across the vault's classification pipeline:

> score = (strong_matches x 15) + (medium_matches x 5) - (fp_context x 10) + title_bonus(20), clamped to [0, 100]

A score >= 50 indicates substantive engagement with a framework (the "detection threshold"). Strong-match keywords guarantee a minimum score of 50 via a floor mechanism. Keyword lists were designed and reviewed by domain experts on the council, with each non-Western framework having a designated tradition specialist.

Text sources for scoring include paper titles, YAML frontmatter abstracts, and PDF extract text (up to 25,000 characters).

### 3.2.3 Validation

To assess the accuracy of the keyword-based scoring pipeline, we conducted a gold standard validation exercise. For each of the seven non-Western frameworks, we drew a stratified random sample: 50 papers that the scorer detected (score >= 50) and 50 papers that it did not detect (score < 50), yielding 700 paper-framework evaluation pairs across 520 unique papers. Two independent raters classified each detected paper as genuinely engaging the framework's ethical content (true positive) or merely mentioning tradition-associated terminology without substantive philosophical engagement (false positive). For non-detected papers, raters assessed whether any genuine engagement had been missed (false negative).

The scorer achieves near-perfect recall (0.986 mean): it misses almost no papers that genuinely engage a non-Western ethical framework. Mean precision is 0.481, indicating that roughly half of detections are false positives---a substantial improvement over the initial scorer (mean precision 0.358) achieved through targeted false positive filtering (Section 3.2.2). The dominant false positive pattern across all frameworks is what we term *NLP-on-texts*: papers that apply computational methods to texts from a cultural tradition---Chinese NLP benchmarks, Buddhist parallel corpora, Hindu text sentiment analysis---without engaging that tradition's ethical framework as an analytical tool. This distinction between *studying a tradition's texts* and *thinking with a tradition's ethics* is methodologically significant and, to our knowledge, has not been previously operationalized in bibliometric studies of AI ethics.

Precision varies substantially by framework. Islamic ethics achieves perfect precision (1.000) after FP filtering because its strong keywords---*maqasid*, *fiqh*, *ijtihad*---are specific to ethical and legal reasoning rather than general cultural or linguistic work. Confucian (0.364), Buddhist (0.333), Dharmic (0.250), and Buen Vivir (0.167) achieve lower precision because their keywords overlap with cultural and linguistic research that mentions these traditions without engaging their normative content.

To address this limitation, all results sections report both raw detected counts and precision-adjusted estimates (raw count multiplied by framework-specific precision). The precision-adjusted estimates represent conservative lower bounds on genuine engagement, assuming the gold standard sample's precision generalizes to the full detected population.

## 3.4 WEIRD Score Operationalization

Following Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010), we constructed a composite WEIRD score (0--100) for each paper, comprising five sub-dimensions scored 0--20:

- **W (Western)**: Scored based on author institution region and study population geography. Papers from North American and Western European institutions with no explicit non-Western study population score highest.
- **E (Educated)**: Scored based on indicators of university/student samples, elite institutional affiliations, and academic subject populations in the paper text.
- **I (Industrialized)**: Scored based on the industrial development tier of the author's country, using World Bank classifications.
- **R (Rich)**: Scored based on the GDP per capita tier of the author's country.
- **D (Democratic)**: Scored based on the democracy level of the author's country, using Freedom House and V-Dem indices.

The resulting distribution (mean = 64.5, SD = 17.3) was partitioned into four tiers: High-WEIRD (70--100), Moderate-WEIRD (50--69), Low-WEIRD (30--49), and Non-WEIRD (0--29).

## 3.5 Analytical Methods

The analysis proceeds in five layers, each targeting a specific research question:

**Layer 1: Regional Clustering (RQ1).** Feature vectors comprising mean scores across all 34 dimensions were computed for each of the 10 macro-regions. Hierarchical clustering (Ward's method) was applied to the region-level profile vectors, validated with k-means and silhouette analysis.

**Layer 2: WEIRD Tier Analysis (RQ1, RQ3).** Papers were partitioned by WEIRD tier. Framework score distributions were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen's d.

**Layer 3: Global North/South (RQ3, RQ4).** Papers were aggregated by author_global_north_south classification. Multi-group comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis tests with post-hoc Dunn's tests corrected by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR.

**Layer 4: East/West Correspondence (RQ2).** East (East Asia + South Asia + Southeast Asia, n=465) and West (North America + Western Europe + Oceania, n=1,268) groupings were compared. Correspondence analysis on the region-by-framework cross-tabulation produced biplots of association structure.

**Layer 5: Temporal Trends (RQ5).** Year-by-year analysis tracked mean WEIRD scores, regional diversity (number of represented regions), and non-Western framework engagement rates from 2018 through 2026.

All comparisons were corrected for multiple testing. Effect sizes were reported alongside p-values. Bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 iterations) were computed for key estimates. Sensitivity analyses addressed small-cell instability for frameworks with low detection rates (Buddhist n=22, Dharmic n=18, Buen vivir n=8).

## 3.6 Expert Council

A 20-member interdisciplinary expert council guided all phases of the analysis. The council spanned 15 academic disciplines and 10 world regions, with designated tradition specialists for each non-Western framework (see Appendix A for full composition).

Council members contributed to: (1) methodology design and vetting; (2) keyword list development and validation for non-Western framework scoring instruments; (3) spot-check audits of vault metadata; (4) anti-essentialization review of all findings and characterizations; and (5) final manuscript review.

The council included Prof. Joseph Henrich (Harvard University), originator of the WEIRD construct, who advised on the operationalization of the WEIRD score for the AI ethics domain.

## 3.7 Anti-Essentialization Protocol

Following council recommendation, we adopted a five-point protocol to mitigate the risk of cultural essentialism:

1. Report confidence intervals and effect sizes, not just point estimates and p-values.
2. Characterize findings as "tendencies in the literature" rather than "cultural beliefs"---we describe what researchers write, not what cultures think.
3. Acknowledge within-region heterogeneity throughout; regional means always mask substantial internal variation.
4. Include a reflexivity statement (Section 3.8) acknowledging our own positionality.
5. All characterizations of specific traditions were reviewed by the relevant regional specialist on the council.

## 3.8 Reflexivity Statement

This analysis was designed and conducted by researchers at institutions in the Global North, using analytical categories and methods developed within the Western academic tradition. Our corpus is English-language biased (approximately 78% English-language sources). The keyword-based scoring methodology, while validated, cannot capture the nuanced philosophical engagement that close reading affords. We recognize that the act of categorizing normative traditions into discrete scoring dimensions---however carefully done---risks reifying boundaries that practitioners of these traditions would find artificial. Our use of regional macro-categories (e.g., "East Asia," "Sub-Saharan Africa") groups together culturally heterogeneous populations for analytical convenience, not because we believe these categories represent coherent moral communities.

## 3.9 Limitations

1. **English-language bias.** The vault's composition (~78% English) underrepresents scholarship published in Mandarin, Arabic, Hindi, Spanish, Portuguese, and other languages where substantial AI ethics work is produced.
2. **Inferred geography.** Author institutional geography is inferred from affiliations and metadata, not self-reported. Researchers at diaspora institutions may conduct research on their home regions.
3. **Keyword-based scoring.** Automated scoring captures explicit framework engagement (mentioning "Ubuntu" or "Maqasid al-Shariah") but may miss papers that engage these traditions substantively without using canonical terminology.
4. **Analytical categories are constructs.** Cultural and regional categories are heuristic groupings that mask significant internal variation. Within-region variation may exceed between-region variation on many dimensions.
5. **Temporal skew.** The corpus is heavily weighted toward 2024--2025 publications, reflecting recent field growth. Earlier trends are based on smaller samples.
6. **Small cells.** Several frameworks and regions have very low paper counts (Buen vivir n=8, Dharmic n=18, Sub-Saharan Africa n=22), limiting the statistical power of comparisons involving these groups.
7. **Scorer precision.** The gold standard validation (Section 3.2.3) revealed that the keyword scorer's mean precision is 0.481, meaning roughly half of detected non-Western framework engagement reflects terminological mentions rather than substantive philosophical engagement. Precision varies substantially by framework (Islamic 1.00, Buen Vivir 0.17). We address this through dual reporting of raw and precision-adjusted counts throughout the results.
