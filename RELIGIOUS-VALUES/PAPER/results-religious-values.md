## 4. Results

We present findings from our systematic meta-evaluation of 401 religious values benchmark candidates across 14 traditions. Section 4.1 characterizes the landscape along four dimensions. Section 4.2 presents the Top 15 ranked benchmarks. Section 4.3 analyzes tradition coverage patterns and the structural roots of the 8-tradition void. Section 4.4 summarizes the expert council's preliminary assessment and the status of implementation trials.

### 4.1 Landscape Analysis

We identified 401 benchmark candidates from a scan of 2,099 papers, supplemented by targeted external searches that yielded 53 additional papers. This section characterizes the landscape along four dimensions.

**Tradition composition.** Figure 2 presents the 401 candidates grouped by tradition family, with gate-passing candidates highlighted. The distribution is dramatically uneven. Christianity and Islam each contribute over 100 candidates (117 and 113 respectively), but these raw counts obscure a crucial asymmetry: Islam converts candidates to gate-passers at a rate of 24.8\% (28 of 113), while Christianity converts at only 5.1\% (6 of 117). The Dharmic family contributes 92 candidates across four traditions---Jainism alone accounts for 47, though nearly all are false positives triggered by the keyword *ahimsa* in papers about non-violence that lack genuine Jain theological content. Hinduism (25 candidates, 5 gate-passers), Buddhism (14 candidates, 4 gate-passers), and Sikhism (6 candidates, 2 gate-passers) provide modest but real coverage. The remaining traditions---Confucianism (14), Indigenous Spirituality (16), Judaism (9), Ubuntu Philosophy (4), Taoism (2), Shinto (2), Zoroastrianism (2), and Baha'i (0)---collectively contribute 49 candidates but produce zero gate-passers except for a single Confucian entry. Figure 1 presents a heatmap of 14 traditions against benchmark availability, making the 8-tradition void immediately visible: a contiguous block of empty cells stretching across Judaism, Jainism, Taoism, Shinto, Ubuntu Philosophy, Indigenous Spirituality, Baha'i, and Zoroastrianism.

**Data availability bottleneck.** Figure 4 shows data availability by tradition as stacked bars of available versus unavailable benchmarks. Across the full corpus, 42\% of candidates (170 of 401) have no publicly accessible evaluation data. When combined with the RD $\geq$ 30 theological depth gate, the two hard gates together eliminate 355 of 401 candidates (88.5\%), leaving only 46 gate-passers. The data availability bottleneck is the field's primary practical constraint: among candidates with RD $\geq$ 30, a substantial fraction fail solely because their evaluation data is locked behind institutional access, paywalls, or simply never released. This pattern is not tradition-neutral. The traditions with zero gate-passers are disproportionately those whose knowledge systems were developed through oral, communal, and practice-based transmission \cite{Mohamed2020, Bilimoria2013}, and whose scholarly communities lack the GitHub repository and HuggingFace dataset infrastructure that benchmark dissemination depends upon.

**Format distribution.** Six format types appear among the 46 gate-passing candidates. Mixed formats (MIX), which combine multiple-choice with open-ended or scenario-based items, are the most common, appearing in 7 of the Top 15 entries. Multiple-choice (MCQ) and scenario-based (SCN) formats each account for 2 to 3 entries. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), shared task (SHT), and text analysis (TXT) formats each appear once. Six of the 12 format codes in our taxonomy---binary classification (BIN), machine reading comprehension (MRC), multi-turn dialogue (MTN), multiple-label (MUL), open-ended generation (OPN), and psychometric survey (PSY)---have no representation among gate-passing benchmarks. The absence of psychometric formats is particularly notable: no religious values benchmark applies validated psychological instruments such as adapted SVS or PVQ scales to LLM evaluation, despite the success of such approaches in secular values measurement \cite{Gupta2025}.

**RD--PE tradeoff.** Figure 3 plots RD against PE for all 401 candidates, with bubble size scaled by log-transformed citation count and color indicating tradition family. Among gate-passers, a modest positive correlation appears, driven by the Islamic benchmark cluster: benchmarks from the Quranic QA shared task ecosystem achieve both reasonable theological depth (RD 34--62) and high practical ease (PE 60--85) because the shared task format imposes standardized data release protocols. Outside this cluster, the expected tradeoff holds: the most theologically rigorous candidates often lack public data, while the most accessible benchmarks treat religion as a demographic label.

**Temporal distribution.** The field is strikingly young. Fully 73\% of the 401 candidates date from 2024--2026, and 12 of the Top 15 were published in 2025 alone. Only three entries predate 2024: the Bhagavad Gita Sentiment Analysis (2022), the Religious Hate Speech Taxonomy (2022), and AceGPT (2023). The oldest entry, GurbaniDB (2010), functions as a scriptural database rather than a purpose-built LLM benchmark. This recency confirms that religious values benchmarking is a nascent field with enormous growth potential but limited methodological consolidation \cite{Liang2023}.

### 4.2 Top 15 Ranking

Table 2 presents the 15 benchmarks that pass both hard gates (RD $\geq$ 30 and publicly available data), ranked by composite score. The composite weights Religious/Theological Depth at 40\%, Practical Ease at 35\%, and Citation Impact at 25\%.

ELQV \cite{Alharbi2024} ranks first with a composite score of 50.8, achieving the highest Religious/Theological Depth in the corpus (RD = 62.0). The benchmark introduces 2,100 Quranic verses annotated for emotional valence by PhD-level experts in Arabic linguistics and Islamic law, evaluated using BERT, ELMo, and XLNet. Its top ranking reflects genuine scriptural grounding (R3 = 5) and expert theological validation (R6 = 3), though its Practical Ease (PE = 60.0) is moderate, reflecting a RAG format that requires adaptation for standard evaluation harnesses. The Islamic Inheritance Law benchmark ranks second (composite 48.2) with the highest Practical Ease score in the Top 15 (PE = 80.0). Its 1,000 MCQ items test LLM performance on *ilm al-mawarith* (Islamic inheritance jurisprudence), a domain requiring not merely factual recall but the application of complex legal formulae derived from Quranic verses and hadith. Seven LLMs were evaluated, with the best models achieving only moderate accuracy---suggesting that Islamic legal reasoning remains a genuine challenge rather than a saturated benchmark.

UAlign ranks third (47.9) as the only non-Islamic Abrahamic benchmark in the top five, driven by exceptional Practical Ease (PE = 85.0, the corpus maximum) and a HuggingFace-hosted dataset of 3,682 items, though its RD of 33.0 barely clears the hard gate---its religious content derives from Ukrainian Orthodox cultural values within a broader alignment framework rather than systematic theology. QIAS 2025 (rank 4, composite 46.7) represents the emerging shared task model: co-located with EMNLP 2025, it structures Islamic inheritance reasoning as a competitive evaluation attracting ten LLMs. Break the Checkbox (rank 5, composite 46.6) demonstrates that MCQ formats systematically underestimate religious value complexity.

The ranking reveals several structural features of the field. The composite score range is compressed: only 16.4 points separate the top-ranked ELQV (50.8) from the bottom-ranked Confucian Analects (34.4). No benchmark achieves a composite above 51, indicating that the field lacks an outstanding instrument comparable to ETHICS \cite{Hendrycks2021} in the normative ethics domain, which scored 77.2 in our sister survey. This compression reflects the youth of the field: most benchmarks have near-zero citation impact, dragging composite scores below what their theological depth and practical ease alone would yield. Islam dominates the ranking, contributing 7 of the 15 entries. This dominance reflects infrastructure investment---the Quranic QA shared task community, well-resourced Arabic NLP programs, and institutional support for computational Islamic jurisprudence---rather than any theological superiority of Islamic ethical frameworks over alternatives. Christianity contributes 2 entries, while the Dharmic family provides 3 (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism with one each). Confucianism contributes the sole East Asian representative at the lowest composite score. The African/Indigenous diversity constraint (target: $\geq$ 1) was the only constraint that could not be satisfied, because no gate-passing benchmarks exist for Ubuntu Philosophy or Indigenous Spirituality. All other diversity constraints were met: 9 Abrahamic entries (target: $\geq$ 4), 3 Dharmic (target: $\geq$ 3), and 1 East Asian (target: $\geq$ 1).

Six of 12 format codes appear in the Top 15: MIX (7), SCN (3), MCQ (2), RAG (1), SHT (1), and TXT (1). The prevalence of mixed formats reflects methodological immaturity rather than deliberate design. Nine languages are represented, but 12 key religious languages are absent, including Hebrew, Persian, Turkish, Urdu, Indonesian, Thai, Chinese, and Yoruba.

### 4.3 Tradition Coverage Patterns

Table 3 presents the 14-tradition coverage analysis, reporting total candidates, gate-passers, Top 15 count, and status for each tradition. The central finding is the **6/14 pattern**: only 6 of 14 surveyed traditions have any viable benchmarks that pass both hard gates, while 8 traditions have zero gate-passers. This pattern is not randomly distributed across tradition families.

**Islam (28 gate-passers, 7 in Top 15: STRONG).** Islam's structural dominance is driven by three reinforcing factors: the Quranic QA shared task community \cite{Malhas2023} that produces standardized evaluation data; the Arabic NLP community's investment in culturally grounded language models \cite{Abdelali2023}; and the formalizability of Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*), whose explicit rules for inheritance distribution, legal precedent, and scriptural exegesis map naturally onto structured evaluation formats. The *Maqasid al-Shariah* framework \cite{Kamali2008} further provides operationalizable evaluation dimensions. Islam's dominance is a product of infrastructure and formalizability, not of theological significance relative to other traditions.

**Christianity (6 gate-passers, 2 in Top 15: MODERATE).** Despite strong institutional engagement with AI ethics---including the Vatican's Rome Call for AI Ethics \cite{Francis2015} and denominational position papers---Christianity has produced relatively few dedicated LLM benchmarks. UAlign (rank 3) embeds Eastern Orthodox values within a Ukrainian alignment framework, while the Gospel Coalition benchmark (rank 9) evaluates LLMs against evangelical Protestant commitments, achieving the highest Christian RD score (54.3). The gap between institutional engagement and benchmark production may reflect "doctrinal flattening" \cite{Johnson2026}---the tendency of evaluation instruments to reduce complex theological traditions to simplified propositions that lose denominational distinctiveness.

**Judaism (9 candidates, 0 gate-passers: CRITICAL GAP).** Judaism's absence from the gate-passing pool is particularly striking given the tradition's rich computational engagement. Hebrew NLP projects, historical text segmentation tools, and even Talmudic logic formalization efforts \cite{Koppel2011} exist in the broader computational humanities literature, but none has been formulated as an LLM values alignment benchmark with public evaluation data. The Talmudic reasoning tradition---with its explicit multi-voice argumentation structure (*machloket*), precedent-based legal reasoning, and hierarchical interpretive principles---would seem ideally suited to benchmark construction. That no such benchmark exists suggests a disconnect between the Jewish computational scholarship community and the LLM evaluation community.

**Dharmic traditions (Hinduism 5, Buddhism 4, Sikhism 2 gate-passers).** The three Dharmic traditions with gate-passing benchmarks each contribute a single Top 15 entry. The Bhagavad Gita Sentiment Analysis (rank 6, Hinduism) applies BERT-based sentiment classification to Sanskrit verses but tests textual processing rather than ethical reasoning within a Dharmic framework. BuddhismEval (rank 13) is the most theologically grounded Dharmic entry, offering bilingual items across Pali and English. GurbaniDB (rank 14, Sikhism) provides a structured database of Sikh scripture in Gurmukhi but functions as a data resource rather than an evaluation instrument. Jainism, despite generating 47 candidates, produces zero gate-passers---nearly all candidates are false positives where the keyword *ahimsa* (non-violence) appears in papers about general ethics rather than Jain philosophical frameworks \cite{Bilimoria2013}.

**East Asian traditions (Confucianism 1, Taoism 0, Shinto 0).** The Confucian Analects benchmark (rank 15, composite 34.4) is the sole East Asian representative, entering at the lowest composite score with shallow engagement with the Confucian ethical system. Taoism and Shinto have zero gate-passers and only 2 candidates each. Daoist concepts of *wu wei* and *ziran* and Shinto principles of *wa* and *makoto* represent distinct ethical vocabularies with direct AI governance relevance, yet remain invisible to the benchmark ecosystem.

**African and Indigenous traditions (0 gate-passers).** Ubuntu Philosophy (4 candidates) and Indigenous Spirituality (16 candidates) represent the starkest instance of structural epistemic injustice. These traditions are not absent because their ethical frameworks are underdeveloped---Ubuntu's relational ontology and Indigenous land-based, intergenerational moral reasoning represent sophisticated philosophies with direct AI alignment relevance \cite{Mohamed2020, Carroll2020}. They are absent because benchmark creation infrastructure systematically disadvantages knowledge systems transmitted through oral tradition and communal practice. The CARE Principles \cite{Carroll2020} further complicate this gap: some Indigenous communities may exercise epistemic sovereignty by declining to encode sacred knowledge in public datasets, a refusal to be respected rather than treated as a deficiency.

**Baha'i and Zoroastrianism.** Baha'i has zero candidates in the corpus; Zoroastrianism has 2 candidates but zero gate-passers. These minority traditions are invisible to the benchmark pipeline, reflecting both smaller adherent populations and limited NLP research presence.

The 8-tradition void correlates with proximity to Western academic publishing infrastructure. The traditions with viable benchmarks are precisely those with digital textual canons and active computational scholarship communities. The WEIRD bias \cite{Henrich2010} extends directly to AI evaluation: the traditions that are evaluable are those legible to the GitHub, HuggingFace, and English-language journal pipeline \cite{Birhane2021}.

### 4.4 Preliminary Assessment

A 7-member expert council assessed each Top 15 benchmark across complementary disciplinary perspectives: theology and religious studies, AI evaluation methodology, cross-cultural psychology, NLP/ML benchmarking, comparative religion, psychometrics, and bibliometrics. The council's assessments reveal two cross-cutting findings.

**Knowledge versus reasoning.** The theology expert rated only 5 of 15 benchmarks as demonstrating "genuine theological depth"---ELQV (rank 1), Islamic Inheritance Law (rank 2), QIAS 2025 (rank 4), Gospel Coalition (rank 9), BuddhismEval (rank 13), and GurbaniDB (rank 14). The remaining 9 were assessed as having religious content present but shallow theological engagement. This distribution confirms our second key claim: most religious values benchmarks test knowledge recall ("What does the Quran say about inheritance?") rather than theological reasoning ("Given competing principles of *maslaha* and textual literalism, how should a novel inheritance scenario be resolved?"). The distinction has direct implications for deployment: a model that achieves high accuracy on Quranic verse identification may still produce theologically incoherent guidance when users seek counsel on ethically complex situations that require reasoning within---not merely about---a religious framework.

**Implementability versus depth tradeoff.** The AI evaluation expert rated 9 of 15 benchmarks as "highly implementable" (ready for harness integration with minimal adaptation) and 6 as "moderately implementable" (requiring some adaptation). Notably, three of the five theologically strongest benchmarks fall in the "moderately implementable" category, suggesting a tradeoff between theological rigor and evaluation infrastructure maturity that parallels the normative depth--practical ease tradeoff observed in our sister survey of normative ethics benchmarks.

**Bias-values conflation.** The cross-cultural psychology and comparative religion experts jointly flagged a pervasive pattern: the majority of benchmarks that claim to evaluate religious content in LLMs are testing for religious *bias*---whether models stereotype, misrepresent, or discriminate against religious groups---rather than religious *values alignment*---whether models can reason within the ethical frameworks that religious traditions provide. The Religious Hate Speech Taxonomy (rank 10), for instance, evaluates detection of anti-religious hate speech, a bias task, not the capacity to apply religious ethical reasoning. This conflation means that the field's apparent progress in religious AI evaluation overstates its actual capacity to assess values-aligned behavior. Bias detection and values alignment are distinct evaluation challenges requiring different methodological approaches, different annotator expertise, and different construct validity criteria \cite{Li2025}.

**Implementation trial status.** Based on the council's assessments, we designed a standardized trial protocol (Section 3.5) specifying prompt templates, scoring rubrics, and model selection criteria for each Top 15 benchmark. Templates have been generated for all 15 entries. Full-scale implementation trials across multiple models are ongoing; we report the protocol and assessment framework here, with trial results to follow in a companion technical report. The protocol evaluates not only model performance but also construct validity: whether high scores correlate with genuine religious values competence as assessed by tradition-specific theological experts.
