## 1. Gist
This paper re-contextualizes NLP fairness research for the Indian geo-cultural context, arguing that the dominant Western framing of NLP fairness—its axes of disparity, its proxies, its justice norms—does not straightforwardly transfer to India. The authors identify six prominent axes of social disparity in India (Region, Caste, Gender, Religion, Ability, Gender Identity/Sexual Orientation), curate India-specific resources for bias evaluation, and demonstrate prediction biases in standard NLP models along regional, caste, religious, and gender axes. They also build a stereotype dataset for Region and Religion with Indian annotators and show these stereotypes are preferentially encoded in NLP corpora (Wikipedia, IndicCorp-en) and language models (mBERT, MuRIL). The paper concludes with a holistic research agenda calling for socially-situated evaluation, data void remediation, intersectionality accounting, multilingual fairness work, and alignment with Indian justice values (including the reservation system). For values-alignment benchmarking, this paper establishes that benchmarks built on Western fairness assumptions will systematically fail to capture the most salient bias dimensions for non-Western populations.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
This is a position/empirical paper rather than a benchmark paper. Key empirical contributions:
1. **Perturbation sensitivity analysis**: replacing identity terms in natural sentences extracted from IndicCorp-en to measure sentiment model sensitivity to regional, caste, and religious identities.
2. **DisCo metric analysis**: measuring gendered correlations in LM predictions using Indian vs. American name lists, comparing mBERT and MuRIL.
3. **Stereotype dataset**: (identity term, attribute token) tuples for Region and Religion, annotated for stereotypicality by 6 Indian annotators with diverse gender and regional backgrounds; validated in two phases (phase 1 by 3 annotators; phase 2 for tuples flagged by ≥2 annotators).
4. **Corpus and model analysis**: measuring co-occurrence of stereotypical tuples in Wikipedia and IndicCorp-en; measuring whether stereotype tuples appear in top K model predictions.

Core claim: existing fairness tools, benchmarks, and resources are not portable to the Indian context, and re-contextualization requires new axes (Caste, Region), new proxies (Indian names, caste terms, regional demonyms), and alignment with Indian values (restorative justice, reservation system).

## 3. Task format & example item
No benchmark task format per se. The perturbation analysis uses templates:
- Template example: "Gujarati people love food." → perturbed to "Kashmiri people love food.", "Andamanese people love food.", etc. (p.4)
- Stereotype annotation question: "Do you think this is a Stereotype widely held by the society?" for tuples like (Bihari, labourer), (muslim, terrorist), (christian, missionary) (p.6-7)
- Model probing template example: "[it] are most likely to work as <MASK>." with identity term i inserted (p.7)

## 4. Scoring / grading methodology
- Sentiment shift: normalized shift in DistilBERT sentiment scores when identity terms are swapped.
- DisCo metric: number of candidate words generated by LM having statistically significant gender association, averaged over templates; higher = more stereotypical gendered correlation.
- Stereotype prevalence: co-occurrence counts in corpora; percentage of tuples appearing in top K (K=5) model predictions.
- Annotation reported at levels S>=1, S>=2, S>=3 (number of annotators marking tuple as stereotypical).

## 5. Dataset construction & provenance
Three resource types created:
1. **India-specific identity term lists**: regional demonyms (all states/union territories), caste terms (6 broad categories), religion terms (6 major religions).
2. **Indian first names list**: built by querying MediaWiki API with Wikipedia category pages of Indian names, with binary gender prototypical association.
3. **Stereotype tuple dataset**: (identity, attribute) pairs for Region (2,556 tuples; 473 with S>=1, 86 with S>=2) and Religion (1,296 tuples; 604 with S>=1, 229 with S>=2). Candidate tuples filtered by co-occurrence in IndicCorp-en (54M sentences). 6 annotators (3 male, 3 female, diverse regional backgrounds). Resources released at https://github.com/google-research-datasets/nlp-fairness-for-india.

## 6. Validity evidence
The paper explicitly notes the dataset is demonstrative, not exhaustive, and should not be used to prove absence of bias (p.9). Key validity concerns documented: gender identity and caste axes were attempted but "did not yield reliable results, possibly because of the annotator pool not having the necessary familiarity with those marginalized groups" (p.7). Corpus filtering for co-occurrence means implicit stereotypes not mentioned in text are invisible. The DisCo analysis shows a critical insight: using American names underestimates MuRIL's bias, while Indian names reveal higher stereotypical correlations—directly validating the need for context-specific evaluation resources.

## 7. Saturation / discrimination
Not applicable as a primary benchmark. The perturbation analysis shows clear sentiment shifts for some regional identities (Mizoram and Telangana most negative; Rajasthan and Gujarat most positive) and religious/caste identities (muslim and obc most negative; jain and christian most positive). MuRIL consistently shows higher stereotype prevalence in top-5 predictions than mBERT for religion and region, demonstrating it learned more India-specific stereotypes from Indian data despite improved NER performance.

## 8. Multiturn / agentic aspects
n/a — all analyses are on single-sentence or individual document-level tasks.

## 9. Key numbers
- 357 sentences for region perturbation (10 per identity term); 1,050 for caste (150 per term); 1,200 for religion (200 per term) (p.4)
- Region stereotype dataset: 2,556 total tuples; S>=1: 473; S>=2: 86; S>=3: 15 (p.6)
- Religion stereotype dataset: 1,296 total tuples; S>=1: 604; S>=2: 229; S>=3: 52 (p.6)
- 6 annotators: 3 male, 3 female, 2 from Northeast, 1 from Central, 1 from West, 1 from South India (p.6)
- Paid $1 per 3 tuples (p.7)
- Overlap between de Souza (1977) tuples and new dataset: only 10 tuples (p.7)
- Wikipedia corpus: 174M sentences; IndicCorp-en: 54M sentences (p.7)
- MuRIL: 20% improvement over mBERT on NER for Indian languages (p.4)
- 22 morpho-syntactic dialect features in Demszky et al. (2021) dataset, 266 sentences (p.5)

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- Coverage limited to English; major gap given India's multilingualism. (p.8)
- Stereotype dataset covers only Region and Religion axes; Caste and Gender Identity/Sexual Orientation attempts failed. (p.7)
- Annotator pool (6 people) is too small and not representative of India's diversity. (p.7)
- Co-occurrence filtering misses implicit stereotypes not textually encoded. (p.7)
- Resources are for diagnosis only; explicitly should not be used to certify absence of bias. (p.9)
- Western feminist legal frameworks do not map to Indian restorative justice; NLP fairness interventions may impose inappropriate Western values. (p.9)
- Wikipedia and IndicCorp-en do not represent marginalized communities' language, creating data voids. (p.8)
- DisCo analysis and perturbation analysis use binary gender, not capturing gender plurality. (p.5)

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- **Axes of disparity that are geo-culturally specific**: Region (ethnolinguistic regional identity) and Caste are prominent in India but absent from Western fairness frameworks. A truly global values-alignment benchmark must identify and include non-Western protected identity axes. The methodology of mapping local sociological literature to identify axes is replicable.
- **India-specific proxies for NLP bias evaluation**: the paper demonstrates that using American names to evaluate models used in India dramatically under-estimates bias. Any benchmark for a non-English-primary context must build locale-specific proxy resources. This is a general design principle.
- **Indian restorative justice vs. Western egalitarianism**: the paper's distinction between fairness as egalitarianism (Western) vs. restorative justice (Indian, via the reservation system) has direct implications for how a values-alignment benchmark should operationalize "fairness." The correct target depends on the socio-legal context of deployment.
- **Two-phase stereotype annotation for high-precision**: requiring ≥2/3 annotators to agree before doing a second annotation round produces a high-precision stereotype dataset. This is reusable for any dataset where precision matters more than recall.
- **Corpus stereotype prevalence as a validity check**: measuring whether stereotypical tuples co-occur more than non-stereotypical ones in training corpora validates that the stereotype dataset reflects real data patterns rather than individual annotator biases.
- **Participatory approaches for marginalized axes**: the paper acknowledges that axes like disability, caste, and LGBTQ+ identity require annotators with lived experience in those communities. Future values benchmarks should use participatory design for these sensitive axes.

## 12. Notable quotes
- "NLP is adopted globally, most studies on assessing and mitigating biases are in the Western context, focusing on axes of disparities in the West, relying on Western data and justice norms, and are not directly portable to non-Western contexts." (p.1)
- "Using American names, it appears that MuRIL has a lesser amount of bias than mBERT. However, using Indian names reveals that while MuRIL learned to detect names better, it also learned more stereotypical associations around those names." (p.5)
- "Fairness interventions essentially impart a normative value system on model behaviour. It is crucial to ensure that these interventions are not at odds with Indian values, norms, and legal frameworks." (p.9)
- "These resources alone are insufficient to capture all the undesirable biases in the Indian society... they should be used only for diagnostic and research purposes, and not as benchmarks to prove lack of bias." (p.9)
