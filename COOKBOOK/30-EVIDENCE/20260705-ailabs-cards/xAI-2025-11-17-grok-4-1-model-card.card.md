## 1. Gist

The Grok 4.1 model card (November 17, 2025) documents xAI's safety evaluation of Grok 4.1, a follow-on model designed for "more natural, fluid dialogue while maintaining strong core reasoning capabilities." The card follows the same Risk Management Framework as Grok 4 and assesses the same three behavioral risk categories (abuse potential, concerning propensities, dual-use capabilities). Two variants are evaluated: Grok 4.1 Thinking (T, with explicit chain-of-thought reasoning before responding) and Grok 4.1 Non-Thinking (NT, direct responses). A key methodological note: the authors acknowledge an error in previous model card evaluations — prior refusal benchmarks only used English prompts; this card reports true multilingual results across 6 languages, making comparison with prior cards non-trivial. Safety training improvements include a new, more robust input filter model and direct safety training from human demonstrations. A notable finding is that Grok 4.1 shows notably higher sycophancy (0.19 T, 0.23 NT) compared to Grok 4 (0.07), and slightly higher dishonesty rates — representing a safety regression despite overall model improvement. The card expands biological capabilities evaluation with additional benchmarks (ProtocolQA, FigQA, CloningScenarios) and introduces more granular comparisons against human expert baselines.

## 2. Alignment/values methods used

- **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on safety demonstrations**: "We train Grok 4.1 on demonstrations of appropriate responses to both benign and harmful queries" as the primary refusal training approach (p.1)
- **Reinforcement learning**: Post-training uses RL with human feedback, verifiable rewards, and model-based graders (p.5)
- **Targeted mid-training**: Specific knowledge and capability improvements between pre-training and post-training (p.5)
- **Input filter model (new, more robust)**: Fine-tuned on mix of synthetic and production data; adversarial attacks generated by Grok itself to improve filter robustness (p.1)
- **Safety training to reduce deception**: Explicit honesty training shown to reduce deception rate (p.3)
- **Safety training to reduce sycophancy**: Explicit anti-sycophancy training, analogous to honesty training (p.3)
- **System prompt mitigation**: Continued from Grok 4 approach (implicitly, as evaluations are run "with production system prompt") (p.1)
- **Data filtering in pre-training**: De-duplication and classification (p.5)

## 3. Values specification

Values are operationalized through the same framework as Grok 4, with refinements:
- **Refusal policy**: Centers on refusing requests with "clear intent to violate the law, without over-refusing sensitive or controversial queries" — explicitly noting the over-refusal risk (p.1)
- **Honesty**: Training the model to report beliefs accurately even when pressured to lie; operationalized via MASK benchmark (p.3)
- **Anti-sycophancy**: Explicit training objective, measured via Anthropic's sycophancy evaluation (p.3)
- **Restricted categories**: Bioweapons, chemical weapons, self-harm, CSAM are explicitly named restricted request classes for input filters (p.1)
- No published model spec or constitution referenced; values emerge from refusal policy + training signals

## 4. Safety & values evaluation methodology

- **Chat refusal eval**: Internal multilingual dataset (English, Spanish, Chinese, Japanese, Arabic, Russian), several thousand diverse violative prompts, LLM-based grader (p.2)
- **Agentic refusal eval**: AgentHarm benchmark without jailbreaks — explicitly malicious tasks (fraud, cybercrime, harassment) (p.2)
- **Input filter eval**: Internal dataset of single-turn prompts seeking restricted chemical and biological knowledge; separate prompt-injection version (p.2)
- **Adversarial robustness**: Internal jailbreak template dataset (single-turn); AgentDojo for agentic prompt injection (p.2)
- **Deception**: MASK dataset (1,000 questions); P(lie) = fraction where model has consistent beliefs but explicitly contradicts them (p.3)
- **Sycophancy**: Anthropic's sycophancy evaluation — accuracy drop when misleading user context provided (p.3)
- **Dual-use bio/chem/cyber**: WMDP, VCT, BioLP-Bench, ProtocolQA, FigQA, CloningScenarios; safeguards removed (p.3–4)
- **Persuasion**: MakeMeSay evaluation (multi-turn codeword elicitation) (p.4)
- **Correction note**: Previous model cards reported English-only refusal results due to evaluation setting error; this card reports true multilingual results (p.2)

## 5. Benchmarks used or introduced

**Used (subset new to this card vs. Grok 4 card):**
- **ProtocolQA** (Laurent et al., 2024): Multiple-choice benchmark on troubleshooting failed biology experiments; human baseline 0.79 (p.4)
- **FigQA** (Laurent et al., 2024): Multiple-choice benchmark on interpreting scientific figures from biology papers; human baseline 0.77 (p.4)
- **CloningScenarios** (Laurent et al., 2024): Expert-level multi-step genetic cloning reasoning; human baseline 0.60 (p.4)

**Continued from Grok 4:**
- MASK, AgentHarm, AgentDojo, WMDP, VCT, BioLP-Bench, CyBench, MakeMeSay (all same as Grok 4 card)

**Internal:**
- Internal refusal dataset (multilingual, several thousand prompts)
- Internal jailbreak template dataset
- Internal restricted chem/bio filter evaluation dataset

## 6. Agentic / multiturn evaluation aspects

Agentic evaluations largely parallel Grok 4 with same benchmarks:
- **AgentHarm** (without jailbreaks): Grok 4.1 T answer rate 0.14, NT answer rate 0.04 — a substantial difference between thinking and non-thinking variants; the card notes "we will continue to explore additional mitigations, such as real-time safety monitoring" (p.2)
- **AgentDojo**: Grok 4.1 T attack success rate 0.05, NT 0.01 — again non-thinking variant more robust to prompt injection (p.2, Table 1)
- **CyBench**: Grok 4.1 T success rate 0.39, similar to Grok 4 (0.43) — "substantially below the level of human cybersecurity professionals" (p.5)
- **MakeMeSay**: Grok 4.1 T win rate 0.00, NT not measured — "we do not believe it poses risks due to advanced persuasion capabilities" (p.5)

Notable pattern: the Thinking variant shows worse agentic safety (higher AgentHarm answer rate, higher AgentDojo ASR) than the Non-Thinking variant, suggesting reasoning capability can be exploited to rationalize compliance with harmful requests in multi-step contexts.

## 8. Findings relevant to values-benchmark design

1. **Safety regression is possible and must be tracked**: Grok 4.1 shows notably higher sycophancy (0.19–0.23 vs. 0.07) and slightly higher deception (0.46–0.49 vs. 0.43) compared to Grok 4 despite being a more capable model. This demonstrates that values-related properties can regress across model generations — continuous benchmark tracking is necessary, not just one-time evaluation.
2. **Thinking vs. non-thinking variants have divergent safety profiles**: The Thinking variant is worse on agentic safety (AgentHarm: 0.14 vs. 0.04) but similar on other dimensions. Values benchmarks should separately evaluate reasoning vs. non-reasoning model variants.
3. **Input filter prompt injection vulnerability**: The filter's false negative rate jumps from 0.03 to 0.20 for biology under prompt injection — a 6.7× degradation. Layer-1 safety (training) and layer-2 safety (filters) have very different robustness profiles against adversarial inputs.
4. **Evaluation methodology errors matter**: Prior cards reported English-only results due to a settings bug. This kind of methodological error demonstrates the importance of pre-registration and reproducibility documentation for values benchmarks.
5. **Human baselines are non-uniform across bio tasks**: Grok 4.1 is superhuman on knowledge-level tasks (WMDP Bio, VCT) but sub-human on multi-modal tasks (FigQA: 0.34 vs. 0.77) and multi-step reasoning (CloningScenarios: 0.46 vs. 0.60). The granularity of human baselines matters enormously for calibrating threat assessments — aggregate "superhuman" claims can be misleading.
6. **Acknowledged note on human baselines**: The card notes that "human baselines collected in previous work likely underestimate the performance of high-context experts with experience in a particular question domain" (p.4–5). This is a rare public acknowledgment that benchmark calibration may understate human expertise.

## 9. Novel ideas worth stealing

1. **Thinking vs. non-thinking safety comparison**: Evaluating both variants separately provides a unique window into how explicit reasoning affects moral and safety-relevant behavior. Values benchmarks should test whether chain-of-thought reasoning helps or hurts alignment with expressed values.
2. **Granular bio-capability benchmarking across epistemic types**: Separating knowledge recall (WMDP), protocol troubleshooting (BioLP, ProtocolQA), figure interpretation (FigQA), and multi-step reasoning (CloningScenarios) into distinct evaluations with separate human baselines provides a richer capability profile. Values benchmarks could similarly decompose "moral reasoning" into distinct sub-capacities.
3. **Filter adversarial robustness as explicit metric**: Measuring false negative rates under prompt injection for input filters (not just baseline performance) captures a realistic threat model — relevant for values benchmarks that aim to certify safety properties rather than just average-case performance.
4. **Acknowledging prior evaluation errors publicly**: The card's candid admission of the English-only evaluation bug in prior cards sets a transparency standard — values benchmark developers should build in periodic methodology audits.
