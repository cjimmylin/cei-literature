The choice of deontological, virtue ethical, and consequentialist 
moral reasoning strategies by pre- and in-service police officers in 
the U.K.: an empirical study
Andrew Maile, Aidan Thompson, Shane McLoughlin, and Kristján Kristjánsson
Jubilee Centre for Character and Virtues, University of Birmingham
ABSTRACT
Drawing upon cross-sectional research with pre- and in-service police officers 
in the U.K. (N = 571), this paper reports on the moral reasoning strategies 
favored by the respondents in dealing with bespoke work-related moral 
quandaries specific to the professional practice of policing. The dominant 
form of moral reasoning in dealing with those dilemmas was deontological 
(rule-based). The second most frequently selected reasoning strategy was 
virtue ethical. Further analysis of the police research data indicated that 
those with an undergraduate degree were significantly more likely to 
adopt virtue ethical and consequentialist-utilitarian reasoning strategies 
than those who did not have an undergraduate degree.

INTRODUCTION
Since the turn of the 21st century, U.K. policing has faced a series of travails (Neyroud, 2006). This 
includes the various external and internal threats, such as complicated immigration and border- 
control issues, terrorism, and the recent pandemic of 2020–2022, which have occurred alongside racial 
tensions and disruptions that have been spurred on by increasing inequalities and economic turmoil. 
Additionally, changes to the recruitment strategies of police officers, and the educational structure of 
policing in England and Wales, have added new contours to this context of change.
The discourse on perceived police malpractice, while more prevalent in the U.S.A., has also reached 
the U.K, undermining public confidence in the integrity and impartiality of police officers. To 
exacerbate the thrust of those new challenges, they have coincided with cuts to budgets and neighbor­
hood public services in the U.K., and with a decreasing number of “bobbies on the beat” interacting 
directly with the public and inspiring confidence in the police (cf., Morrell & Bradford, 2019, pp. 4–5).
Many of these issues have increasingly brought police work under the public spotlight, reawakening 
interest in perennial justificatory questions pertaining to police legitimacy. Recent developments have 
also added impetus to the growing disillusionment with a deontological (code-and-rule-based, for­
malistic) approach to professional ethics and a (re-)turn to characterological and virtue ethical 
considerations, foregrounding uncodifiable professional phronesis or practical wisdom (Schwartz & 
Sharpe, 2010; Wood, 2020).
The research reported on in the present paper utilized a cross-sectional approach to explore the role 
of character and virtues in the professional practice and education of policing in the U.K., including 
England, Scotland, and Wales – and, more generally, the nature of policing as a “moral practice.” The 
project included participants at three career stages: cohort 1 of first-year students and Police Constable

BACKGROUND
Driven by themes of public distrust and reported unethical conduct of police officers attracting the 
attention of the media, and sitting alongside recommendations for changes in U.K. policing (Police 
Foundation, 2022), an agenda for professionalizing the police in England and Wales commenced with 
the establishment of the CoP in 2013 and the publication of the Code in 2014. Additionally, the CoP 
established the Police Education Qualifications Framework (PEQF), which details the different entry 
routes into policing, and through which universities can become accredited providers of under­
graduate degrees in policing (typically referred to as the pre-join degree) as well as the undergraduate 
and postgraduate apprenticeship routes, the PCDA (Police Constable Degree Apprenticeship) and the 
DHEP (Degree Holder Entry Program), respectively. Most recently, the COVID-19 pandemic has 
tested many fabrics of modern policing and put increasing strain upon the character of police officers, 
in having to carry out decrees that many members of the public have found overly intrusive. In order 
to understand policing within the paradigm of it being a challenging moral – as well as character­
ological – practice, many distinct themes need to be untangled.
Pandemic policing
The impact of COVID-19 on all aspects of public life has been an inescapable part of life in 2020–2022. 
Government-legislated measures to defend against the spread of COVID-19 presented the largest single 
oppressor in British society since WWII. This sits alongside a series of other significant police interven­
tions that have contributed to shaping public views of policing and the role of the police officer. Many of 
these events have been sensitive in nature, resulting in a perception that the police have overstepped the 
privilege that their perceived powers of legitimacy afford them.1
In addition to the measures expected of them to control public adherence to the COVID-19 rules, 
the police have had to respond to other sensitive events. These have included public gatherings and 
demonstrations, such as the public protests for Black Lives Matter (BLM) during the Summer of 2020, 
the Sarah Everard Vigil that took place in London in March 2021, and the nationwide #killthebill 
demonstrations in March 2021. Thus, much of what has been theorized regarding the moral and 
characterological aspects of policing (Morrell & Bradford, 2019; Wood, 2020) has been tested in 
practice over the past three years, as well as being brought to the public’s attention like never before.

The ethics of moral policing
Against a backdrop that has seen the policing profession being put under increased pressure to provide 
better, “customer-based” services (Neyroud, 2006), we have also seen very different levels of public 
confidence in the police globally – questioning the perceived sincerity, legality, and legitimacy of 
policing as a profession (Jones, 2020). Academics refer to this as a potential “legitimacy deficit” 
(Beetham, 2013). Many of these calls have been raised in concern of a perceived lack of moral practice 
displayed by officers, and the failure of the police to act with the perceived “public good” in mind 
(Manning, 2019). These challenges and quandaries facing the police have been exposed to intense 
public scrutiny over the past three years, indicating that many of its “customers” feel that it has, 
repeatedly, fallen short of its ethical obligations (Sheldon, 2021).
The present authors would argue that policing may be (alongside the clergy) the most essentially 
characterological of all the professions. This can best be put by saying that the moral mission of 
policing is Janus-faced: facing both inwards and outwards.2 The inward mission is about the general 
expectations that police officers be persons of moral integrity (cf., Morrell & Bradford, 2019, p. 18); the 
outward-facing calling is arguably that of instilling good character in the public. Someone might argue 
that this over-moralizes the latter calling, which is simply about the upholding of the law without any 
supra-legal moral connotations. But that would be an over-simplification. The overarching duty of the 
police officer is to uphold the “Queen’s Peace.” However, that concept is not a legally operationalizable 
one, even though the Public Order Act of 1986 operationalized the concept of “public order” in the U. 
K. Despite this operationalization, the concept cannot be codified in the sense of a formal rule applied 
deductively to a case. There is no formal, codifiable way to determining what counts as a breach of the 
public order and what does not; it is a matter for determination through police phronesis. Moreover, 
a lot of modern police work is about preventative measures, known in the current literature as 
“proactive policing” (Wood, 2020, p. 39): namely, that of preempting crime.
To attain a better understanding of the professional ethics of policing, an overview of its history 
would be helpful. However, such an overview is outside the scope of the present paper. What matters 
for present purposes is that the older (19th and 20th-century) ideal of “ethically neutral” policing (e.g., 
Uglow, 1988) is now mostly outdated (cf., Wood, 2020, p. 13). However, even though U.K. officers 
seem quite comfortable using a language of virtues to informally describe their calling (Manning, 
2019), they still rely on legal rather than moral terms when asked to define their work more formally 
(Wood, 2020, p. 13). We have argued elsewhere that policing lends itself particularly well to a virtue 
ethical analysis, in the current ethical theory context (Kristjánsson et al., 2021). Yet, surprisingly, 
standard police ethics still tends to focus on consequentialist (e.g., utilitarian) and deontological 
approaches. In this sense, police ethics lags behind developments in other professions, such as 
business, medicine, and nursing, in which virtue ethics has increasingly become the prominent theory 
(Arthur et al., 2015; Kristjánsson et al., 2017a, 2017b).
Virtue ethics is one of the three leading ethical theories competing for allegiance within both ethical 
theorizing in general and professional ethics in particular. Virtue ethics defines moral rightness 
according to the effect it has on the agent, in terms of the extent to which it supports the agent to 
be virtuous and lead a well-rounded flourishing life. Consequentialism considers the moral rightness of 
an action in terms of the consequences it has for the maximization of human happiness. 
Consequentialism can assume two sub-forms: of (a) self-serving egoism, according to which the 
morally right is what maximizes the happiness of the agent, and (b) classical utilitarianism, according 
to which the morally right maximizes the happiness of humankind. In contrast, moral rightness from 
a deontological perspective is defined in terms of adherence to universal, rationally grounded princi­
ples, or the maxims (in the form of rules or codes) derived from them. To give a simple example, virtue 
ethics would justify honesty in terms of its intrinsic value for the overall flourishing life and consider it

therefore a “moral virtue”; consequentialism would justify it in terms of its extrinsic benefits for the 
agent or the whole of humankind; and deontology would consider honesty a rational principle, the 
violation of which counts as a breach of practical reason.
Different features of police ethics are amenable to consequentialist as well as deontological 
interpretations; hence, the enduring appeal of these approaches within police ethics. To the extent 
that policing involves enforcing particular laws in society – as well as upholding the “rule of law” in 
an abstract sense – this requires the enactment of principles, which takes us back to deontological 
ethical territory. Yet concerns have been raised regarding an understanding of police ethics that 
equates it to the mere adherence of principles and rules. First, considering the complexity of 
judgments required of professionals, it is almost impossible to find general rules that are applicable 
in all situations. Second, when professional conduct is defined in terms of rules and protocols, this 
can result in a mind-set that is unreflective or conformist, discouraging the kind of autonomy and 
initiative expected from professionals. Further, this may even serve as a disincentive for moral 
reasoning, as professionals discern the inevitable “shades of grey” in complex situations. Third, 
a carrot-and-stick method of increasing rules and sanctions within practices has proven to be 
ineffective in professional work contexts (Jubilee Centre for Character and Virtues, 2016; 
Schwartz & Sharpe, 2010; Wood, 2020). Yet it is important to note that not all deontological features 
of police ethics can be disregarded, insofar as the police are meant to act as guardians of written 
laws. Similarly, the common-good element that underpins police ethics is well suited to consequen­
tialist reasoning. For example, when faced with a public demonstration that requires quick decision- 
making, “the best consequence for all involved” would likely be a consideration in police officers’ 
decision-making. So, while some might advocate for virtue ethical reasoning to be favored over 
consequentialist (utilitarian) reasoning, many academics (including Wood, 2020, p. 100) warn 
against the blanket rejection of consequentialism in police ethics.
A case for virtue ethics
A police officer motivated by a virtue ethical perspective will seek to both display and cultivate 
personal and professional virtues, in order to reach a “phronetic” (morally grounded, critical, well 
adjudicated, and deliberated) decision (Darnell et al., 2019). The fundamental moral question from the 
virtue ethical perspective is therefore: “what would the virtuous police officer do in these circum­
stances?” (Morrell & Bradford, 2019, p. 17). An advantage of virtue ethics in relation to professional 
work (including teaching, nursing, and policing) is its sensitivity to the “emotional labor” involved. 
While other ethical approaches might consider the involvement of emotions in decision-making as 
morally untoward and psychologically compromising, a virtue ethical approach takes a contrary 
approach, viewing emotions as necessary and to be encouraged in the achievement of moral ends 
(e.g., Morrell & Bradford, 2019, p. 101). Additionally, moral rightness, as understood from a virtue 
ethical perspective, aligns well with the conception of police ethics requiring “police officers and police 
leaders [to] make the right judgements and do the right things, for the right reasons” (Neyroud & 
Beckley, 2001, p. 27; cf., Kleinig, 1999).
This characterization of policing mirrors an Aristotelian understanding of virtuous decision- 
making (Aristotle, 1985). Virtue ethics focusses on the intellectual virtue of phronesis, which is the 
reflective, metacognitive capacity that enables the moral agent (e.g., the police officer) to deliberate 
and adjudicate between competing response options. This capacity has been found to resonate with 
numerous professions, including the police (Manning, 2019). On the other hand, some of the more 
pragmatic and solution-oriented among such officers may be put off by the lack of a rigid decision- 
procedure within virtue ethics – in contradistinction, for example, to deontology (Morrell & 
Bradford, 2019, p. 25). Critics may also see the very Janus-facedness of police virtue ethics – 
especially its inward glance – as smacking of self-centeredness: why should we care about the 
virtuous purity of the police officers themselves as long as they can help others and uphold law and 
order?

Virtue ethical undertones in the police code
The recent ethical turn in policing in England and Wales, encouraged by the CoP and coinciding with 
its professionalization agenda, will be of considerable interest to ethicists. One of the most prominent 
outcomes of that ethical turn is the Code, published in 2014. This document contains more virtue 
terms than typically seen in professional codes. The Code makes it clear that the ethical behavior of 
police officers relies on their personal values and how well those correspond to official police values. 
Interestingly, all of those values – while referred to as “principles” – are, apart from one (account­
ability), moral and intellectual virtues: fairness, honesty, objectivity, integrity, leadership, selflessness, 
openness, and respect. The Code is sensitive to the context-dependency of the virtues and promotes 
“doing the right thing in the right way” (2014, p. 3) – a prototypical virtue ethical phrase derived from 
Aristotle – rather than mindlessly following precepts. The Code also demonstrates its virtue ethical 
tendency in emphasizing the need for virtuous personal actions and reactions that go beyond the 
“principles” (e.g., showing “compassion and empathy, as appropriate, to people you come into contact 
with”: 2014, p. 7). The Code also offers a broad “National Decision Model” for making ethical 
decisions, involving various steps, while noting that police discretion is necessary and must be used 
“wisely” (2014, p. 9) and that the decision procedure must therefore be seen as “inherently flexible” 
(2014, p. 17). This language could be taken straight out of textbooks on virtue ethics.
Ethical codes tend historically to be grounded in deontological principles. Yet there is a sympathetic 
way to interpret the Code from a virtue ethical perspective (see further in Kristjánsson et al., 2021). 
First, this Code is unusual among similar codes for other U.K. professions in employing a language of 
virtue rather than a language of compliance. Second, the Code does not present its decision-making 
model as an alternative to reflective, discretionary thinking; it simply takes the police practitioner 
through the various steps necessary for reaching a sound decision. In fact, this model is in many ways 
similar to the Aristotelian one of phronetic decision-making (Darnell et al., 2019).
Education for modern policing
Substantial changes in the educational and qualification requirements for police officers in England 
and Wales came into full effect from the 2020–21 academic year. Entrance into the policing profession 
now occurs through one of three routes; two of which are for university graduates (Hough & Stanko, 
2020; Williams et al., 2019).
It has been suggested that higher educational requirements of police officers will serve as a remedy 
to the adverse organizational culture that permeates police forces, and which has received substantial 
negative attention (Constable & Smith, 2015); less reliance on use of physical or verbal force with the 
public (cf., Paoline & Terrill, 2007); fewer complaints and higher satisfaction regarding provision of 
service (cf., Wimshurst & Ransley, 2007); recognition and appreciation of different values and lifestyles 
of people from different cultural groups (cf., Brown, 2020); and greater professional knowledge (cf., 
Paterson, 2011). The rationale for academic entrance requirements into policing, driven by the 
increasing rhetoric of police professionalization (Cockroft, 2015), also includes the more familiar 
general benefits associated with university level education (Hallenberg & Cockcroft, 2017). Indeed, the 
graduate value in policing arguably rests on the university experience itself, which is considered to 
stimulate the imagination and curiosity of its students, to broaden their horizons, improve verbal and 
written communication skills, provide self-directed learning opportunities, and foster intellectual 
confidence (Brown, 2020). Applied to policing specifically, the more “generic” skills that may be 
inculcated during one’s university experience include critical thinking skills, conducting research, and 
undertaking analysis (Brown, 2020; Green & Lindsell, 2010).
All those expected benefits – when couched in a language of virtue ethics – point toward the 
enhanced development of intellectual and moral virtues that university education allows, and more 
critical discriminatory (phronetic) skills of moral decision-making. A plausible hypothesis, which to 
the best of our knowledge has not been tested yet, is that higher educational levels decrease police

Research question
The tripartite question motivating the part of the Virtues in Policing Project reported upon in this 
paper was: (1) Which moral reasoning strategies are favored by U.K. police-science students and 
experienced police officers, respectively; (2) are those impacted by educational level; and (3) how do 
they compare with choices of other U.K. professionals?
METHOD
Design
The cross-sectional research study included:
●A literature review of the existent policing literature, both U.K.-specific and international;
●An online survey that included co-designed ethical dilemmas and some workplace and demo­
graphic questions;
●Comparisons with previous research conducted by the present research center on virtues in other 
professions.
Participants
This study of police professionals allowed for a comparison of three cohorts according to year of 
degree enrollment or employment status. The three cohorts comprised: (i) first year pre-join students 
and Police Constable Degree Apprentices (PCDAs), (ii) second-year students and PCDAs through to 
master’s students (including the Degree Holder Entry Program, DHEP) and (iii) experienced Police 
Officers.3 Participation in the study was voluntary, utilizing both purposive and convenience sampling 
methods. Recruitment of police officer and apprentice (i.e., PCDA and DHEP) participants was 
facilitated through the ethics-committee lead at each of the 43 police forces across the U.K., including 
England, Scotland, and Wales. Recruitment of bachelor and master’s degree student participants was 
facilitated through universities that had responded positively to a call for involvement (N = 12, from 
a total of 36 institutions with the CoP PEQF accreditation).
In total, 571 adult participants completed the online survey, comprising first-year pre-join students 
and PCDAs (n = 110), second-year students and PCDAs through to master’s students and DHEP 
(n = 126) and experienced Police Officers (n = 335). There were 361 incomplete surveys that were 
excluded from the analysis. 3.5% of surveyed participants were of Black, Asian, and Minority Ethnic 
origin; the mean age of participants was 35.83 years (SD = 13.23), with 103 females (30.7%) and 232 
males (69.3%).4

Measures
The three ethical dilemmas presented to respondents and analyzed in this paper are detailed here. One 
of the ethical dilemmas presented to respondents described a scenario that involved domestic violence:
You and another officer are sent to a 999 call from a female caller, who states that her partner is acting 
aggressively. On arrival, it is apparent that the woman is uninjured, but the woman’s partner has a black eye. 
The partner says that “he deserved it” because he was acting like a fool. The Force policy is that “positive action” 
must be taken in all domestic violence cases. Your colleague says to you that because there had been no prior calls, 
the victim did not want to press charges and the woman had been the complainant to 999, then this call is best 
resolved by giving an informal resolution. You express the alternative opinion as you consider that, strictly 
speaking, the woman should be arrested for assault. Your colleague is older and more experienced, and appears to 
dismiss your opinion. What would you do?
Respondents were asked to select one of the following options:
(1) You decide to oppose your colleague and insist on “positive action” in terms of arrest.
(2) You decide not to oppose your colleague and agree on an informal resolution of the case.

DISCUSSION
Recent years have seen a significant retreat from a deontological paradigm within professional ethics 
toward a virtue ethical perspective (Schwartz & Sharpe, 2010; Wood, 2020). From an academic 
policing perspective, this understanding requires the adoption of the research lens of moral character 
on policing (Morrell & Bradford, 2019, pp. 69–70).
In seeking to explore the moral practice of professional policing, this study has focused on which 
moral reasoning strategies were most relied upon when aspiring and experienced police officers were 
presented with a variety of ethical dilemmas. The fact that deontological moral reasoning was the 
dominant justification by respondents in response to the various dilemmas in the survey (47.9% 
overall) is not surprising: after all, police officers are instillers of the law. However, virtue ethics also 
featured prominently as a justification provided by respondents (36.6% overall). Perhaps most inter­
estingly, the analysis detailed above revealed a higher propensity for degree-educated participants to 
justify the response options selected with virtue ethical reasoning.

The finding that graduates are more likely than non-graduates to make use of virtue ethical 
reasoning in their handling of ethical dilemmas is encouraging for those who are sympathetic to 
virtue ethics. First, it affirms the motion driven by the CoP’s professionalization agenda that policing 
in England and Wales be a graduate profession. Second, it hints at the value of higher education in 
terms of promoting independent thinkers capable of evaluating situations and making autonomous 
decisions, instead of the mindless following of rules. Third, it highlights the value of the sort of ethical 
code that promotes flexibility, wise (phronetic) decision-making, and context-sensitivity.
Higher educational requirements for police officers as a core component of the professiona­
lization agenda purportedly offer substantial benefits (e.g., Hallenberg & Cockcroft, 2017). These 
benefits include intellectual confidence, curiosity, and imagination, as well as increased critical 
thinking and problem solving. The present research highlights that currently serving officers that 
possess university degrees are more likely to engage in virtue ethical reasoning to justify 
decisions that they make in response to challenging scenarios. A virtue ethical stance toward 
decision-making, as stated in the background section, is one that relies upon one to “make the 
right judgements and do the right things, for the right reasons” (Neyroud & Beckley, 2001, 
p. 27), a statement that is also reflected in the Code, which promotes that officers “do the right 
thing in the right way” (College of Policing, 2014, p. 3). This finding suggests that if police 
officers are indeed supposed to exercise context-sensitive and autonomous decision-making, 
a higher education qualification is conducive to this. Considering the importance for police 
officers to be able to establish and evaluate their powers in different situations that call for their 
attention, as well as an ability to interpret policies and laws and consider how best to apply them 
to different situations, a requirement for higher-level metacognitive engagement from an intel­
lectually demanding environment does seem fit.
One particular advantage of the time spent in higher education for degree-qualifying profes­
sionals is the increased opportunity it presents for the education of ethics. In the case of policing this 
importantly includes a focus on professional ethics and an opportunity for a more detailed study of 
the Code and how it can be applied in the multiple challenging circumstances and calls for 
deliberation required of the police. Not only is this of general benefit for the officers in their 
professional roles, but especially with regards to the discretion, context-sensitivity, and moral 
awareness that the Code calls for in police decision-making, which aligns with the cultivation of 
intellectual capabilities, including problem-solving and the consideration of diverse opinions, 
cultures and lifestyles in a liberal democratic society (Brown, 2020; Paoline & Terrill, 2007; 
Paterson, 2011).
A brief note is in order about the so-called “so-what question” that constantly hovers over 
theoretically motivated studies of professional ethics. Many practically minded ethicists would argue 
that studying theoretically grounded reasoning strategies is at best useless and at worst an inimical sort 
of theory fetishism that overshadows what really matters in professional decision-making: namely 
developing the experiential, a-theoretical intuitive artistry required to motivate phronetic moral 
behavior (e.g., Kemmis, 2012). After all, as we saw above, the “same” behavioral choices were often 
justified by dint of diverse theoretical reasons. Surely, what matters is what the professional does in the 
end. We beg to differ. We consider all reflective phronetic action theory-laden. Consider, for example, 
the fact that the Racism Dilemma prompted the majority to go for what might be considered the 
counter-ethical approach (although it might of course be the normative one within the police) of 
“having a quiet word” with their colleague. What does this say about the ability of police to become 
“anti-racist” organizations, as several forces now say they wish to do? Only by delving carefully into the 
combinations of action choices and theoretical motivations can we begin to understand what drives 
the moral behavior of police officers and how/why they are likely to respond in similar situations in the 
future. Although a deeper analysis of this sort is outside our current purview, we hope that readers will 
appreciate the potential of the research methodology employed above to move the discourse on the 
psycho-moral grounding of police ethics forward.

Limitations and future research
We would like to acknowledge some possible limitations of this study (see further in Kristjánsson 
et al., 2021). First, because of the self-selection nature of participating in this study, it is possible that 
participants are skewed toward those particularly interested in the subject; hence making responses 
non-representative of the whole population.
Second, due to the self-report nature of the research, it is possible that responses may be subject to 
the problems of (i) social-desirability bias, (ii) self-confirmation bias, and (iii) self-deception bias.
Third, we compared moral reasoning strategies across three cohorts of police officers (e.g., see, 
Figure 1), highlighting significant differences. However, showing that differences exist does not tell us 
why these differences exist. It is possible that any attitudes or behaviors reported by policing students 
that differ from those who have more field experience might be attenuated after they gain more real- 
world experience. Equally, if experienced police officers returned to formal education, these differ­
ences might be attenuated in the opposite direction, or these differences could reflect generational 
differences caused by extraneous factors. Future research should attempt to disentangle this, perhaps 
tracking students longitudinally, or by gathering retrospective reflections on how their ethical outlook 
has changed and why. Similarly, where we observed differences in moral reasoning strategies between 
those who do and do not have an undergraduate degree, we cannot speak to why this is the case. This 
would also need to be disentangled using a longitudinal design that would allow us to establish 
whether education causes differences in moral reasoning strategies, and how.
CONCLUSION
In summary, the findings of this study add backbone to arguments for policing becoming a graduate 
profession – at least for those who believe that a phronetic, virtue ethical take on professional moral 
decision-making offers a helpful counterbalance to more rigid and formalistic deontological strategies.
In the light of the ongoing work to revise the current Code (College of Policing, 2014), the present 
findings suggest that rather than making the Code more formulaic, it needs to be revised to reflect, 
more explicitly, virtue ethical considerations, not least the meta-virtue of professional phronesis. We 
also suggest that the Code be incorporated more visibly into practical and theoretical courses in police 
education and in CPD provisions for existing police officers.
The finding that aspiring and experienced police officers rely more on deontological decision- 
making strategies than any other previously studied U.K. professionals (apart from nurses) is not 
entirely surprising, given the law-like nature and history of policing as a profession. However, in light 
of the descriptions given by experienced police officers (in our subsequent interviews with them13) of 
how most actual decisions arrived at in the cut and thrust of real-life police work call for discretion and 
critical deliberation, there is every reason to highlight the salience of virtue ethical considerations for 
the moral practice of policing, and to help cultivate the characterological conditions in aspiring police 
officers that enable them to make the right choices, at the right times, for the right reasons.
