## 1. Introduction

Religious values shape the moral reasoning of more than 80\% of the world's population \cite{PewResearch2017}. From the Maqasid al-Shariah's preservation of life and intellect in Islamic jurisprudence to the Confucian virtue of ren (benevolence), from the Buddhist precept of ahimsa to Ubuntu's communal ethic of interconnectedness, religious and spiritual traditions provide the normative vocabularies through which billions of people evaluate right action, resolve ethical conflicts, and assign moral weight to competing goods. The field of AI alignment has recognized that large language models must be evaluated against human values \cite{Gabriel2020, Ji2024, Klingefjord2024}, and a growing literature proposes methods for eliciting, reconciling, and training on those values. Yet this literature treats "values" as a largely secular construct---operationalized through preference rankings, constitutional principles, or survey-derived cultural dimensions---while the religious traditions that generate the majority of the world's moral reasoning remain either invisible or flattened into demographic categories. The result is a paradox: dozens of benchmarks now claim to evaluate cultural alignment, religious bias, or values-sensitive behavior in LLMs, but no systematic framework exists for determining whether these instruments actually test religious values alignment, rather than religious knowledge recall or stereotype detection.

The stakes of this omission are consequential and growing. LLMs are increasingly deployed in contexts where religious values directly shape user expectations: pastoral counseling applications, halal certification advisory systems, religiously diverse content moderation, and educational tools serving faith-based institutions \cite{Bianchi2024}. The dominant meta-benchmarks that structure LLM evaluation---HELM \cite{Liang2023}, BIG-Bench \cite{Srivastava2023}, and their successors---treat religion in one of two ways: as a demographic variable for measuring bias (does the model stereotype Muslims?) or as a knowledge domain for measuring factual recall (can the model identify the Five Pillars of Islam?). Neither framing evaluates whether a model can reason *within* a religious ethical framework. The field can test whether an LLM knows that Muslims fast during Ramadan, but not whether it can apply the principle of maslaha (public interest) when Islamic legal principles conflict---the kind of theological reasoning that characterizes how religious traditions actually navigate moral complexity \cite{Alharbi2024}. This conflation of knowledge with reasoning, and of bias detection with values alignment, means that high benchmark scores may certify familiarity rather than competence, producing a false signal of religious values capability.

Three interrelated problems motivate this work. The first is **tradition asymmetry**. Our survey of 401 benchmark candidates across 14 religious and philosophical traditions reveals that 8 traditions---Judaism, Jainism, Taoism, Shinto, Ubuntu Philosophy, Indigenous Spirituality, Baha'i, and Zoroastrianism---have zero viable benchmarks that pass minimal gates for theological depth and data availability. Islam dominates the benchmark ecosystem, accounting for 28 of 46 gate-passing candidates and 7 of the Top 15, driven by a mature Quranic question-answering shared task community and well-resourced Arabic NLP infrastructure. The evaluation landscape can assess Islamic textual knowledge in considerable depth but cannot evaluate Dharmic, East Asian, African, or Indigenous values systems at all. The second problem is **knowledge--reasoning conflation**. The majority of religious values benchmarks test factual recall---"What does tradition X say about Y?"---rather than theological reasoning---"Given competing principles within tradition X, how should dilemma Y be resolved?" This distinction matters for deployment: a model that scores highly on Quranic verse identification may still produce theologically incoherent guidance when users seek counsel on novel ethical situations. The third problem is **structural epistemic injustice**. The 77\% of candidates (355 of 401) eliminated by the hard gate requiring publicly available evaluation data are not randomly distributed across traditions. The traditions with zero benchmarks---Indigenous, African, Dharmic minority, East Asian minority---are precisely those whose knowledge systems were developed through oral transmission, communal practice, and embodied ritual rather than through the textual, peer-reviewed, and digitally archived channels that benchmark creation depends upon \cite{Mohamed2020, Bilimoria2013}. When benchmark availability correlates with proximity to Western academic publishing infrastructure, the resulting gaps are not neutral absences but instances of what Mohamed et al. term "algorithmic coloniality"---the reproduction of epistemic hierarchies through technical systems \cite{Mohamed2020}. The WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) sampling bias documented by Henrich et al. \cite{Henrich2010} in psychology extends directly to AI evaluation: the traditions that are evaluable are those legible to the GitHub repository, HuggingFace dataset, and English-language journal pipeline \cite{Birhane2021, Carroll2020}.

We address these problems through a systematic meta-evaluation whose contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a **3-axis scoring framework** organized around Religious/Theological Depth (RD), Practical Ease (PE), and Citation Impact (CI), with seven meta-evaluation criteria that decompose the construct of "religious values alignment" into measurable components: tradition coverage, scriptural grounding, denominational awareness, pluralistic framing, and expert validation (Table 1). This framework draws on recent psychometric critiques of LLM benchmarking \cite{Gupta2025, Li2025} to impose construct validity requirements that the field currently lacks. Second, we present a **tradition coverage analysis of 401 candidates across 14 traditions**, revealing the Islam dominance pattern, the 8-tradition void, and the data availability bottleneck. We release a ranked Top 15 with composite scores and detailed profiles, providing the first curated inventory of religious values benchmarks suitable for implementation (Table 2). Third, we offer an **epistemic injustice diagnosis** that names the structural reasons for tradition gaps rather than treating them as incidental data limitations. The correlation between benchmark availability and Western academic infrastructure is itself a finding: it demonstrates that the current evaluation ecosystem cannot assess religious values alignment for the majority of the world's faith traditions, and that closing this gap requires not merely more benchmarks but fundamentally different approaches to benchmark construction---approaches that respect oral, communal, and practice-based knowledge transmission \cite{Carroll2020, Rao2025}.
