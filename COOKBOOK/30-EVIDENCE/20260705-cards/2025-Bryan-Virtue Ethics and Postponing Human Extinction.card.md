## 1. Gist
This short peer-reviewed essay in the AMA Journal of Ethics argues that virtue ethics — specifically the seven classical virtues (wisdom, justice, temperance, courage, faith, hope, love) — should supplement deontological and consequentialist approaches when reasoning about existential threats including rogue AI applications, climate change, and nuclear warfare. Drawing on Van Rensselaer Potter's original bioethics framework as a "science of survival," the author (a physician-bioethicist) contends that recent advances in neuroscience and social science provide new support for virtue ethics as a practical guide for decision-makers on "Spaceship Earth." The paper is a normative reflection piece rather than an empirical study, and its relevance to values-alignment benchmarking lies primarily in its articulation of virtue ethics as a complement to rule-based and outcome-based moral frameworks in high-stakes collective decision-making contexts.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
This is a normative/theoretical paper. Core claims:

1. Virtue ethics (character ethics) should supplement, not replace, deontological and consequentialist ethics in existential decision-making. Hursthouse's observation that virtue ethics "fails to provide action guidance when we come to hard cases or dilemmas" (p.3) is acknowledged, but the author contends virtues condition us to make wise choices in tough situations.

2. The seven classical virtues receive cross-cultural empirical support from the Values in Action (VIA) Classification Project (Peterson and Seligman, 2004), which identified 6 character strength clusters and 24 sub-virtues endorsed across most human cultures (p.3).

3. Neuroimaging research shows overlapping brain activation patterns for cardinal virtues (wisdom, justice, temperance, courage) and transcendent virtues (faith, hope, love), supporting the idea that transcendent virtues should inform cardinal virtues in decision-making for the common good (p.4).

4. The author advocates for some form of world federalism to manage existential threats, arguing inclusive democracy fails because people place self-interests above future generations (p.4).

5. "Artificial wisdom" — using AI to modulate emotional input in practical decision-making — is briefly mentioned as an open question (p.5).

## 6. Validity evidence
The paper cites the VIA Classification Project (Peterson and Seligman, 2004) as cross-cultural empirical validation for the seven classical virtues, noting that "throughout history, most cultures have endorsed 6 clusters of character strengths" corresponding to the virtues discussed (p.3). However, the author acknowledges VIA is "not without criticism" (p.3, citing Banicki, 2014). Neuroimaging studies cited support the neural reality of moral reasoning constructs but do not directly validate virtue ethics as a decision framework.

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- Very short essay (8 pages) with broad scope — arguments are suggestive rather than developed
- The advocacy for world federalism over inclusive democracy is normatively contested and underdeveloped
- Neuroscience citations (showing overlapping brain activation) do not establish that virtue ethics is the correct normative framework, only that virtues engage distinct but related neural circuits
- No engagement with non-Western virtue traditions beyond brief acknowledgment that VIA found cross-cultural support
- The author's definition of virtues, values, morals, and ethics as a hierarchy is presented as "the present author's simplification" (p.3)
- No discussion of how virtue ethics specifically applies to AI system design or alignment — the AI risk angle is mentioned only briefly
- "Artificial wisdom" is mentioned but not analyzed in any depth

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
1. **VIA taxonomy as a benchmark scaffold**: The 24 character strengths / 6 virtue clusters from Peterson and Seligman (2004) provide a culturally validated, empirically grounded vocabulary for constructing virtue-ethics benchmark items. This taxonomy could be used to systematically sample the virtue space in values-alignment evaluations.
2. **Transcendent + cardinal virtue integration**: The distinction between virtues oriented toward practical excellence (wisdom, justice, temperance, courage) and transcendent virtues (faith, hope, love/compassion) suggests that benchmark items should probe not only practical reasoning but also whether LLMs show appropriate care and long-term concern for others — not just rule-following or consequence-calculation.
3. **Existential-stakes framing**: The paper frames virtue ethics in terms of collective civilizational decision-making about extinction risks. Benchmark scenarios involving genuine high-stakes collective action (climate, AI safety, pandemics) would test whether LLM moral reasoning remains coherent at the civilizational scale, not just the individual case level.
4. **"Civilizational virtues and vices" concept** (citing Ord's The Precipice): Testing whether LLMs exhibit systematic strengths or weaknesses in reasoning about humanity's collective long-term flourishing, as opposed to individual cases, could be a distinctive benchmark dimension.
