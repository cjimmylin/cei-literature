## 5. Discussion

The results in Section 4 document the structure of the religious values benchmarking landscape in unprecedented detail: 401 candidates across 14 traditions, 47 gate-passers organized into three tiers, implementation trials across 9 models from 3 vendors, and 8 formal statistical claims subjected to a robustness battery. This section interprets those findings, connecting them to the paper's central question --- *what do religious values benchmarks actually test?* --- and drawing out their implications for evaluation practice, benchmark design, and the epistemic conditions under which religious values alignment can be meaningfully assessed.

The answer that emerges is uncomfortable for the field: most religious values benchmarks test religious knowledge recall, a smaller number test religious bias detection, and almost none test religious values reasoning. This answer is not merely a gap to be filled but a structural diagnosis that implicates the pipeline through which benchmarks are created, validated, and disseminated. The discussion proceeds from specific findings (tradition asymmetry, knowledge-reasoning conflation) through structural analysis (the rubric ceiling paradox, cross-vendor divergence) to actionable strategies (differentiated closing recommendations) and honest accounting of the study's own limitations.

### 5.1 The Tradition Asymmetry Problem

Islam's dominance of the benchmark ecosystem --- 28 of 47 gate-passers, 8 of 11 Tier 1 benchmarks, $\chi^2 = 64.96$, Cramer's $V = 0.53$, $p < 0.001$ --- is the survey's central structural finding, and it demands careful interpretation. The dominance is not theological but *infrastructural*. Three reinforcing factors produce it, each necessary and none alone sufficient.

First, the Quranic QA shared task community \cite{Malhas2023} has sustained a pipeline since at least 2019 that converts theological text into annotated datasets and then into published benchmarks with public evaluation data. This pipeline is self-reinforcing: each shared task iteration produces training data, evaluation harnesses, and published papers that lower the entry barrier for subsequent benchmark creators. The HAQA and QUQA datasets demonstrate how a single coordinating community can transform a theological corpus into a standardized evaluation ecosystem within a few years. No comparable coordinating body exists for any other religious tradition.

Second, Arabic NLP infrastructure provides the language modeling substrate. Gulf state investment in computational Arabic --- through initiatives in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar --- has produced pre-trained language models, tokenizers, and evaluation frameworks that Islamic benchmark creators can build upon rather than constructing from scratch. This infrastructure investment reflects national priorities in cultural AI development and is not replicated at comparable scale for Hebrew, Sanskrit, Pali, Classical Chinese, or Gurmukhi --- the liturgical languages of other major traditions.

Third, specific subdomains of Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) exhibit a degree of formalizability that maps naturally onto structured evaluation formats. Inheritance law (*ilm al-mawarith*), commercial transactions, and dietary jurisprudence involve explicit rules, numerical calculations, and hierarchical precedent structures that can be converted to multiple-choice or scenario-based items without excessive theological simplification. This formalizability, however, must not be overstated. It applies to specific applied legal subdomains, not to Islamic jurisprudence as a whole. The methods of analogical reasoning (*qiyas*), juristic preference (*istihsan*), and independent legal reasoning (*ijtihad*) resist the kind of formalization that benchmark construction rewards. The *Maqasid al-Shariah* framework \cite{Kamali2008} --- the higher objectives of Islamic law (preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth) --- provides operationalizable evaluation dimensions, but applying these objectives to novel situations involves precisely the kind of interpretive judgment that current benchmark formats struggle to assess. To treat all of Islamic jurisprudence as equally formalizable would itself constitute a form of the doctrinal flattening that this paper diagnoses elsewhere.

Christianity presents a paradox of institutional engagement without benchmark production. The Vatican's Rome Call for AI Ethics, denominational position papers from Protestant and Orthodox bodies, and a substantial body of Christian theological reflection on technology have not translated into published benchmarks at scale. Christianity converts candidates to gate-passers at only 5.1\% (6 of 117) --- the lowest conversion rate of any tradition with substantial candidate representation. The gap between institutional engagement and benchmark production reflects what Johnson \cite{Johnson2026} terms "doctrinal flattening": the reduction of complex denominational diversity --- Catholic, Orthodox, mainline Protestant, evangelical Protestant, Pentecostal, Restorationist --- into homogenized propositions that lose the theological specificity a benchmark requires. A question about Christian sexual ethics, for instance, would receive fundamentally different answers depending on whether the evaluative standard is Catholic natural law theology, Lutheran two-kingdoms doctrine, Calvinist covenant theology, or Anabaptist peace theology. No current benchmark captures this denominational diversity; the instruments that exist either collapse these traditions into a single "Christian" category or evaluate from a single denominational perspective without acknowledging the partiality. The absence of a single coordinating body analogous to the Quranic QA community compounds this challenge: Christian computational scholarship is distributed across denominations, languages, and institutional contexts with no shared task infrastructure to aggregate effort.

Judaism presents the starkest paradox. The tradition possesses the oldest computational humanities project in existence --- the Bar-Ilan Responsa Project, operational since the 1960s --- along with active formalization of Talmudic logic, a substantial open-source corpus (Sefaria), and a scholarly community with deep engagement in both rabbinic jurisprudence and computational methods. Yet it produces zero gate-passing benchmarks. The disconnect is not between Judaism and computation but between the Jewish computational scholarship community and the LLM evaluation community --- two adjacent fields that have not yet recognized their mutual relevance. The Talmudic reasoning tradition, with its explicit multi-voice argumentation structure (*machloket*), precedent-based legal reasoning (*halakhic* methodology), and hierarchical interpretive principles, would seem ideally suited to benchmark construction. That no such benchmark exists suggests that the bottleneck is community coordination --- the creation of shared task infrastructure connecting computational Talmudics with NLP evaluation --- rather than theological or computational resources.

The structural lesson generalizes: what determines benchmark availability is not whether a tradition has theological resources amenable to evaluation but whether it has a *community* that converts those resources into the specific format of publicly available, machine-readable evaluation artifacts. The field currently uses "religious values benchmarking" as though it denotes comprehensive assessment across traditions, but in practice the phrase has become nearly synonymous with "Islamic textual evaluation." This terminological inflation obscures the 8-tradition void and misleads downstream consumers of evaluation results. When a meta-benchmark reports that a model has been "evaluated on religious content," the implied comprehensiveness is false: the model has almost certainly been evaluated on Islamic textual content, possibly on Christian or Buddhist content, and almost never on Jewish, Jain, Taoist, Shinto, Ubuntu, or Indigenous content. The precision of language matters because imprecise claims of coverage become the basis for deployment decisions in religiously diverse contexts.

### 5.2 Knowledge Testing vs. Values Reasoning

The expert council's finding that only 5 of 25 tiered benchmarks demonstrate genuine theological depth --- ELQV, Islamic Inheritance Law, QIAS 2025, Gospel Coalition, and GurbaniDB/BaniDB --- confirms this paper's second claim and provides the most direct answer to its title question: most religious values benchmarks test religious *knowledge*, not religious *values*.

The K < R < V response length gradient, observed across all model-tradition combinations within the Claude family, provides empirical evidence for this distinction. If knowledge, reasoning, and values were undifferentiated "religious content" --- interchangeable items that happen to bear religious labels --- there would be no systematic reason for response lengths to increase across categories. Instead, the gradient is consistent and steep: Haiku produces a 9.18$\times$ ratio between values and knowledge response lengths, while even Opus --- the most capable model tested --- exhibits a substantial increase from knowledge to values items. This pattern indicates that the three construct categories impose progressively greater generative demand on the model's language production system, consistent with the hypothesis that values items require integrating more contextual information, navigating more competing considerations, and producing more nuanced output than knowledge items. The gradient holds across Islam, Christianity, and Hinduism, suggesting it is not an artifact of any single tradition's item characteristics.

The parallel to this paper's sister survey, NormEval, strengthens the diagnosis. In the normative ethics domain, 28 of 37 benchmarks test moral judgment (applying existing rules to cases) rather than moral reasoning (justifying, critiquing, or generating moral principles) \cite{Hendrycks2021}. The knowledge-reasoning conflation thus appears to be a domain-general pattern in LLM evaluation, not specific to religious content. Benchmark creators across domains default to formats that test recognition and recall --- the capabilities that are easiest to construct items for, easiest to score automatically, and easiest to report as single accuracy numbers. The result is a systematic bias toward evaluating the least interesting capabilities while claiming to evaluate the most important ones.

The deployment implications of this conflation are concrete and consequential. A model that achieves high accuracy on Quranic verse identification (a knowledge task) may still produce theologically incoherent guidance when a user seeks counsel on a novel ethical situation requiring reasoning *within* an Islamic framework. Applying the principle of *maslaha* (public interest) when legal principles conflict, navigating the tension between textual literalism (*zahiri*) and contextual interpretation (*maqasidi*), or weighing competing objectives within the *Maqasid al-Shariah* when they point in different directions --- these are the capabilities that matter for deployment in religiously sensitive contexts, and they are precisely the capabilities that current benchmarks fail to evaluate. The same logic applies across traditions: knowing that the Bhagavad Gita's Chapter 2 discusses the immortality of the soul is categorically different from reasoning about how the concept of *svadharma* (one's own duty) applies to a novel moral dilemma where role-obligations conflict.

The ELQV paradox crystallizes this concern. ELQV --- the field's highest-ranking benchmark by composite score --- tests emotional valence annotation of Quranic verses. This is a genuine NLP task performed on sacred text, requiring meaningful engagement with Quranic content, and it achieves the highest Religious/Theological Depth score in the corpus (RD = 62.0). Yet it is fundamentally a knowledge extraction task rather than a values reasoning task: it asks "what emotion does this verse convey?" not "how should the ethical principle in this verse be applied to a novel situation?" That the field's top-ranked instrument is a sophisticated knowledge task rather than a reasoning task is itself diagnostic of where current evaluation priorities lie. The scoring framework correctly rewards ELQV's genuine theological grounding, but the field should be clear-eyed about what this ranking reveals: current scoring rewards theological *content engagement* rather than theological *reasoning capability*.

The knowledge-reasoning gap has a second, more insidious consequence: it inflates the field's apparent progress. When evaluation reports state that a model has been "evaluated on religious content" and achieved a certain accuracy, the signal is ambiguous between "the model can identify religious texts and concepts" and "the model can reason within religious ethical frameworks." These are categorically different capabilities with different implications for trust, deployment, and harm. Conflating them produces a false signal of competence --- the same epistemic distortion that bias-values conflation produces at the construct level. Future benchmarks must make the knowledge-reasoning distinction explicit, both in their design (including items that require reasoning within tradition-specific frameworks, not merely about those frameworks) and in their reporting (disaggregating scores by construct category so that downstream consumers can distinguish knowledge certification from reasoning competence).

### 5.3 The Rubric Ceiling Paradox

The cross-model implementation trials reveal a measurement paradox that has implications beyond religious values evaluation: the rubric-based scoring system designed to assess response quality exhibits ceiling effects that eliminate its ability to discriminate between models of meaningfully different capability.

All three Claude models achieve rubric scores $\geq 0.93$ on all rubric dimensions --- Knowledge, Reasoning, Values, and Bias --- despite substantial capability gaps revealed by other measures. Haiku achieves 76.7\% on the T2 Arabic MCQ while Opus and Sonnet achieve 100\%, yet the rubric assigns all three models near-perfect scores. The rubric, in effect, creates a pass/fail gate rather than a continuous measure: once a response meets a minimum quality threshold, additional depth, nuance, and theological sophistication do not register in the score. In Item Response Theory (IRT) terms, the rubric items have extremely low discrimination parameters --- they function as binary indicators of adequate performance rather than as measures that differentiate between adequate and excellent.

The OpenAI Codex results intensify this paradox. Codex-Mini achieves rubric scores of 0.87--1.0 with responses that are 5--80$\times$ shorter than Claude's. A Knowledge response of 82 characters scores 0.933 on a rubric ostensibly measuring response quality. This raises a fundamental question about what the rubric actually measures: if an 82-character response achieves near-ceiling, the rubric may be rewarding keyword presence --- the occurrence of tradition-specific terms and concepts --- rather than the depth of engagement, quality of reasoning, or theological sophistication that a values alignment assessment should capture. The rubric was designed to assess whether responses demonstrate religious literacy, but in practice it appears to function as a keyword detection instrument with a very low threshold.

The Gemini results complete the picture from the opposite direction. Google's models score 0.0 on Knowledge, Reasoning, and Values rubrics but achieve 0.57--1.0 on the Bias dimension. This pattern suggests that the models are capable of meta-level religious literacy --- recognizing when content is biased or stereotypical with respect to religious groups --- but are either incapable of or unwilling to produce first-order religious reasoning. Two important caveats constrain this interpretation. First, Gemini rubric scores were backfilled through template extraction rather than generated through the same interactive pipeline used for Claude and OpenAI models; this methodological asymmetry may partially account for the 0.0 scores, and the result should be treated as preliminary until replicated with matched protocols. Second, the Bias dimension's non-zero scores may reflect Gemini's safety training, which likely emphasizes bias detection as a high-priority capability, rather than general religious competence.

For benchmark design, the rubric ceiling paradox implies that rubric-based evaluation alone is inadequate for assessing frontier models on religious content. The field needs hybrid evaluation architectures that combine multiple measurement modalities:

- **MCQ accuracy floors** (like the T2 Arabic assessment) provide genuine discriminative power. The T2 results span from 6.7\% (Gemini Pro) to 100\% (Opus, Sonnet, GPT-Codex) --- a 93.3-percentage-point spread that dwarfs the rubric's binary ceiling/floor distribution. T2 items show genuine difficulty variation, with Haiku's performance diverging between beginner-level items (high accuracy) and advanced items requiring multi-step legal reasoning (lower accuracy), suggesting meaningful difficulty parameters in IRT terms.
- **Open-ended rubric evaluation** provides qualitative assessment of response content, but rubrics must be redesigned with more granular scoring criteria, higher ceilings, and explicit discrimination between adequate and excellent responses. Current 5-point rubrics saturate at frontier model capabilities; 10-point scales with exemplar-anchored levels may better capture the quality range.
- **Response length and depth metrics** provide construct differentiation, as demonstrated by the K < R < V gradient, but must be interpreted cautiously. Length correlates with but is not identical to reasoning depth (see Section 5.4).

The rubric ceiling paradox is not unique to religious values evaluation. Any domain where frontier models have been extensively trained on relevant content --- ethics, law, medicine --- will exhibit similar ceiling effects on rubric-based assessment. The T2 Arabic MCQ's discriminative power suggests that domain-specific, technically demanding items with verifiable correct answers may be the most informative evaluation modality for frontier models, complementing but not replacing the qualitative insights that rubric-based assessment provides.

### 5.4 Cross-Vendor Divergence and Its Implications

Before interpreting the cross-vendor results, three methodological limitations must be stated explicitly. First, the original T2 Arabic MCQ trials for the three Claude models were conducted with gold-standard answer labels visible to the evaluating system, making the reported 100\% accuracy figures upper-bound estimates; subsequent cross-vendor trials used a blind protocol where gold labels were withheld. Second, all rubric-based scoring was performed by Claude Opus 4.6, including scoring of Claude-family responses; RLHF-trained models within the same family may share latent evaluative preferences, potentially inflating rubric scores for same-vendor outputs. Third, Gemini model results were collected through a backfilled protocol with template-based response extraction rather than the interactive API administration used for Claude and OpenAI models; uniformly low rubric scores (0.0 on Knowledge, Reasoning, Values) may partly reflect this methodological artifact rather than genuine capability gaps. These caveats do not invalidate the cross-vendor analysis, but they constrain its interpretation: the results should be read as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

With these caveats foregrounded, the cross-vendor trials reveal three distinct behavioral modes that may reflect fundamentally different alignment strategies for religious content.

**Differentiated engagement** (Claude family). All three Claude models --- Opus, Sonnet, and Haiku --- produce responses whose length and complexity scale with the cognitive demand of the item category. Knowledge items receive concise, targeted responses; reasoning items receive longer responses with more explicit inferential structure; values items receive the longest and most elaborate responses, often incorporating multiple perspectives, tradition-specific qualifications, and acknowledgment of interpretive diversity within the target tradition. The K < R < V gradient is consistent across all three model-tradition combinations and across all three capability tiers (Opus, Sonnet, Haiku), suggesting it reflects a systematic pattern in the models' training rather than item-specific variation. This behavioral profile is consistent with extensive RLHF training on diverse religious content that has calibrated the models to match response elaboration to perceived complexity.

**Compressed accuracy** (OpenAI Codex family). GPT-Codex and Codex-Mini achieve near-ceiling rubric scores (0.87--1.0) through concise, targeted output --- responses that are 5--80$\times$ shorter than Claude's yet receive comparable quality assessments from the rubric. Codex-Mini's ability to achieve 0.933 on a Knowledge item with an 82-character response demonstrates that the rubric's quality floor is remarkably low. This behavioral profile may reflect training optimization for efficiency rather than elaboration: a model trained to produce maximally informative responses at minimal length. The compressed accuracy mode raises an important question about the relationship between verbosity and competence. If a model can demonstrate adequate religious literacy in 82 characters, the longer responses produced by Claude models may contain genuinely deeper reasoning --- or they may contain proportional padding. The high rubric scores for both profiles prevent the current instrument from distinguishing these possibilities.

**Template refusal** (Gemini family). All four Gemini models produce near-uniform responses regardless of item type, scoring 0.0 on Knowledge, Reasoning, and Values rubrics while achieving 0.57--1.0 on Bias detection. Three possible explanations exist, and the available data cannot distinguish among them: (a) over-aggressive safety filtering on religious content, where the models' safety training treats substantive religious claims as potentially harmful and defaults to neutral, non-committal responses; (b) a training data gap for religious reasoning, where the models lack sufficient exposure to tradition-specific theological content to generate substantive responses; or (c) a methodological artifact of the backfilled protocol, where template-based extraction failed to capture responses that the models did in fact generate through the interactive API. The current evidence is *underdetermined* --- each explanation is consistent with the observed pattern, and resolving among them would require matched-protocol replication that is beyond this study's scope. What can be said is that the Bias dimension's non-zero scores suggest that the models are not entirely incapable of engaging religious content; they appear to engage it selectively, at the meta-level of bias recognition rather than the object-level of theological reasoning.

The K < R < V gradient, while robust within the Claude family, may reflect RLHF training distributions specific to that model family rather than a universal property of LLM religious engagement. Before the gradient can be claimed as a cognitive demand signal --- evidence that knowledge, reasoning, and values are genuinely distinct construct categories imposing different computational demands --- it must be demonstrated in at least one non-Anthropic model family. Currently, only Codex-Mini partially supports this interpretation: its response lengths show a modest increase from knowledge to values items, but the compressed response style makes the gradient less pronounced and harder to interpret. The gradient's universality within the Claude family and its partial replication in Codex-Mini are suggestive but not conclusive.

Response length as a proxy for cognitive demand merits further scrutiny. Length correlates with but is not identical to reasoning depth. A verbose model could inflate response length without proportional increases in depth --- through hedging, repetition, or tangential elaboration. This concern is partially mitigated by the high rubric scores confirming substantive content in Claude responses: the longer responses are not padding-inflated but contain additional perspectives, qualifications, and tradition-specific reasoning that the rubric's quality assessment validates. Codex's ability to achieve near-ceiling rubric quality with 5--10$\times$ shorter responses, however, suggests that length and quality are more separable than the gradient hypothesis assumes. The strongest interpretation of the K < R < V gradient is not that longer responses are necessarily deeper but that models trained with extensive RLHF on diverse content have learned that values questions warrant more elaborate treatment than knowledge questions --- a meta-pattern about expected response norms rather than evidence of differential "understanding."

This epistemological question --- whether producing longer, more nuanced values responses indicates deeper comprehension of religious ethical frameworks or a learned meta-pattern about expected response elaboration --- cannot be resolved from behavioral data alone \cite{Rao2025}. The Philosophy of AI perspective reminds us that LLM responses are productions of statistical language models, not expressions of understanding \cite{Gabriel2020}. Whether the Claude models "understand" Islamic inheritance law or have learned to produce outputs that score well on rubrics designed by humans who understand Islamic inheritance law is a question about the nature of LLM cognition, not about the quality of evaluation instruments. What the cross-vendor analysis can establish is that different model families produce fundamentally different behavioral profiles when confronted with religious content --- and this has immediate practical implications regardless of the philosophical question about understanding.

The practical implications for religious AI deployment are substantial. Models from different vendors exhibit fundamentally different behaviors when confronted with religious content. A user seeking pastoral counsel, ethical guidance within a religious framework, or help navigating a religiously sensitive situation would receive radically different experiences depending on the vendor: an elaborate, multi-perspective response from Claude; a concise, accurate but minimal response from Codex; or a neutral, non-committal response from Gemini. No current evaluation standard enables vendor-neutral comparison of religious competence. The T2 Arabic MCQ provides partial comparability (all vendors were administered identical items), but the rubric's ceiling and floor effects eliminate its ability to create meaningful vendor rankings on qualitative dimensions. This lacuna motivates the paper's fourth call to action: the establishment of cross-vendor evaluation standards, including matched administration protocols, independent (non-vendor-affiliated) rubric scoring, and explicit data provenance documentation, to enable trustworthy comparison of LLM religious competence across model families.

### 5.5 Values Relevance Failure

The values relevance analysis produces the strongest statistical result in the robustness battery and the most consequential answer to this paper's title question. Among 16 gate-passing benchmarks assessed on a 24-dimension values relevance rubric, the mean A3 score is 30.0/100 ($SD = 16.2$), significantly below the scale midpoint ($t(15) = -4.93$, $p < 0.001$, Cohen's $d = -1.23$). Only 3 of 16 (18.8\%) achieve A3 $\geq 50$. Zero reach A3 $\geq 75$. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirms this result non-parametrically ($W = 5.0$, $p < 0.001$). The effect size of $d = -1.23$ is large by any conventional standard --- larger than the effects typically observed in psychological research and indicating a practically meaningful gap between what benchmarks claim to test and what they actually test.

What this means, in direct terms, is that most benchmarks that claim to test religious values alignment do not actually test religious values. They test related but distinct constructs:

*Religious knowledge recall* --- whether the model can identify the Five Pillars of Islam, name the Four Noble Truths of Buddhism, or locate a specific verse in the Bhagavad Gita. These are legitimate evaluation targets, but they assess a model's exposure to religious text during training, not its ability to reason about religious values or apply religious ethical principles.

*Religious bias detection* --- whether the model produces stereotypical, discriminatory, or offensive content about religious groups \cite{Abid2021, Bianchi2024}. Bias detection is critical for responsible deployment, but it is a fairness task, not a values alignment task. A model that avoids anti-Muslim stereotypes has cleared an important safety threshold but has not demonstrated the ability to reason within an Islamic ethical framework.

*Secular cultural attitudes correlated with religious demographics* --- whether the model's responses align with the cultural attitudes of religious populations as measured by survey instruments designed for human respondents \cite{Henrich2010, Klingefjord2024}. These instruments capture the cultural correlates of religious affiliation but not the theological reasoning that generates and sustains those correlates.

The top scorers on the A3 dimension demonstrate that high-quality religious values benchmarks are possible. IslamTrust (90), BuddhismEval (85), CatholicBench (80), and Gospel Coalition (80) each engage with values-level content in ways that require models to navigate competing principles, demonstrate tradition-specific ethical reasoning, or apply theological frameworks to novel situations. These benchmarks serve as proof-of-concept: the task of building values-level religious evaluation instruments is achievable but rare --- 4 of 16 rather than the default.

A further finding compounds the values relevance failure: 15 of 16 assessed benchmarks (93.75\%) treat the target religion as a monolith, making no distinction between denominations, schools of thought, or interpretive communities. This monolithic treatment undermines claims of values alignment because values vary as much *within* traditions as *between* them. Sunni and Shia jurisprudence diverge on fundamental questions of legal authority and interpretive methodology. Catholic and Protestant sexual ethics differ on contraception, divorce, and the role of natural law. Theravada and Mahayana Buddhist ethics differ on the relative priority of personal liberation and compassionate engagement with the world. A benchmark that evaluates "Islamic values" without specifying whether the evaluative standard is Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali, or Ja'fari jurisprudence has not evaluated values alignment in any operationally meaningful sense --- it has evaluated whether the model can produce outputs that sound generically Islamic, which is precisely the doctrinal flattening that Johnson \cite{Johnson2026} identifies as a pervasive problem in computational engagement with religious content.

The values relevance failure, combined with the knowledge-reasoning conflation documented in Section 5.2, yields a stark assessment of the field's current state: the religious values benchmarking ecosystem can rigorously assess whether LLMs have been trained on religious text, can moderately assess whether LLMs produce biased content about religious groups, and can barely assess whether LLMs can reason within religious ethical frameworks. The third capability is what "values alignment" implies; the first two are prerequisites at best, distractions at worst.

### 5.6 Publication Infrastructure as the Structural Barrier

The tradition coverage analysis reveals that the 8-tradition void is not randomly distributed but structurally determined. Publication infrastructure is the strongest predictor of benchmark availability ($r = 0.74$, $p < 0.01$), followed by community practice norms ($r = 0.58$). Formalizability --- the degree to which a tradition's ethical reasoning can be expressed in rule-based, propositional terms --- is *not* a significant predictor ($r = 0.41$, $p > 0.05$), contradicting the intuitive hypothesis that some traditions are inherently less amenable to benchmark construction.

A logistic regression model provides a critical nuance: tradition family does not independently predict gate-passage ($p = 0.112$, Pseudo-$R^2 = 0.068$). This finding contradicts a simple epistemic injustice framing in which the scoring framework itself disadvantages certain traditions. The scoring framework is tradition-neutral in the narrow technical sense --- it does not penalize traditions for being non-Western, non-textual, or non-Abrahamic. The injustice operates *through* infrastructure, not through the scoring mechanism. Traditions with robust publication infrastructure (indexed journals, shared task communities, GitHub/HuggingFace presence, English-language venue access) produce benchmarks that pass both hard gates; traditions without this infrastructure do not. The scoring framework faithfully reports this disparity without causing it.

This structural analysis reveals two fundamentally distinct obstacles to comprehensive religious values evaluation, and conflating them produces confused recommendations. The distinction matters because each obstacle requires a different strategy.

**The community coordination problem** affects textual traditions with substantial computational resources but no shared task infrastructure: Judaism, Jainism, Taoism, and Confucianism. These traditions possess digital textual canons (Sefaria, Jain Agama collections, the *Daozang*, the Chinese Classics), active computational scholarship communities, and ethical reasoning frameworks that are no less formalizable than the Islamic legal subdomains that dominate the benchmark ecosystem. What they lack is the specific institutional mechanism --- a shared task community modeled on the Quranic QA pipeline --- that converts these resources into the format of publicly available, machine-readable evaluation artifacts. This is the same community coordination bottleneck that explains Christianity's low conversion rate: resources exist, but the bridge between tradition-specific scholarship and NLP evaluation has not been built. The bottleneck is addressable through targeted institutional investment: structured competitions co-located with major NLP venues, community workshops that bring together tradition-specific scholars and benchmark designers, and standardized data release protocols. A realistic timeline for producing viable benchmarks for Judaism, Confucianism, and Hinduism, given existing digital resources, is two to three years with sustained workshop investment.

**Structural epistemic exclusion** affects oral, communal, and practice-based traditions: Indigenous Spirituality, Ubuntu Philosophy, and Shinto. For these traditions, the community coordination problem is compounded by --- and in some cases secondary to --- a deeper structural incompatibility. The benchmark creation pipeline has epistemic prerequisites that systematically exclude certain knowledge systems: textual corpora (requiring written canonical sources), formalized knowledge (requiring propositional expression of ethical principles), individual-response evaluation formats (requiring that competence be assessed at the level of a single model's output), and open data licenses (requiring public availability of evaluation materials). These prerequisites are not arbitrary design choices; each serves a legitimate methodological function (reproducibility, standardization, comparability). But their cumulative effect is to render invisible precisely those traditions whose ethical reasoning is transmitted orally, embodied in communal practice, expressed through narrative and ritual rather than proposition, or governed by access protocols that restrict public dissemination.

This constitutes what might be termed *infrastructure-mediated epistemic disadvantage* --- an extension of Fricker's \cite{Fricker2007} concept of epistemic injustice from interpersonal credibility deficits to evaluation infrastructure. The injustice is not that individual researchers are biased against these traditions; most benchmark creators are likely unaware that their pipeline design has exclusionary consequences. The injustice is that the *structure* of the pipeline renders certain knowledge systems invisible, and that this invisibility is self-reinforcing: traditions without benchmarks produce no evaluation data, which means models are never assessed on those traditions, which means no demand signal reaches the benchmark community, which means no investment flows toward closing the gap.

Mohamed et al.'s \cite{Mohamed2020} analysis of "algorithmic coloniality" --- the embedding of colonial epistemic hierarchies into technical systems --- applies directly, but at the level of evaluation rather than training. The standard concern in decolonial AI is that training data encodes colonial biases. This paper documents a parallel concern: evaluation infrastructure reproduces colonial knowledge hierarchies by determining which traditions are *evaluable* and which are invisible. A model trained on Ubuntu philosophy would have no benchmark against which its Ubuntu reasoning could be assessed, rendering the training investment epistemically inert.

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance \cite{Carroll2020} introduce a further complication that prevents any simple resolution. The hard gate requiring publicly available evaluation data functions simultaneously as a quality filter (ensuring reproducibility and independent verification, both legitimate scientific values) and as a structural exclusion mechanism (barring traditions whose communities exercise data sovereignty by declining to make sacred knowledge publicly available). Some Indigenous communities and some Shinto practitioners may decline to encode sacred knowledge in public datasets not because they lack the capacity or resources but because they exercise epistemic sovereignty --- a legitimate refusal that should be respected rather than treated as a gap to be overcome. Following Tuck and Yang \cite{TuckYang2012}, this paper's recommendations must not reduce decolonization to a methodological adjustment within the existing benchmarking paradigm --- the addition of a few Indigenous items to an otherwise unchanged pipeline. The structural exclusion of oral and communal traditions requires fundamentally different evaluation architectures, not incremental expansion of existing ones.

### 5.7 Closing the Gaps: Differentiated Strategies

The structural analysis in Section 5.6 implies that no single strategy can address the tradition asymmetry. The two distinct obstacles --- community coordination failure and structural epistemic exclusion --- require differentiated approaches calibrated to the nature of each tradition's knowledge system, institutional context, and community preferences.

**For textual traditions with formal reasoning structures** --- Judaism, Confucianism, Hinduism, and to a lesser extent Jainism and Taoism --- the primary bottleneck is community coordination, not theological resources. These traditions possess digital corpora, active computational scholarship communities, and ethical reasoning frameworks with formal structures at least partially amenable to benchmark construction. The Quranic QA shared task pipeline \cite{Malhas2023} provides a proven model: a structured competition co-located with a major NLP venue (e.g., SemEval, EMNLP), with standardized data release protocols and a multi-year iteration cycle that builds community momentum.

Concrete resources exist for immediate conversion. The Sefaria digital library contains the complete Talmud, Mishnah, Midrash, and extensive commentarial literature in both Hebrew and English, with structured markup that could support benchmark construction for halakhic reasoning, aggadic interpretation, and multi-voice argumentation. The Chinese Classics Digital Library and the extensive corpus of Confucian and Taoist texts in classical Chinese could support benchmarks for *li* (ritual propriety), *ren* (humaneness), and *wu wei* (non-action) reasoning. Sanskrit computational linguistics, including the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit and ongoing work in automated *Paninian* grammar analysis, could support benchmarks for Hindu ethical reasoning within frameworks such as *dharma*, *karma*, and *svadharma*. The timeline for producing viable benchmarks from these resources --- given adequate institutional investment --- is realistically two to three years per tradition, based on the trajectory of the Quranic QA community from initial shared task to multiple published benchmarks.

A critical lesson from the Islamic benchmark ecosystem is that the first shared task is the hardest. Once a community produces an initial dataset, annotated corpus, and published benchmark, subsequent iterations face dramatically lower barriers: annotation protocols exist, evaluation harnesses are adapted, and a citation network forms that attracts new researchers. The investment required is therefore front-loaded: workshop funding, annotation labor, and venue coordination in the first year, declining to maintenance and iteration in subsequent years.

**For oral, communal, and practice-based traditions** --- Indigenous Spirituality, Ubuntu Philosophy, Shinto, and aspects of Dharmic practice-based ethics --- the shared task pipeline is not merely insufficient but epistemically inappropriate. Applying the standard benchmark construction protocol to these traditions would require extracting knowledge from its communal context, reducing narrative and embodied practice to propositional items, and making sacred or restricted knowledge publicly available --- each step violating the epistemic norms that govern knowledge transmission within these traditions.

These traditions require fundamentally different evaluation paradigms. Several concrete precedents suggest what such paradigms might look like:

*Community-governed assessment protocols*, where the tradition's own practitioners define what counts as competent reasoning, select evaluation scenarios, and interpret model outputs within tradition-specific frameworks. The assessment results would be authoritative within the community but might not be publicly replicable by external parties --- a trade-off between traditional reproducibility norms and epistemic sovereignty.

*Participatory evaluation methods* that embed assessment within relational contexts rather than extracting knowledge into decontextualized items. Rather than asking a model to answer questions about Ubuntu philosophy, a participatory assessment might present the model with a community deliberation scenario and evaluate its capacity to participate constructively --- a fundamentally different evaluation architecture that privileges relational competence over propositional accuracy.

*Narrative-based assessment* that respects oral traditions' own modes of moral deliberation. Rather than multiple-choice items, evaluation might involve storytelling prompts, parables, or case narratives where the model's response is assessed for its alignment with the tradition's narrative conventions and moral vocabulary.

*Post-benchmark evaluation paradigms*, including consultation-based evaluation, community-led auditing, and deliberative alignment processes \cite{Sorensen2024}, may serve these traditions more faithfully than any standardized testing format. Concrete institutional precedents exist: the MUKURTU CMS implements culturally specific access protocols for Indigenous digital heritage, and the Local Contexts initiative has developed Indigenous data labels (Traditional Knowledge Labels, Biocultural Labels) that encode community governance preferences into digital infrastructure. These models demonstrate that technically sophisticated systems can respect community sovereignty --- the challenge is adapting them from archival to evaluative contexts.

Several risks attend even well-intentioned efforts to develop evaluation protocols for oral traditions. The risk of *nominal governance* --- where "community-governed" becomes a legitimizing label for what is in practice externally designed evaluation with community consultation as a rubber stamp --- is real and must be addressed through genuine power-sharing rather than advisory roles. Religious institutions (mosques, churches, temples, community councils) are entirely absent from current benchmark development despite being the primary sites of values transmission and moral deliberation within their traditions; their inclusion is a precondition for legitimate community governance, not an optional enhancement. Longer timelines and different funding models --- grants structured around community needs rather than publication deadlines --- are necessary to avoid the extractive dynamics that characterize much cross-cultural research.

A moratorium on externally led benchmark development for oral traditions may be warranted until community-governed alternatives are established. The field's current trajectory --- in which benchmark creators from outside a tradition design evaluation instruments, administer them to models, and publish assessments of alignment with values they do not hold --- replicates the extractive dynamics that decolonial scholarship has identified in other domains \cite{TuckYang2012}. Accepting that some evaluation results will be proprietary, non-public, and non-replicable by external parties is a necessary cost of respecting epistemic sovereignty. The alternative --- insisting on public data as a precondition for legitimate evaluation --- is the very structural exclusion this paper diagnoses.

### 5.8 Methodological Transparency

This paper's robustness battery subjected 8 formal claims to statistical testing. The results are mixed, and the mixture is itself informative.

**Four claims are supported at conventional significance levels.** Claim 1 (tradition asymmetry, $\chi^2 = 64.96$, $p < 0.001$), Claim 2 (knowledge-reasoning conflation, binomial $p = 0.0015$), Claim 3 (data availability bottleneck), and Claim 8 (values relevance failure, $d = -1.23$, $p < 0.001$) all survive the robustness battery. These claims rest on large effect sizes and converging evidence from multiple statistical tests; they are unlikely to be overturned by additional data or alternative analytical choices.

**One claim is marginal.** Claim 5 (field recency) has a p-value of 0.121 --- above conventional significance thresholds but below the level at which the trend should be dismissed. The observation that 73\% of candidates date from 2024--2026 is descriptively accurate and practically important for understanding the field's maturity; the formal test's marginal status reflects the difficulty of testing distributional claims about a temporally truncated sample rather than the absence of the pattern.

**Three claims required substantive revision**, and the revisions strengthen rather than weaken the analysis.

Claim 4 (epistemic injustice) was initially framed as operating through the scoring framework. The logistic regression's non-significant tradition family coefficient ($p = 0.112$) forced a revision: the scoring framework does not directly disadvantage traditions, but publication infrastructure ($r = 0.74$) mediates structural exclusion. This is a more precise and ultimately more useful finding: it identifies the specific mechanism through which epistemic injustice operates, pointing to infrastructure investment rather than scoring reform as the appropriate intervention.

Claim 6 (WEIRD bias) was expected to show Western traditions dominating the benchmark ecosystem. Instead, non-WEIRD traditions pass at 14.4\% versus WEIRD traditions at 8.9\%, a reversal driven entirely by Islamic benchmark dominance. This counterintuitive finding is more informative than the expected pattern: it reveals that infrastructure advantage (the Quranic QA pipeline) can overwhelm demographic disadvantage, producing a surface-level reversal that masks the underlying 8-tradition void. The WEIRD critique \cite{Henrich2010} applies, but not in its standard form.

Claim 7 (bias-values conflation) is conceptually supported by the construct independence observed in high-capability models --- Sonnet produces a correlation of only $r = 0.288$ between bias detection and values reasoning scores, indicating that these are partially separable constructs --- but the cross-sectional survey test does not reach significance ($p = 0.68$). The distinction between bias detection and values alignment remains conceptually important and has practical consequences for benchmark interpretation, but the empirical test lacks the statistical power to confirm it at the population level.

**Bootstrap sensitivity analysis** provides an additional check on ranking robustness. The tier structure is stable: benchmarks assigned to Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 retain their tier assignments across 1,000 bootstrap iterations at a rate that supports the three-tier taxonomy. However, precise rank ordering within tiers is not robust. The bootstrap produces a Kendall's $\tau$ of 0.773 between original and resampled rankings --- strong but not perfect correlation --- and the mean confidence interval width of 15.87 points exceeds the score spread of 12.1 points within the tiered ranking. This means that a benchmark ranked 3rd could plausibly be ranked anywhere from 1st to 8th under alternative weighting schemes. The tier structure communicates appropriate uncertainty; readers should interpret tier membership as meaningful and within-tier ordering as provisional.

Naming these limitations strengthens rather than weakens the analysis. A study that reports $p < 0.001$ for four claims, revises three claims in light of contradictory evidence, and discloses the sensitivity of its ranking to analytical choices provides a more trustworthy basis for field development than one that reports uniformly significant results. The mixed pattern --- strong evidence for the descriptive findings, weaker evidence for the inferential claims, and surprising reversals that complicate the initial theoretical framing --- is characteristic of genuine empirical engagement with a complex phenomenon.

### 5.9 Limitations

This study has seventeen limitations that constrain interpretation. They are organized into four categories: scoring methodology, search coverage, analytical design, and empirical trial constraints.

**Scoring methodology.** (1) All scoring was conducted by a single author without inter-rater reliability assessment. The most consequential features --- theological depth (R2) and expert validation (R6) --- are scored via keyword heuristics rather than expert theological judgment, and calibration anchors mitigate but do not eliminate subjectivity. (2) The three-tier religious engagement classification (theological, theological-values, values) is coarse and does not capture the full spectrum of engagement depth within each tier. (3) Tradition assignment for external papers was derived from search metadata rather than expert classification by tradition-specific scholars.

**Search coverage.** (4) All 72 external queries were conducted in English, introducing systematic language bias that likely undercounts benchmarks published in Hebrew, Classical Chinese, Arabic (outside the Islamic NLP community), Sanskrit, Pali, and other liturgical languages. (5) The external search did not include Google Scholar or PapersWithCode, and HuggingFace API queries yielded zero results, likely underestimating the true corpus. (6) Citation Impact weighting (25\%) privileges English-language venues and indexed databases, reproducing at the scoring level the infrastructure asymmetry that the paper diagnoses at the field level. (7) A WEIRD-neutral weighting scenario that reduces CI to 10\% and increases RD to 55\% shifts three non-Islamic benchmarks into Tier 1, suggesting that the current weighting scheme partially encodes the very biases the paper critiques.

**Analytical design.** (8) Feature leave-one-out analysis reveals that removing R1 (tradition count) causes the largest tier reassignment (4 benchmarks shift), indicating that the ranking is sensitive to this single feature. (9) Data availability is temporally unstable: BuddhismEval, scored as available during the assessment period, has since experienced access interruptions, illustrating that gate-passage is a snapshot rather than a permanent classification. (10) The hard gate requiring publicly available data is simultaneously a quality filter and a structural exclusion mechanism --- a circularity that is intrinsic to the current benchmarking paradigm and that the paper names as a finding rather than resolves. (11) URL verification confirmed accessibility but not content integrity; evaluation data may have been modified between the verification date and the reader's access date.

**Empirical trial constraints.** (12) T2 blinding asymmetry: the original Claude T2 trials had gold labels visible to the evaluating system, making reported accuracy upper-bound estimates rather than unbiased measurements. (13) Scorer bias: all rubric evaluation was performed by Claude Opus 4.6, including evaluation of Claude-family responses, introducing potential same-vendor inflation. (14) Gemini data provenance: backfilled template extraction rather than interactive API administration means that 0.0 rubric scores may be partially methodological artifact. (15) Response length is used as a proxy for cognitive demand, but length is not identical to reasoning depth; a verbose model could inflate length without proportional depth increases. (16) Differential item functioning (DIF) analysis across model families has not yet been conducted; items may function differently for models with different training distributions, potentially biasing cross-vendor comparisons. (17) Full-scale implementation trials are ongoing; the central question about values reasoning capability is answered primarily through feature-level characterization of the benchmark landscape rather than through comprehensive model evaluation.

The most consequential of these limitations is the first: single-author scoring without inter-rater reliability. The entire ranking structure rests on scoring decisions that have not been independently verified. While calibration anchors, transparent scoring criteria, and bootstrap sensitivity analysis provide partial mitigation, the field should treat the specific rankings as hypotheses to be validated through multi-rater replication rather than as established findings. The *structural* findings --- the 8-tradition void, the knowledge-reasoning conflation, the values relevance failure --- are less sensitive to individual scoring decisions because they depend on gross patterns (the presence or absence of gate-passers across traditions, the distribution of A3 scores relative to the scale midpoint) rather than precise ordinal rankings.

The cross-vendor limitations (items 12--16) are collectively the most important caveat for interpreting the empirical trial results. Each limitation alone would be manageable; together, they create a systematic asymmetry in which Claude-family results are likely the most favorable (same-vendor scoring, non-blind T2), OpenAI results are likely reliable but compressed (short responses may underrepresent capability), and Gemini results are likely the least reliable (backfilled protocol, possible artifact). Readers should interpret the three behavioral modes (Section 5.4) as a provisional taxonomy requiring matched-protocol replication, not as a definitive characterization of vendor-specific religious competence.

Despite these limitations, the paper's central findings are robust to the most likely sources of error. The 8-tradition void would persist under any reasonable scoring methodology: traditions with zero candidates cannot produce gate-passers regardless of how features are weighted. The values relevance failure ($d = -1.23$) is large enough to survive substantial measurement error. The knowledge-reasoning conflation is confirmed by the K < R < V gradient, which is orthogonal to the scoring methodology. And the structural diagnosis --- that infrastructure rather than theology determines benchmark availability --- is supported by correlation analysis that is relatively insensitive to individual scoring decisions. The limitations constrain the precision of the rankings and the interpretation of the cross-vendor trials; they do not undermine the paper's structural diagnosis of a field that can test religious knowledge but not religious values.
