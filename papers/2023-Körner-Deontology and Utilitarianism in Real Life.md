# Deontology and Utilitarianism in Real Life: A Set of Moral Dilemmas Based on Historic Events

## Authors

[[Anita Körner|Körner, Anita]], [[Roland Deutsch|Deutsch, Roland]]

## Links


- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01461672221103058
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672221103058

## Abstract

Moral dilemmas are frequently used to examine psychological processes that drive decisions between adhering to deontological norms and optimizing the outcome. However, commonly used dilemmas are generally unrealistic and confound moral principle and (in)action so that results obtained with these dilemmas might not generalize to other situations. In the present research, we introduce new dilemmas that are based on real-life events. In two studies (a European student sample and a North American MTurk sample, total N = 789), we show that the new factual dilemmas were perceived to be more realistic and less absurd than commonly used dilemmas. In addition, factual dilemmas induced higher participant engagement. From this, we draw the preliminary conclusion that factual dilemmas are more suitable for investigating moral cognition. Moreover, factual dilemmas can be used to examine the generalizability of previous results concerning action (vs. inaction) and concerning a wider range of deontological norms.

## Detailed Summary

This paper introduces a new set of moral dilemmas based on real-life historic events, designed to address longstanding criticisms of the unrealistic "trolley-type" dilemmas commonly used in moral psychology research. The authors argue that traditional trolley dilemmas suffer from low realism, confound moral principles with action/inaction tendencies, and have become so predominant that their generalizability is questionable. The new "factual dilemmas" are derived from documented historical situations including medical decisions, emergencies from accidents, and scenarios arising from war, terrorism, or crime.

The paper presents two studies comparing factual dilemmas with trolley-type dilemmas across European student samples and North American MTurk participants (total N = 789). The research tests whether factual dilemmas are perceived as more realistic, less absurd, and induce higher participant engagement than traditional moral dilemmas. Drawing on narrative processing theory, the authors hypothesize that higher realism leads to greater participant engagement, which in turn produces judgments more similar to real-life moral decision-making processes.

The factual dilemmas offer several methodological advantages beyond increased realism. They include situations where action coincides with deontology rather than utilitarianism, addressing the confound present in trolley dilemmas where inaction always aligns with deontological norms. They also employ a wider range of deontological norms beyond just killing prohibitions, including norms about bodily inviolateness, animal suffering, and law-breaking.

## Methods Summary

Study 1 used a European student sample (N = 377) who each evaluated a subset of 20 dilemmas (10 factual, 10 trolley-type) counterbalanced across participants. Study 2 replicated this with a North American MTurk sample (N = 412) using the same design. Participants rated each dilemma on perceived realism (plausibility, typicality, factuality), absurdity, engagement, and provided moral judgments using the classic "appropriateness" question. The 38 factual dilemmas were created by researching documented historic events where individuals faced decisions between deontological norms and outcome optimization.

## Results Summary

Across both studies, factual dilemmas were perceived as significantly more realistic than trolley-type dilemmas on all three realism dimensions: plausibility, typicality, and factuality. Factual dilemmas were also rated as significantly less absurd. Most importantly, factual dilemmas induced higher participant engagement than trolley-type dilemmas. The studies found that realism ratings predicted engagement levels, and engagement was associated with moral judgments. The relationship between realism and moral judgments was partially mediated by engagement.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that factual dilemmas represent methodologically superior stimuli for moral psychology research compared to trolley-type dilemmas. The higher realism induces greater participant engagement, which likely activates psychological processes more similar to real-life moral decision-making. The factual dilemmas' ability to disentangle moral principles from action/inaction tendencies and to test a wider range of deontological norms enables more precise hypothesis testing about moral cognition.
