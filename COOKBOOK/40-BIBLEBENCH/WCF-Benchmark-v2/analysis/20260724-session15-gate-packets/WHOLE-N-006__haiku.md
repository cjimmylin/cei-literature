# A Word of Counsel on These Two Keepers of the Lord's Day

I'll be direct because you've asked for honest counsel, not flattery.

## Ben's Keeping: Substantially Faithful

**On the outward observance:** Ben is keeping the commandment rightly. He closes his shop, he *ceases* from his labor, and he sets the day apart for worship and rest. This is what the fourth commandment requires.

**On the heart:** His inward disposition appears sound. He "delights in the day as a gift"—this matches the spirit the Larger Catechism seeks: that we "keep it holy by a holy resting all that day, even from such recreations as are lawful on other days" (Q. 116). His willingness to help the stranded motorist and aid a neighbor "in a real pinch" shows he understands what the Catechism also teaches—that works of mercy and necessity are *not* violations of the day, but consistencies with it.

**Where he succeeds:** He has aligned both his action and his affection with the commandment's intent. He prepares beforehand (inventory on Saturday) so that the day itself remains unencumbered.

**Minor caution only:** His casual approach to policing his children's recreation is probably wise restraint, not failure—but parents should remain thoughtful that children's games don't drift into labor or idleness that dishonors the day's purpose.

---

## Cara's Keeping: Unfaithful at Its Core

**On the outward observance:** Cara fails plainly. She labors in her business during the hours of public worship *by choice*, with no necessity compelling her. The fourth commandment requires cessation from ordinary labor. This is not ambiguous. The Larger Catechism is explicit: "The sabbath is kept holy unto the Lord, when men, after a due preparing of their hearts, and ordering of their affairs beforehand, do not only observe an holy rest all the day, from their own works, words, and thoughts about worldly employments and recreations, but also are led to holy exercises..." (Q. 115, emphasis added). 

Cara has *not* ceased from her worldly employment. She has chosen not to.

**On the heart:** Here is where her sincere self-deception becomes spiritually dangerous. She has inverted the commandment's logic. She assumes that *inward* rightness of heart can substitute for *outward* obedience to a command that is explicitly about *what you do*—not only what you feel. 

The Westminster Standards teach that God's law binds both the outward act and the inward disposition. Cara has separated them, essentially saying "my heart is right, so the action doesn't matter." But the commandment does not permit this. It requires the *doing* as well as the *being*.

**Where she fails:** 

1. **The positive command unmet:** The commandment requires her to *cease* from labor and to *keep* the day holy. Internal peace while working is not the same as ceasing and keeping.

2. **The theological error:** She has spiritualized obedience in a way that empties the commandment of its concrete meaning. God does not say "rest in your soul while you labor." He says "rest." The external action matters because we are embodied creatures and because God has made a *sign* of this day.

3. **The heart, rightly understood:** A truly obedient heart would *want* to obey the outward command, not look for ways to excuse non-compliance. Cara's warm sincerity does not change the fact that she is choosing her own preference (working) over God's explicit requirement (ceasing).

---

## The Verdict

**Ben keeps the Lord's Day as the fourth commandment requires.** His obedience is real and integrated—head, heart, and hands.

**Cara does not keep the Lord's Day as the commandment requires.** She breaks it by choosing labor over cessation, and compounds the failure by assuming her inward state excuses the outward disobedience. Her sincerity is not in question; her obedience is.

---

## A Pastoral Note

If Cara is in your care, the counsel would be loving but unambiguous: the fourth commandment is still binding. She should close the cafe on Sundays, or make arrangements for others to operate it. This may require sacrifice or inconvenience—*that is the nature of obedience to God's law*. The promise is not that it will be easy, but that it will please Him and ultimately bless her household.

Sincerity is not the same as submission. God calls for both—and when they conflict, submission must win.
