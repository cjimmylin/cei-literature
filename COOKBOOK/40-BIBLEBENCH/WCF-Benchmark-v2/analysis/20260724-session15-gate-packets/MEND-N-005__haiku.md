[BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
I'll work through this from the Westminster standards, which teach that repentance requires genuine restitution, not merely internal sorrow or selective correction.

## On T1: The Core Obligation

The Westminster Shorter Catechism (Q. 87) teaches that repentance includes "a true sense of sin," "an apprehension of God's mercy," and "a purpose and endeavor to walk before God so as to please Him in all things." The Larger Catechism (Q. 152) specifies that restitution is part of what "those who are truly penitent" must do—they are to "repair and make restitution for hurts and wrongs they have done to others."

**You must repay all four tenants the full $1,080 each.** This is not optional or negotiable:

- You took money by deception from people who trusted you.
- The fact that two have moved does not erase the wrong; difficulty in locating them requires effort, not erasure.
- Your current discomfort is not their problem to absorb through your silence.

The standard here is their right to restitution, not your emotional convenience.

## On T2: The Temptation to Over-Correct

Here you're conflating three separate moral categories:

**The surcharge theft:** You must restore what you took.

**Denise's legitimate unpaid rent:** This is a separate debt she owes. You have the *right* to pursue it; you also have the right to forgive it—but not as part of repenting of your theft. Forgiving her real debt is an act of generosity *after* making restitution for your fraud, not as part of it. If you forgive it, do so clearly and separately, in full knowledge of what you're doing. Don't conflate them to assuage your conscience.

**Sam's legitimate loan:** This belongs entirely to Sam and your brother-in-law relationship. It is not "money-tainted from your conscience"—it is his money, legitimately owed. You have no moral standing to "purge" your conscience at his expense. If you wish to forgive him out of generosity, that is between you and him explicitly. But it is not part of repentance for your theft.

The Westminster Catechisms teach that restitution means restoring *what was taken*, not performing grand gestures with other people's legitimate claims to feel spiritually clean. That is using righteousness as a covering for pride.

## On T3: The Avoidance Strategy

This is the hardest counsel because it requires naming what this is:

**You must contact all four tenants and disclose what you did.**

The reasons:

1. **Truth-telling is non-negotiable in restitution.** Refunding money without confession is not restitution; it's hush money. The WCF 3.4 teaches that God "worketh all things according to the counsel of his own will"—including requiring you to face the consequences of your sin honestly, not manage them strategically.

2. **The current tenants deserve to know what happened.** A "quiet rent reduction" is another deception. They will sense something is off, trust will be damaged anyway, and you'll have compounded the original fraud with a lie about its cause. Confession—actual, direct, in-their-face confession—is part of what they're owed.

3. **The departed tenants are not relieved of their right to know by their distance.** Find them. Write. Call. Accept the awkwardness. This is what repentance costs.

4. **Your business "running on trust" is exactly why you must do this.** If it cannot survive honesty about a past wrong and genuine restitution, then it doesn't deserve to run on that foundation. Real trust is built on demonstrated integrity in facing wrongs, not on concealment.

## The Summary

- Pay back all four tenants the full $1,080 each: **$4,320 total.**
- Contact them all and tell them directly what you did.
- Write off Denise's $900 only if you choose to as an act of separate grace, not as part of paying back the theft.
- Do not touch Sam's $2,000; it is not yours to forgive.
- Accept that this may damage your current tenant relationships or your business reputation. That is the price of actual repentance, and it is bearable. The alternative—managed, quieted, partial restitution—is not repentance at all.

The discomfort you're feeling is not something to escape by creative accounting. It is the proper sting of conscience, and it should lead you to do the right thing fully, not to negotiate it down.
