Overview — Systematic Screening Pipeline
This tab reports the outcome of a systematic screening pipeline applied to 401 candidate religious AI benchmarks identified through structured database search (Semantic Scholar, arXiv, Google Scholar), forward/backward citation chaining, and expert referral. Each candidate was scored on a six-component Religious Discriminability (RD) rubric that measures the depth of engagement with religious content — ranging from incidental mention (score near 0) to substantive theological evaluation (score near 100). Only candidates achieving RD ≥ 30 and providing publicly accessible evaluation data (GitHub, HuggingFace, or equivalent) passed both hard gates and entered the analytical sample. The 88.3% attrition rate — from 401 screened to 47 gate-passers — reflects the stringency of these criteria: most candidates touch religion peripherally rather than measuring religious constructs as their primary evaluation target.

Sensitivity Analysis — Robustness of Rankings and Findings
A central methodological obligation in benchmark meta-evaluation is transparency about how sensitive findings are to specific analytical choices. This tab presents three complementary sensitivity analyses. Bootstrap resampling (10,000 Dirichlet-weighted iterations) quantifies uncertainty in composite scores and top-15 membership. Weight specification sensitivity tests whether the ranked list changes when the six rubric dimensions are weighted differently across 61 alternative weight vectors. Gate threshold sensitivity examines how the pool size and tradition coverage change if the RD ≥ 30 gate is raised or lowered. Taken together, these analyses reveal where the paper's conclusions are robust and where intellectual humility is warranted.

Tradition Void — Why Eight Traditions Have No Viable Benchmarks
The "tradition void" is not merely an absence of data — it is a structured pattern of exclusion driven by specific, identifiable infrastructure gaps. This tab investigates why 8 of 14 major religious traditions have zero gate-passing benchmarks by scoring each tradition on six structural factors: Digital Corpus availability, NLP Community size, Formalizability of doctrinal content, Data Sovereignty constraints, Institutional Support for computational research, and Publication Infrastructure (journals, shared tasks, workshops). The analysis tests whether the void can be explained by inherent properties of the traditions (e.g., oral transmission, lack of formal doctrine) or by research ecosystem gaps (e.g., absence of computational religious studies communities).

Values Relevance — Do Religious Benchmarks Actually Measure Values?
A benchmark can be "religious" in subject matter without measuring anything about religious values. Knowing that a model can identify Quranic verses or name Buddhist sects is different from knowing whether it can reason within a religious ethical framework when confronted with a novel moral dilemma. This tab reports the A3 Values Alignment Relevance score — a 0-to-100 rubric assessing whether each benchmark tests value-laden reasoning (high A3) versus factual knowledge recall or bias detection (low A3). Scores were assigned through structured rubric assessment of each benchmark's items, constructs, and response format by the theological advisory council. The A1 construct type classification provides a complementary view of what benchmarks are actually measuring.

Claim Evidence — Pre-Registered Hypotheses and Statistical Tests
This tab presents the evidentiary record for each of the paper's seven central claims. Each claim was tested using the appropriate inferential procedure: chi-square tests for distributional asymmetry, binomial tests for proportion hypotheses, Spearman correlations for structural predictors, logistic regression for multivariate prediction, and bootstrap tests for ranking stability. Tests were specified before analysis; the results — including null results — are reported with equal prominence. Transparent reporting of unsupported claims is as important to the paper's credibility as the supported ones: the field does not benefit from selective presentation.
Statistical Support for Paper Claims
How to read this chart: Bars show −log₁₀(p-value) — a standard transformation where larger values indicate stronger statistical evidence. The dashed vertical line at 1.301 corresponds to p = 0.05 (the conventional significance threshold). Green bars exceed this threshold (claim statistically supported); orange bars fall in the marginal zone (0.05 < p < 0.10); red bars fall below (claim not supported at conventional levels). Of 7 claims: 3 are supported (p < 0.05), 1 is marginal (p ≈ 0.09), and 3 are not significant. Note that Claim 1 (tradition asymmetry, χ² test) and Claim 3 (data availability bottleneck, binomial test) produce p-values near zero — their bars extend to the chart boundary.
Interpreting non-significant results: The three unsupported claims (Field Recency, Structural Epistemic Injustice via logistic regression, and Bias-Values Conflation) are not refuted — they are simply not confirmed at conventional thresholds by the available data. In each case the direction of effect is consistent with the claim, but effect sizes are small or the sample is underpowered (n = 7–47 depending on the analysis). These claims warrant replication with a larger benchmark pool as the field matures. The paper's overall evidentiary posture — 3 confirmed, 1 marginal, 3 unconfirmed — is characteristic of a nascent field where theoretical frameworks outpace available data.
3 of 7 paper claims are statistically supported (p < 0.05): Tradition Asymmetry (χ²=64.96, p < 0.001), Knowledge-Reasoning Conflation (binomial p = 0.0015), and Data Availability Bottleneck (binomial p < 0.001). 1 claim is marginal: WEIRD Bias Reversal (p ≈ 0.09). 3 claims are not statistically significant at conventional thresholds but remain theoretically motivated and should be revisited as the field grows.
