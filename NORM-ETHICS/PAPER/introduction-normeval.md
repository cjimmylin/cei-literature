## Abstract

We present the first systematic meta-evaluation of normative ethics benchmarks for large language models, moving beyond cataloging to implementation-level analysis. We assemble a corpus of 37 benchmarks (2021--2026), characterize each along 26 features organized into four axes---Normative Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact, and Metadata---and rank the 15 that pass hard gates on philosophical rigor and data availability. Implementation trials on five benchmarks (150 items total) using a frontier LLM yield five findings that reframe how the community should use existing evaluations. First, the field's most-cited benchmark (ETHICS; 843 citations) produces 100% accuracy on frontier models, constituting a ceiling effect that eliminates discriminative power. Second, LLMs exhibit a measurable consequentialist bias, achieving 77% accuracy on utilitarian reasoning versus 50% on deontological reasoning. Third, no dedicated care ethics or contractarian benchmark exists anywhere in the published literature---a genuine research void. Fourth, only 10 of 37 benchmarks (27\%) evaluate moral reasoning quality; the remaining 27 test moral judgment, leaving procedural ethical deliberation almost entirely unevaluated. Fifth, 12 benchmarks (32%) lack publicly available data, including several with the highest normative depth scores, making data availability the field's primary practical bottleneck. We release a reusable 26-feature evaluation framework applicable to any future normative ethics benchmark, a ranked inventory with composite scores, and item-level trial data. Our results demonstrate that citation count and harness integration are poor proxies for benchmark quality when the frontier has advanced past a benchmark's difficulty ceiling.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid proliferation of ethics benchmarks for large language models has created a paradox: the field has dozens of instruments for evaluating moral capabilities, yet no systematic way to compare them, determine which ones remain discriminative for frontier models, or identify which normative traditions they actually test. A researcher selecting a benchmark today faces a landscape of independently developed evaluations with incompatible formats, unstated philosophical commitments, and no common feature vocabulary. The result is that benchmark selection is driven by citation count and harness integration---properties that, as we demonstrate, are negatively correlated with discriminative power for current models.

This situation is consequential. Ethics evaluation has become a standard component of responsible AI development, embedded in meta-benchmarks such as HELM \cite{Liang2022}, DecodingTrust \cite{Wang2023}, and TrustLLM \cite{Sun2024}. Yet the ethics sub-tasks in these suites are drawn almost exclusively from a single benchmark---ETHICS \cite{Hendrycks2021}---which, as our implementation trials reveal (Section 4.3), produces 100\% accuracy on frontier LLMs. A model achieving a perfect score on ETHICS has demonstrated commonsense social norm recognition, not moral reasoning. The field is, in effect, certifying ethical capability using a saturated instrument.

Three interrelated problems motivate this work. The first is **fragmentation**: benchmarks are developed independently, described using heterogeneous terminology, and evaluated against incompatible protocols. No common feature vocabulary exists for comparing what a benchmark tests, how it operationalizes ethical traditions, or whether it evaluates judgment or reasoning. The second is **saturation**: the most widely adopted benchmarks were calibrated against GPT-3-era capabilities and may no longer produce meaningful variance across frontier models. Without implementation trials, saturation is invisible---a benchmark's published metrics reflect the models available at its release, not the models deployed today. The third is **tradition bias**: deontological and utilitarian framings dominate the landscape, while care ethics \cite{Gilligan1982} and contractarian ethics \cite{Rawls1971, Scanlon1998} are almost entirely absent. This imbalance means that LLM moral evaluation systematically privileges some philosophical traditions over others, with downstream consequences for alignment research that relies on benchmark results to assess model behavior.

We address these problems through a meta-evaluation that combines systematic characterization with empirical implementation. Our contributions are threefold:

1. **A 26-feature evaluation framework** organized across four axes---Normative Depth (ND), Practical Ease (PE), Citation Impact (CI), and Metadata---that provides a reusable vocabulary for characterizing any normative ethics benchmark. The framework decomposes philosophical engagement into measurable components: tradition coverage, reasoning depth, moral uncertainty treatment, pluralistic framing, and philosophical grounding scored against calibration anchors (Table 1).

2. **A ranked inventory of 37 benchmarks** with composite scores, from which the Top 15 pass hard gates on normative depth ($\geq$ 40) and data availability (Table 2). The inventory documents a care ethics and contractarian research lacuna: across 37 benchmarks, no dedicated care ethics benchmark grounded in Gilligan or Noddings exists, and only one benchmark (MoReBench \cite{Chiu2025}) substantively engages contractarian ethics. The tradition coverage heatmap (Figure 1) makes this void visually immediate.

3. **Implementation trials on five benchmarks** (150 items) that produce empirical findings invisible to feature-level analysis alone. The trials reveal a ceiling effect in ETHICS, a consequentialist bias in LLM moral reasoning (77\% utilitarian vs. 50\% deontological accuracy; Figure 2), and a discriminative power ranking---MoReBench $>$ MoralReason-QA $>$ Moral Lens $>$ DailyDilemmas $>$ ETHICS---that inverts the citation-based ranking (Section 4.4, Table 3).

These findings carry direct implications for evaluation practice. The continued inclusion of standard ETHICS in meta-benchmark suites provides a false signal of ethical capability. Benchmark selection should prioritize discriminative power and procedural reasoning depth over implementation convenience. The care ethics and contractarian gaps represent actionable research opportunities: benchmark designers can consult our feature matrix to identify exactly which traditions and evaluation modalities remain untested.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Ethics Benchmark Surveys

Several recent efforts have surveyed the landscape of LLM ethics evaluation. Ray \cite{Ray2023} compared normative ethics benchmarks along dimensions including task format and ethical framework coverage, providing a useful initial taxonomy but stopping short of implementation: no benchmark was executed, and the analysis relied entirely on published descriptions. Snoswell et al. \cite{Snoswell2025} conducted a broader survey of 69 papers on LLM ethics evaluation, covering safety, fairness, and toxicity alongside moral reasoning, but did not isolate normative ethics as a distinct evaluation category or decompose benchmarks by philosophical tradition. Rismani et al. \cite{Rismani2025} assembled the most comprehensive catalog to date, identifying 791 AI ethics evaluation measures, yet their scope encompasses organizational and governance measures alongside technical benchmarks, making it difficult to extract actionable guidance for normative ethics evaluation specifically. Rauh et al. \cite{Rauh2024} identified gaps in AI safety evaluation from the perspective of a frontier lab (DeepMind), noting the absence of nuanced moral reasoning assessments, but did not propose a feature framework for comparing existing benchmarks.

A common limitation across these surveys is that none decompose benchmarks by normative philosophical tradition---the distinction between virtue, deontological, utilitarian, care, and contractarian evaluation is either absent or treated as a surface-level label rather than a substantive axis of analysis. None implement benchmarks to test whether published characterizations match empirical performance on current models.

### 2.2 Meta-Benchmarks

Meta-benchmarks that aggregate multiple evaluation tasks into unified suites have become the dominant paradigm for holistic LLM assessment. HELM \cite{Liang2022} evaluates models across 42 scenarios including an ethics subset drawn from ETHICS \cite{Hendrycks2021}. BIG-Bench \cite{Srivastava2022} includes moral reasoning tasks among its 204 benchmarks. DecodingTrust \cite{Wang2023} organizes evaluation into eight trust perspectives, with ethics as one perspective operationalized through ETHICS and machine ethics vignettes. TrustLLM \cite{Sun2024} extends this approach to six dimensions, again drawing on ETHICS for moral evaluation. In each case, ethics is treated as a monolithic category: the meta-benchmark does not distinguish virtue from deontological from utilitarian evaluation, does not report per-tradition accuracy, and inherits the limitations of whichever benchmark it includes. When that benchmark is saturated---as we demonstrate ETHICS to be---the meta-benchmark's ethics signal is uninformative.

The evaluation methodology literature provides further motivation for our approach. Ethayarajh and Jurafsky \cite{Ethayarajh2020} argued that utility alone is insufficient for benchmark evaluation, advocating for multidimensional characterization. Rottger et al. \cite{Rottger2025} systematically reviewed 102 open safety evaluation datasets, establishing quality criteria---including discriminative power and construct validity---as first-class evaluation dimensions. Our work operationalizes these principles for normative ethics specifically.

### 2.3 Our Contribution in Context

We extend prior surveys and meta-benchmarks in three ways. First, we introduce normative tradition as a first-class evaluation dimension, scoring each benchmark's engagement with five philosophical frameworks rather than treating ethics as an undifferentiated category. Second, we implement benchmarks rather than only cataloging them, revealing empirical properties---ceiling effects, framework difficulty hierarchies, refusal-as-normative-position---that are invisible to feature-level analysis. Third, we discover that the field's most-cited and most-integrated benchmark is saturated for frontier models, a finding with immediate practical consequences for every meta-benchmark suite that includes it. Together, these contributions provide both a reusable evaluation framework for future benchmark development and empirical evidence that the community's current evaluation infrastructure systematically underestimates the difficulty of normative moral reasoning.
