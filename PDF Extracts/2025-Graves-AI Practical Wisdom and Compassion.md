Abstract
Practical wisdom (phronesis) is the context-sensitive capacity to skillfully achieve morally good outcomes in complex situ­
ations. Developing artificial practical wisdom offers a more ethically robust and achievable goal for AI development than 
artificial general intelligence (AGI). While identifying what is morally good in ethically complex situations remains chal­
lenging, grounding artificial practical wisdom explicitly in compassion effectively reduces ethical risks associated with AI-
induced suffering, surpassing conventional alignment strategies like rule-based guardrails or predefined reward systems. 
As a theoretical foundation for initial development of artificial practical wisdom, this virtue ethics approach integrates 
Aristotelian practical wisdom with cross-cultural perspectives on suffering and compassion from utilitarianism, the Capa­
bility Approach, Buddhism, and contemporary moral psychology. Operationalizing compassionate AI involves recognizing 
suffering, empathetic engagement, context-sensitive moral decision making, and motivational responses. Compassionate 
AI not only serves as a foundation for broader practical wisdom development but also demonstrates immediate practi­
cal benefits, particularly in healthcare, by measurably improving patient outcomes, enhancing well-being, and reducing 
caregiver burdens.

2  AI alignment & human suffering
Many concerns about AI ethics, alignment, and safety 
are about the risk of human suffering. One’s employment 
provides resources needed for daily living, and AI job dis­
placement threatens livelihoods, family resources, and per­
sonal and professional identities [15]. AI systems trained 
on biased data can perpetuate and even amplify societal 
bias and discrimination, leading to the increased suffering 
of unrepresented groups [16–18]. The vast data-gathering 
capabilities of AI systems create new threats to privacy, and 
this lack of privacy affects self-integrity and freedom to 
selectively disclose oneself, making people vulnerable and 
feeling exposed, which impacts self-esteem [19, 20]. Loss 
of control affects one’s self and self-efficacy and increases 
real threats of pain. Becoming dependent upon AI and los­
ing personally or professionally meaningful skills affects 
identity and creates loss [21]. Risk of annihilation and other 
scenarios may ultimately eliminate pain but still create suf­
fering through the destroying of selves, a concern voiced 
by those studying existential risks of AI [22]. Although 
uncertainty exists up to and including an AI singularity, the 
unforeseen negative consequences likely involve human 
suffering.
Many hopes for AI futures align with flourishing and alle­
viating suffering. Improvements to healthcare mitigate pain 
and suffering [23–25]. Education increases knowledge and 
skills, enabling self-efficacy to address situations that would 
otherwise create personal, family, and social suffering [26]. 
Progress in managing ecological change through advanced 
AI reduces human pain and suffering and their extension to 
other animals and ecological systems [27]. Imagined sci­
entific innovations and economic growth usually highlight 
improved well-being and flourishing. Reframing the pub­
lic and ethical discourse about AI acceleration and safety 
in terms of positive futures that orient toward flourishing 
and address or alleviate suffering clarifies that discourse and 
connects it to established ethical traditions drawing upon 
long-standing ancient wisdom and deeply embedded cul­
tural values.
Research in AI alignment aims to ensure AI systems act 
consistently with human intentions and values [28]. This 
includes aligning both the external behavior and internal 
objectives of AI systems with human norms, ethical prin­
ciples, and societal goals. These efforts involve technical 
approaches (e.g., reinforcement learning from human feed­
back), philosophical approaches (e.g., value pluralism), 
and sociotechnical mechanisms (e.g., participatory design 
or regulatory oversight). Yet alignment is often constrained 
by ambiguity in value specification, context-sensitivity, and 
the possibility of emergent misalignment at scale. Practical 
wisdom offers a way to navigate these challenges by foster­
ing context-aware, ethically sensitive judgment within AI 
systems.
Some alignment researchers distinguish between outer 
alignment, which evaluates how AI behavior aligns with 
human values and preferences, and inner alignment, which 
addresses whether an AI/ML system internally develops 
and consistently adheres to these values through training

Current research has begun exploring intersections of 
AI and practical wisdom, though primarily focusing on the

From a virtue ethics perspective, practical wisdom involves 
developing character traits and habits through deliberate

Habituation and ML training have notable parallels, and 
practical wisdom and AI alignment have analogous chal­
lenges posed by having complex ends. For practical wis­
dom, one deliberately develops stable internal dispositions 
and ethical skills (virtue) rather than merely ensuring cor­
rect external behaviors or reward-driven performance. An 
Aristotelian understanding of practical wisdom includes the 
ability to deliberate well and both general and situation-spe­
cific understandings of the Good. Deliberating well requires 
a context-aware, value-guided reasoning process to inquire 
about contingent matters within a person’s power. Within 
this framing, responsible AI systems can have arguably rea­
sonable situation-specific understandings of the Good, and 
large AI systems (e.g., LLMs) are gaining the capacity to 
reason better, but current efforts are not driving toward AI 
that could deliberate well and have a general understanding 
of the Good. Thus, we will examine both aspects in more 
depth in the following section, particularly a four-compo­
nent psychological model of moral deliberation (Sect. 3.1) 
and a general theory of the Good drawing upon approaches 
to suffering from multiple ethical traditions (Sect. 3.2).
Developing artificial practical wisdom is both harder 
than and simpler than safe AGI. Deliberating well requires 
all the skills typically imagined for AGI, and moral rea­
soning in complex, real-world situations extends ‘general’ 
intelligence well-past the scientific and technical disciplines 
currently envisioned [40]. Few people would question the 
achievement of AGI should current GenAI chatbots obtain 
practical wisdom—which also requires truthful, appropri­
ate, and responsible interactions. It also seems unlikely that 
obtaining currently envisioned AGI would result in what 
ethicists and moral philosophers would consider artificial 
practical wisdom. Thus, artificial practical wisdom is harder 
than AGI. However, current AGI research is driven by 
architectural innovations [41–43] and an increasing range 
of frontier benchmarks [44–47] that still fall short of even 
attempting the critical thinking taught in the humanities 
[48–50], with AI safety researchers striving to contain an 
unpredictable future with complex values and an unknown 
technical horizon [51, 52]. Against that broad, open-ended, 
and ill-defined landscape, artificial practical wisdom pro­
vides a simpler, tangible, well-studied problem to solve 
that may well be easier than prior AI research accomplish­
ments in natural language processing, computer vision, and 
generative AI. Thus, artificial practical wisdom is simpler 
than AGI. Constructing an expert in practical wisdom, i.e., 
an artificial phronimos in Aristotelian ethics, would satisfy 
many hoped benefits of AGI systems and address many of 
its feared harms.
In summary, AI alignment traditionally focuses on ensur­
ing external behaviors align with human values, but this 
approach struggles with ambiguity and lacks sensitivity to 
context. Ethical concerns and hopes for AI often manifest 
as either threats to increase or promises to reduce human 
suffering, respectively. A solution to AI/AGI causing suffer­
ing is to characterize harmful AI/AGI outcomes as suffering 
and address that directly rather than through proxies, such 
as measures of bias or existential risk, which may otherwise 
add complexity and obfuscation to the fundamental risk of 
increased suffering [53]. Ethically, by focusing on alleviat­
ing and addressing suffering as the core value for a general 
moral good, artificial practical wisdom offers both a clearer 
target for alignment than abstract AGI and a more direct 
response to ethical concerns about AI-driven harm. In par­
ticular, the approach directly addresses the risk that AGI (or 
artificial superintelligence) causes immense suffering [54] 
(outer alignment) and indirectly addresses existential risk (if 
annihilation is included in suffering). In terms of AI devel­
opment, this approach is simultaneously more challenging 
than AGI (requiring sophisticated moral reasoning) and 
more tractable (providing well-defined ethical frameworks 
to implement).

3  Practical wisdom
Practical wisdom represents the capacity to make sound 
moral judgments in contextually-complex situations. Unlike 
theoretical knowledge or technical skill, practical wisdom 
involves the integration of moral perception, deliberation, 
motivation, and action guided by a conception of human 
flourishing. Drawing on philosophical and psychological 
frameworks, this section examines how practical wisdom 
can be conceptualized for AI systems.
Contemporary theories of practical wisdom vary in their 
emphasis on integration versus specialization of moral 
capacities. While some philosophers argue for a unified vir­
tue that coordinates all moral excellences, others suggest 
that the complexity of moral life requires distributed ethi­
cal competencies. For artificial practical wisdom, we adopt 
a framework that combines both perspectives: developing 
specific moral capacities like compassion while maintain­
ing an integrative architecture for coordinating ethical 
judgments.
3.1  Moral deliberation
Several philosophers and psychologists have developed the­
ories of practical wisdom. Daniel Russell develops an Aris­
totelian approach to practical intelligence describing its dual 
role of correctly identifying the mean of the other virtues 
and then integrating them [55, 56]. From Russell’s perspec­
tive, phronesis would be essential to the full development 
of compassion, and the present article focuses primarily on 
that phronetic elaboration of compassion with brief consid­
eration on what would be required for integration across 
multiple virtues. Christian Miller refers to Russell’s and 
similar neo-Aristotelian approaches as a Standard Model of 
practical wisdom, while identifying the wide range of func­
tions that standard practical wisdom must perform and argu­
ing that is too much for any one virtue, a position he calls 
the Eliminativist Model [57, 58]. An intermediate approach 
is the Aretai Model, which claims phronesis is a particular 
kind of expertise that fundamentally is what it means to be 
virtuous, though it manifests itself through clusters of indi­
vidual moral virtues [59–61]. Although most of the subtleties 
are unnecessary for the current treatment, Miller’s analysis 
of phronetic functions could guide specification and devel­
opment of artificial phronesis, and the Aretai Model clarifies 
the unique aspect of integration in phronesis that sets it apart 
from other virtues. In the Aretai model, phronesis is not just 
an additional virtue but a comprehensive capacity that inte­
grates other virtuous characteristics (like justice and com­
passion) and makes them virtues (as even in Aristotelian 
approaches, virtues do not stand alone). Furthermore, Mill­
er’s “handling conflicts” function of phronesis is needed for

3.2  General theory of the good
A general theory of the Good suitable for AI practical wis­
dom must be general enough to orient practical wisdom, sim­
ilar enough to human conceptions to engender trust, broad 
enough to encompass the range of human conditions, and 
integrity. Moral judgment is key to the alignment problem, 
needed for adjudicating among conflicting objectives in 
inner alignment and aligning with the appropriate worthy 
ends in outer alignment. Existing approaches for resolving 
disparate results among AI mixtures of experts or among AI 
agents may serve as an initial foundation for structuring the 
adjudication of conflicts, though modeling the overall moral 
preferences requires a theory of the Good (discussed in the 
next subsection). From the perspective of AI alignment, the 
challenge is that moral judgment is both a tool for achiev­
ing alignment and something that itself needs to be aligned. 
This creates a recursive problem requiring some baseline 
alignment to develop trustworthy moral judgment capabili­
ties, which can then be further developed to maintain and 
improve alignment at scale. For that baseline alignment in 
the present article, we describe adjudicating among possible 
objectives in terms of suffering and flourishing.
Moral motivation consists of prioritizing moral values 
above non-moral considerations and sustaining commit­
ment to moral action despite challenges. Motivation both 
strengthens and depends upon moral identity, integrating 
moral concerns deeply in the individual’s sense of self and 
distinguishing practical wisdom from mere knowledge of 
ethical principles [65, 68–71]. Existing AI systems often 
use goals, utility functions, or reward models as the direc­
tive component closest to motivation, but those do not suf­
fice for the dynamic and developmental aspects of moral 
motivations that are learned over time as people encounter 
new situations and perspectives. Phronetic individuals cul­
tivate intrinsic motivation toward ethical action rooted in a 
personally meaningful conception of the good life. Human 
moral motivation stems from our embeddedness in relation­
ships and communities and includes group belonging, care 
for specific others, social approval, and maintaining one’s 
moral identity within social contexts. This is more par­
ticular and contextual than abstract utility maximization. 
For the present article, we restrict moral identity to being 
compassionate (which also has a motivational element). 
Restricting motivations to alleviating and addressing suffer­
ing can initiate development of meta-learning systems that 
can revise their own objective functions based on experi­
ence and explicit models of human morality. At some level, 
AI systems may have behaviors that appear motivated in 
good directions without sufficiently genuine moral motiva­
tion. This problem occurs in purely human societies, too. 
The application to healthcare later in the article provides a 
context in which to explore these risks and benefits further.

channeling persistent motivations effectively, particularly 
when facing obstacles. In what Aristotle called the “doc­
trine of the mean,” appropriate emotional responses are

In summary, practical wisdom from Aristotelian virtue 
ethics offers an alternative framework that would enable 
AI systems to develop adaptive, context-aware moral judg­
ment through four key processes: perceiving ethical dimen­
sions of situations, weighing competing values, maintaining 
intrinsic motivation toward ethical action, and integrating 
affective value with reason. This approach requires both 
situational deliberation skills and a general understanding 
of the Good that encompasses diverse human perspectives. 
Virtue ethics emphasizes context-sensitive ethical decision-
making, character development, and moral discernment, 
which are qualities notably absent from conventional align­
ment approaches. To adequately align AI, practical wisdom 
must explicitly prioritize addressing ethical harms, particu­
larly human suffering, discussed next, which makes artificial 
precise enough to enable implementation in computational 
context. Such a theory can draw from historical accounts of 
the Good as well as contemporary ethical resources, includ­
ing ethical principles [7, 78], social processes [8, 79–81], 
capabilities [82–85], caring [86–88], and general ethical 
theories [89]. Establishing trust in AI involves more than 
demonstrating its accuracy and fairness; users must believe 
the AI reliably pursues outcomes they recognize as moral 
[90–92]. Additionally, conceptions of the Good should 
extend beyond Western, educated, industrialized, rich and 
democratic (WEIRD) perspectives, encompassing diverse 
cultural and religious views about engagement with tech­
nology [93–96]. The moral theory must also guide its own 
application by clarifying how its key concepts should be 
operationalized and contextualized, how competing values 
should be prioritized, and how feedback should be incorpo­
rated. For the present article, practical wisdom requires a 
theory of the Good that can guide moral deliberation while 
remaining implementable.
Within this framework, compassion serves as a founda­
tional orientation that connects practical wisdom to a sub­
stantive theory of the Good. Rather than treating the Good as 
an abstract ideal or static universal principle, this approach 
conceives of it as a dynamic, socially-embedded interpretive 
process focused on the alleviation and addressing of suffer­
ing and promotion of flourishing. Humans are in the loop, 
but at a speed and scale that fosters nuanced interpretation 
and guidance. The normative focus on suffering and flour­
ishing aligns with multiple ethical traditions described in 
the following section: utilitarian emphasis on reducing pain 
and suffering, the Capability Approach’s focus on removing 
obstacles to human flourishing, and Buddhist teachings on 
the centrality of suffering to the human condition (detailed 
in Sect. 4). By grounding the Good in compassionate 
response to suffering, artificial practical wisdom gains both 
philosophical coherence and practical operationalizability. 
This does not reduce all ethics to suffering-alleviation, as 
virtues like justice and veracity have independent value, but 
provides a tangible starting point for developing AI systems 
capable of moral judgment that can expand through iterative 
sociotechnical processes [81].
This suffering-focused approach addresses the ‘is/
ought’ problem by grounding AI’s normative orientation in 
humanity’s ongoing struggle to determine moral purpose 
rather than predetermined rules. AI’s ‘ought’ becomes par­
ticipation in determining moral purpose alongside humans, 
making the abstract and transcendent Good tractable for 
computational implementation while remaining open to 
diverse ethical traditions. Although not a complete theory of 
the Good, focusing initially on its suffering-related aspects 
creates an accessible entry point that can expand through 
further iteration and sociotechnical interpretive processes.

and experiences; and a pervasive and subtle unsatisfactori­
ness inherent to the impermanence of all conditioned exis­
tence. Understanding the Four Noble Truths and the nature, 
origin, and cessation of suffering is key to developing 
wisdom (prajna). In Buddhist ethics, wisdom arises from 
experiential insight into the nature of reality, especially 
impermanence, non-self, and interdependence, and ethical 
action flows from this wisdom [103]. The attention to suf­
fering and its causes then lead to action.
From a Western scientific perspective, pain is rooted in 
bodily experiences of potential or actual tissue damage, and 
suffering includes the experience of losing some aspect of 
one’s self (or threat of loss) and actual or threatened damage 
to emotional stability or psychological needs [104–106]. 
Suffering can also arise from systemic social disruptions and 
pathologies rather than individual conditions [107–109] and 
from the consequences and collective effects of ecological 
and environmental loss, destruction, and degradation with 
human and non-human impact [110–112]. People also expe­
rience existential suffering stemming from awareness of 
mortality, meaninglessness, isolation, or the uncertainty of 
existence [113–117] and moral and spiritual suffering asso­
ciated with a crisis of faith, loss of connection to the sacred, 
or struggles with ethical commitments and moral and spiri­
tual integrity, including moral distress and injury [118–120]. 
One can search for meaning in the midst of suffering, which 
may lead to changes in one’s self and self-understanding 
[121]. World religions structure this meaning-making in 
some way, generally locating it within a cosmic frame and 
practical wisdom both a more tangible and potentially safer 
goal than traditional AGI.
4  Suffering & compassion
Having established practical wisdom as the framework for 
AI alignment, we now turn to the specific virtue of compas­
sion and its relationship to suffering as the initial focus for 
developing artificial practical wisdom. As argued in the pre­
vious sections, practical wisdom provides a superior ethical 
foundation for AI alignment via a grounding in virtue eth­
ics. However, the proposed approach to artificial practical 
wisdom still requires a nuanced understanding of the human 
suffering that AI should prioritize alleviating.
4.1  Suffering
In this section, Western consequentialist ethics, the Capa­
bility Approach, and Buddhist ethics are introduced to aug­
ment Aristotelian practical wisdom with a broad ethical 
foundation for defining suffering and compassion. While 
these approaches originate from different philosophical tra­
ditions, they are unified by their shared focus on the primacy 
of suffering in ethical deliberation. Whether it is the hedo­
nistic calculus of utilitarianism, attention to thwarted capa­
bilities, or the Buddhist emphasis on suffering (dukkha), 
all these frameworks identify the alleviation of suffering as 
a core moral imperative. This contrasts with other ethical 
approaches, such as deontology, that prioritize duties and 
rules regardless of the consequences for suffering. Scientific 
perspectives on suffering and compassion build upon and 
clarify these traditions and help operationalize compassion, 
making explicit how compassion can guide artificial practi­
cal wisdom if embedded in AI systems and reinforcing the 
overall thesis that compassionate practical wisdom is criti­
cal for ethically aligned AI.
As a consequentialist theory, classic utilitarianism (fol­
lowing Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill) defines 
utility hedonistically with pleasure as good and pain and 
suffering as bad, and one acts to maximize aggregate happi­
ness and minimize aggregate suffering. Although it can be 
challenging to measure and compare suffering, and a focus 
on aggregates can justify violations of individual rights, 
utilitarianism unequivocally establishes suffering as a key 
in determining the Good.
More broadly consequentialist, yet focused on human 
rights and dignity, the Capability Approach [82, 83] frames 
well-being and human development on what people are 
effectively able to do and be rather than resources or out­
comes. It emphasizes the essential freedom of individuals to 
choose and achieve states of being and doing (capabilities)

nature as lacking any permanent self or soul (anatta), and thus 
human nature is fundamentally interconnected with all liv­

the natural response to suffering. Buddhist Brahma-vihāras

into compassion have also guided Western psychology and 
neuroscientific research [124–129].
Within Western psychology, compassion is an emo­
tional response to another person’s suffering that motivates 
one to relieve that suffering. Drawing in part on Buddhist 
understandings, some Western psychologists [130] have 
distinguished five elements of compassion: (a) recognizing 
suffering; (b) feeling for the person in distress; (c) under­
standing the universality of suffering in human existence; 
(d) tolerating uncomfortable feelings; and (e) motivation 
to act or acting to alleviate suffering. Each element plays 
a distinct psychological and ethical role. Recognizing suf­
fering involves cognitive, perception, and attention mech­
anisms to detect distress or harm. Feeling for the person 
activates affective resonance or concern with the other 
person. Understanding the universality of suffering enables 
perspective-taking and broader cognitive awareness of the 
many forms of suffering. Tolerating uncomfortable feel­
ings allows sustained, self-regulated affective engagement 
without emotional distancing, burnout, or avoidance. Moti­
vation to act transforms awareness and concern into inten­
tional behavior to alleviate suffering. These components 
together form a cognitive-affective-motivational framework 
for compassion.
Compassion differs from related concepts of empathy, 
sympathy, and altruism. Empathy overlaps with compas­
sion, as empathy refers to the ability to recognize and feel 
the emotions of another person, being both more general 
by including other emotions, such as joy, and more specific 
by only accounting for the first two aspects of compassion 
(element a and b) [131, 132]. In this article, sympathy refers 
to the heightened awareness of another’s plight as some­
thing to be alleviated [133] (element b and a precursor to 
e). As a further distinction, although atypical, one could be 
highly empathetic, at least in the capacity to recognize the 
emotions of others, yet desire instead to increase another 
person’s suffering [134, 135], while sympathy and compas­
sion require a desire or motivation, respectively, to alleviate 
suffering. Both sympathy and compassion can deteriorate 
into pity, which may indicate an insufficient capacity to tol­
erate uncomfortable feelings (element d) and result from 
a distancing from the ‘other’. Altruism is a selfless and 
disinterested concern for the well-being of others and can 
also motivate one to act to alleviate suffering, but does not 
in comparison to a non-suffering ideal. Abrahamic faiths of 
Judaism, Christianity, and Islam attend to suffering’s per­
sonal implications, and Hinduism and Buddhism character­
ize suffering’s causal and universal existence [122].
Across these approaches, suffering is seen as central to 
ethics, but it is defined in complementary ways as pain and 
pleasure (utilitarianism), deprivation of freedom and dignity 
(capabilities), pervasive unsatisfactoriness (Buddhism), and 
layered biopsychosocial, ecological, existential, and spiri­
tual realities (science and religion). Together, they provide a 
broad ethical map that can inform how AI systems, if guided 
by practical wisdom, might recognize and respond compas­
sionately to human and ecological suffering. For instance, a 
utilitarian analysis might quantify the aggregate pain caused 
by a natural disaster, while the Capability Approach could 
highlight the long-term loss of freedoms and opportunities, 
and a Buddhist perspective could emphasize the psycho­
logical suffering that arises from attachment to what was 
lost. An AI guided by practical wisdom could integrate these 
perspectives to develop a more holistic and compassionate 
response, addressing not only the immediate physical pain 
but also the long-term social and psychological needs of the 
affected population.
Although not all aspects of virtue ethics are related to 
suffering, much of practical wisdom and all of compas­
sion can be framed in terms of their relationship to suffer­
ing, especially a broad conception of suffering that includes 
damage or threats to moral and spiritual integrity. Virtue 
ethics and flourishing (eudamonia) include activities that 
appear intrinsically valuable, such as cultivating excel­
lences, friendship, gratitude, and virtue for virtue’s sake, so 
ethics does not reduce to addressing suffering. However, as 
an initial attempt at characterizing artificial practical wis­
dom, the remainder of the article will focus on the aspects 
of practical wisdom that relate to alleviating or addressing 
suffering (in a broad sense) and the promotion of flourishing 
(in opposition to suffering). A suffering-focused approach to 
practical wisdom does not reduce to compassion; for exam­
ple practical wisdom is still required to identify potential 
harms caused by some compassionate acts and to adjudicate 
between actions that address different types of suffering. A 
subsequent section will also briefly consider justice in terms 
of addressing suffering to better differentiate suffering-
focused practical wisdom and compassion.

5.1  Operationalizing compassion
Although focusing too strongly on the five-part definition 
of compassion risks oversimplification of compassion, con­
sidering the implementation of each element separately 
illustrates how development could begin. To operationalize 
the five-part definition of compassion described above, the 
recognition of suffering (element a) can draw upon natural 
language processing (NLP) to detect expressions of distress 
necessarily incorporate the affective, or emotional, elements 
(b or d).
While compassion provides the foundational orienta­
tion toward alleviating suffering, a fully developed artifi­
cial phronimos would need to integrate a broader suite of 
virtues and orient toward flourishing, which warrants some 
discussion. The significant negative valuation of suffering 
in compassionate deliberation can serve as a foundation 
for expanding integration across additional virtues, such as 
justice and veracity. Justice addresses systemic causes of 
suffering by rectifying harm and ensuring fairness across 
individuals and groups. Basic definitions of fairness in AI 
and data ethics address various types of ethical biases [16], 
which reflect differences from a norm that matter in some 
way that typically involve a harm that results in suffering. 
As some common definitions are fairness are mathemati­
cally incompatible, except in some highly constrained cases 
[136], the ethical determination of which fairness measure 
to use depends upon external criteria, of which the harm and 
suffering caused are highly relevant. Rather than resolving 
trade-offs abstractly, the incorporation of suffering-focused 
practical wisdom allows for asking questions like: Which 
definition of fairness reduces the aggregate suffering (utili­
tarian)? Or, which definition reduces the deprivation of 
freedom and dignity (capabilities)? Or, which definition 
reduces the most severe or unjust suffering in this context? 
(Judeo-Christian) There is still a need for moral identity 
to resolve which of these questions to ask, which further 
engages identity-oriented aspects of practical wisdom, such 
as motivations guided by self-understanding [68]. Veracity 
ensures transparent communication and truthful represen­
tation of AI’s capacities and decisions, which prevents the 
suffering caused by deception and enables the trust neces­
sary for human flourishing. An initial grounding in com­
passion focuses development on the essential awareness 
of suffering, which can also inform the deliberation across 
virtues. Although a richer definition of flourishing would be 
needed for full practical wisdom, flourishing gains much of 
its meaning when contrasted with states of deprivation and 
pain, and thus suffering can ground initial development of 
compassion and practical wisdom in AI.

6  Healthcare applications & well-being
Healthcare exemplifies a domain where AI compassionate 
practical wisdom is urgently needed and can be practically 
evaluated, thus demonstrating and testing the approach. 
Compassion-based approaches can measurably improve 
patient outcomes and caregiver interactions, illustrating the 
broad applicability and impact of compassionate practical 
wisdom. Researchers have begun examining the incorpora­
tion of compassion or empathy in healthcare AI [144, 184, 
186–189]. In healthcare contexts, patient outcomes are pos­
itively affected by compassion and empathetic caregiving 
[149, 190–192] and adversely affected by technology [184, 
186, 193, 194]. If compassion is not incorporated into AI 
technologies in healthcare, then financial incentives driving 
efficient automation can undermine trustworthy care and 
cause clinical harm [187, 195–199]. Alternatively, incorpo­
rating compassion into healthcare AI can improve patient 
communication, trust, health outcomes, recovery, adher­
ence, and well-being [184, 186–188, 199]. There are ethical 
and other concerns that the simulation of uniquely human 
emotions or empathy or the lack of human resonance could 
cause social harm due to a lack of veracity and authenticity, 
especially in vulnerable populations [11–13, 170], though 
others have highlighted the relevance of AI empathetic 
awareness [14] and the importance of AI evaluating the 
effects and outcomes of its actions [151, 184].
A focus on incorporating compassion as an integra­
tive foundation in healthcare AI can initiate development 
of practical wisdom in that context, while improving user 
experience and patient outcomes and reducing caregiver 
cognitive and emotional load. Financial incentives, health­
care worker shortages, and the promise of increasing the 
quality of care are all predicted to increase AI applications 
throughout healthcare [200–203]. The speed, scale, perva­
siveness, and opacity of those AI systems will thwart human 
monitoring and timely evaluation of those systems for their 
effect on suffering, thus requiring AI systems to recognize 
various forms of suffering, monitor other AI for their impact 
on suffering at speed and scale, and ideally modify those 
systems that unnecessarily increase suffering to orient them 
in a compassionate direction.
Some AI and robotic systems may directly incorporate 
compassion and prudential deliberation in their behavior, 
such as in palliative or elder care or in other patient inter­
actions with high levels of intrinsic suffering [147, 148, 
184]. Many current and near-future healthcare technologies 
would benefit from incorporating heightened awareness of 
suffering and appropriate compassionate responses, includ­
ing voice assistants for dementia care, robotic mobility 
assistants, palliative care monitors, and patient and infor­
mal caregiver communication channels [25, 146, 204–206].

7  Conclusion
In conclusion, developing artificial practical wisdom 
grounded explicitly in compassion promises an ethically 
robust approach to AI alignment and safety. The present 
article’s discussions on practical wisdom and AI alignment, 
ethical analyses of suffering and compassion, and concrete 
operational examples in healthcare have shown that integrat­
ing compassion into AI can enhance ethical deliberation and 
decision-making capabilities and directly address concerns 
about AI-driven suffering. By integrating Aristotelian virtue 
ethics with Buddhist and consequentialist understandings of 
suffering, an emphasis on compassion and practical wisdom 
can better orient AI systems toward morally prudent delib­
eration and decision-making, also directly addressing core 
ethical concerns about increased human suffering due to AI 
advancement. Operationalizing practical wisdom and com­
passion in AI systems, particularly through clear computa­
tional criteria and measurable behaviors, is both necessary 
and feasible. Recent advances in natural language process­
ing, affective computing, and robotic care technologies 
illustrate practical pathways for realizing compassionate AI. 
Healthcare provides an ideal environment to test and refine 
these technologies, offering measurable improvements in 
patient outcomes and caregiver experiences, though requir­
ing careful ethical considerations in its deployment. Future 
research should further develop robust metrics for compas­
sion in AI, rigorously evaluate compassionate AI across 
diverse cultural and operational contexts, and explore incor­
porating additional virtues, such as justice and veracity. By 
deliberately cultivating compassion and practical wisdom 
within AI systems, we can better ensure that AI advance­
ments genuinely enhance human flourishing and ethical 
engagement in complex real-world situations.

Practical wisdom is important in such a scenario. Imag­
ine Ann realizes it is her husband’s birthday and starts to 
bake a birthday cake. A response to assist would be compas­
sionate, but that ignores the potential suffering that might 
be caused due to Ann’s diabetes or Bob returning home to 
find his mother with dementia baking a birthday cake for 
his deceased father. By embedding context-sensitive moral 
discernment and compassionate motivation into the sys­
tem’s design, the AI supports prudent responses that go 
beyond conventional alignment strategies based solely on 
rule-following or preference satisfaction. Moral delibera­
tion requires integrating moral sensitivity (detecting Ann’s 
distress), moral adjudication and judgment (determin­
ing contextually appropriate responses), moral motivation 
(prioritizing alleviation of suffering), and affective-cog­
nitive regulation (balancing responses across competing 
demands). The motivational orientation to reduce suffer­
ing and support flourishing helps mitigate concerns raised 
earlier in the paper about emotional manipulation, depen­
dency, and authenticity. Because the AI acts not by simulat­
ing emotions, but by being oriented toward compassionate 
goals with clear explanations of its actions, it facilitates user 
trust and reinforces Bob’s and Ann’s autonomy and essen­
tial freedoms. Motivation-driven action selection avoids 
shallow affective mimicry and grounds AI responses in 
moral deliberation aligned with virtue ethics. It is capacities 
such as this that AGI research hopes to achieve to overcome 
the fragility of narrow AI and align with human values, but 
which requires artificial practical wisdom instead.
Realistically, healthcare systems will likely incorporate 
a mix of AI technologies that augment positive caregiv­
ing and those that exacerbate systemic harms. Navigat­
ing that complexity with AI systems that can quickly and 
effectively identify behaviors leading to increased suffer­
ing and respond with appropriate recommendations and/or 
interventions can benefit patients, their family, professional 
caregiving, and healthcare systems. Like any high-risk AI 
application, development, testing, and deployment must 
be undertaken carefully, but a pressing and growing need 
to increase compassion in technology-infused care already 
exists. Compassionate AI with prudent deliberation and a 
focus on alleviating unnecessary suffering and increasing 
well-being can improve healthcare and set a foundation for 
future, more general compassionate and prudent AI systems.
