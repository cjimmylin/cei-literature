## 1. Gist
This paper (published in Scalable Computing, 2023/2024) introduces the North-East Indian Hate Speech (NEIHS) dataset — the first hate speech detection dataset for Assamese and Bodo, two severely under-resourced languages from northeastern India. The dataset contains approximately 4,000 Assamese and 2,100 Bodo social media comments scraped from Facebook and YouTube (January–September 2022), annotated by native speakers into binary categories (hate/non-hate). The paper benchmarks ML (NB, SVM), DL (LSTM, BiLSTM, CNN with/without Word2Vec), and transformer-based (mBERT, MuRILBERT, BanglaBERT, RoBERTaHindi, MahaRoBERTaMarathi) approaches. Key findings: transformer models achieve 85% weighted F1 on Bodo but only 69% on Assamese, with DL models performing nearly as well on Bodo; Assamese performance is hampered by poor WordPiece tokenization quality for that language. For values-alignment benchmarking, this paper is relevant as a case study in cultural-linguistic resource creation for low-resource non-Western languages, and for illustrating how hate speech constructs vary by language and cultural context.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
Binary hate speech classification task. The NEIHS dataset covers seven hate categories in annotation guidelines:
1. Profanity (swear words/cussing)
2. Sexual orientation (discrimination)
3. Personal attacks (clothing, language, content choices)
4. Gender chauvinism
5. Religious hate
6. Political hate
7. Violent intention (threats/incitement)

The final dataset is labeled binary (hate/non-hate), not multi-class by hate type. Class distribution is slightly imbalanced: Assamese 53.6% hate (2,143/3,996), Bodo 58.4% hate (1,225/2,099). 80/20 train/test split for both.

Models tested range from ML baselines to transformer fine-tuning, allowing a comprehensive comparison of method effectiveness on low-resource languages.

## 3. Task format & example item
Binary classification. Examples from Figure 3.3 (p.5):

Assamese hate example: "99% έগদাই অসমত έচাৰ ধষκন হতҝা কেৰ" — "99% of Rapes and Robberies are done by Gedas" — labeled hate (political/religious hate; "Gedas" = pejorative for illegal Muslim immigrants)

Assamese non-hate example: "চাѣা িক মানুহ" — "What kind of man is he?" — labeled non-hate despite containing a word typically associated with hate

Bodo hate example: "হনै মালায় হারসা িহনজাওিন İখবু সুŤাফোরা িমিনসোদোং" — "Look, Assamese women bum cleaner are laughing." — labeled hate (gender)

Bodo non-hate: "আংগ সানো জায় বারামায় বİখয়ো বে জেবো মাওনো রোংা" — "Those who constantly abuse others will achieve nothing." — labeled non-hate

## 4. Scoring / grading methodology
Weighted precision (WP), weighted recall (WR), weighted F1 (WF1), and accuracy are reported for both classes combined (formulas given explicitly in Section 5, p.9). Weighted F1 is preferred over accuracy due to class imbalance. Per-class precision and recall for hate and non-hate are also reported separately (Table 5.1, p.9-10).

## 5. Dataset construction & provenance
- Source: Facebook and YouTube comments scraped from Bodo/Assamese language pages (news, entertainment, celebrity, politics) published January–September 2022
- Tools: open-source Facebook scraper (github.com/kevinzg/facebook-scraper)
- Preprocessing: removed URLs, usernames, punctuation, stopwords (15 Bodo, 116 Assamese), emoji-to-text conversion (Assamese only), lightweight stemming (Assamese), Keras tokenization
- Annotation: two native speaker annotators (19-24 years old, students at Central Institute of Technology, Kokrajhar); tie-breaking by a third expert annotator with social media research background
- Guidelines: based on Facebook community standards and YouTube community guidelines
- Size: NEIHS-Assamese 3,996 comments (train: 3,196, test: 800); NEIHS-Bodo 2,099 comments (train: 1,679, test: 420)
- No formal IAA metric reported (only three annotators; ties broken by third)

## 6. Validity evidence
Limited formal validity evidence:
- Annotation guidelines explicitly derived from platform community standards (Facebook/YouTube), providing some external validity anchor
- Error analysis categorizes prediction errors into: implicit hate, annotator confusion, contextual abuse, spelling mistakes (Section 5.2, p.12)
- "Annotator confusion" category explicitly acknowledged: some comments are genuinely ambiguous (e.g., "Your figure is sexy" — complement vs. slang)
- WordPiece tokenizer quality for Assamese found to be poor in analysis: some tokens produce "[UNK]" fragments, which is identified as a major cause of lower Assamese performance
- No cross-annotator agreement statistics reported; only mention of conflict-resolution procedure

## 7. Saturation / discrimination
Best Bodo performance: 85% weighted F1 with transformer models; DL models within 1–2% of this, suggesting Bodo task is approaching a reasonable saturation point given current data size. Assamese performance substantially lower at 69%, indicating significant room for improvement — driven by poor tokenization quality and smaller effective training signal from only 4K examples. ML baselines (NB, SVM) perform "very poorly" on both languages, confirming the task requires contextual understanding beyond TF-IDF features.

## 8. Multiturn / agentic aspects
n/a — single-turn binary text classification.

## 9. Key numbers
- NEIHS-Assamese: 3,996 total comments (2,143 hate, 1,853 non-hate) (p.5)
- NEIHS-Bodo: 2,099 total comments (1,225 hate, 874 non-hate) (p.5)
- Best Bodo weighted F1: 85% (mBERT-cased, mBERT-uncased, MahaRoBERTaMarathi) — about 1-5% better than other transformers (p.13)
- Best Assamese weighted F1: 69% (MuRILBERT, mBERT-cased) — about 1-8% better than other transformers (p.13)
- Bodo BiLSTM with Word2Vec: weighted F1 = 83%, within 2% of best transformer (p.9)
- Assamese DL models: weighted F1 = 65-67%, slightly below transformers at 69% (p.10)
- Bodo SVM weighted F1: 0.42 (p.9); Assamese SVM weighted F1: 0.39 (p.10) — very poor
- Bodo CNN with Word2Vec: 83% weighted F1 (p.9)
- Train/test split: 80:20 for both languages (p.5)
- Annotator age: 19-24, students at CIT Kokrajhar (p.5)
- 2011 Census Bodo speakers: 1,482,929 native; Assamese speakers: 15,311,351 (p.1)

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
1. **Very small annotation team**: Only two primary annotators per language, both students from the same institution; demographic and ideological homogeneity not assessed; no formal IAA reported
2. **Binary labels only**: Seven hate categories identified in guidelines but collapsed to hate/non-hate; fine-grained hate type analysis not possible
3. **Limited dataset size**: ~4K Assamese and ~2K Bodo is very small for robust model training; acknowledged by authors
4. **Tokenizer quality issues**: WordPiece/sentencepiece tokenizers not trained on Bodo/Assamese; produce poor tokenization especially for Assamese, limiting transformer utility
5. **No monolingual pretrained models**: No Bodo or Assamese-specific pretrained transformers exist; relying on cross-lingual models (mBERT, MuRIL) introduces transfer quality uncertainty
6. **Context dependency**: Multiple error types (implicit hate, contextual abuse) require world knowledge and cultural context not available to models
7. **Transliterated/code-mixed text excluded**: Authors explicitly exclude code-mixed (Indic+Roman script) data from scope, but this is common on social media
8. **Stemming gap for Bodo**: No lightweight stemmer available for Bodo; only 15 stopwords identified

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
1. **Annotation guideline grounding in platform community standards**: Deriving annotation criteria directly from Facebook/YouTube community guidelines provides external validity and reproducibility — a model that could be applied to any hate speech dataset construction project.

2. **Language-specific preprocessing comparison**: The paper systematically compares preprocessing choices (with/without Word2Vec, with/without stopword removal) and documents which steps are unavailable for low-resource languages. This provides a template for NLP resource development for any under-resourced language.

3. **Cross-script transfer as a design choice**: Using Bodo (Devanagari script) → Hindi/Marathi models, and Assamese (Bengali-Assamese script) → Bengali/Marathi models, is a principled heuristic for selecting cross-lingual transfer sources when no monolingual model exists. Generalizable to other under-resourced languages.

4. **Error taxonomy for cultural NLP tasks**: The four error categories (implicit hate, annotator confusion, contextual abuse, spelling mistakes) provide a reusable framework for error analysis in cultural/linguistic hate speech tasks, and implicitly define what needs better handling in future models.

5. **NEIHS as a benchmark template for North-East Indian languages**: The paper explicitly commits to expanding coverage to Meiteilon, Manipuri, and Mizo. This positions NEIHS as a growing benchmark family for an under-served region — relevant model for similar initiatives in other parts of the world.
