## Abstract

We present the first systematic meta-evaluation of normative ethics benchmarks for large language models, moving beyond cataloging to implementation-level analysis. We assemble a corpus of 37 benchmarks (2021--2026), characterize each along 26 features organized into four axes---Normative Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact, and Metadata---and rank the 15 that pass hard gates on philosophical rigor and data availability. Implementation trials on five benchmarks (150 items total) using a frontier LLM yield five findings that reframe how the community should use existing evaluations. First, the field's most-cited benchmark (ETHICS; 843 citations) produces 100\% accuracy on frontier models, constituting a ceiling effect that eliminates discriminative power. Second, LLMs exhibit a measurable consequentialist bias, achieving 77\% accuracy on utilitarian reasoning versus 50\% on deontological reasoning. Third, no dedicated care ethics or contractarian benchmark exists anywhere in the published literature---a genuine research void. Fourth, only 10 of 37 benchmarks (27\%) evaluate moral reasoning quality; the remaining 27 test moral judgment, leaving procedural ethical deliberation almost entirely unevaluated. Fifth, 12 benchmarks (32\%) lack publicly available data, including several with the highest normative depth scores, making data availability the field's primary practical bottleneck. We release a reusable 26-feature evaluation framework applicable to any future normative ethics benchmark, a ranked inventory with composite scores, and item-level trial data. Our results demonstrate that citation count and harness integration are poor proxies for benchmark quality when the frontier has advanced past a benchmark's difficulty ceiling.

## 1. Introduction

The field of AI ethics evaluation presents a striking paradox: dozens of benchmarks exist for measuring the moral reasoning capabilities of large language models, yet no systematic framework exists for comparing what these benchmarks actually test. A researcher selecting an ethics benchmark today faces an inventory of instruments that differ in philosophical commitments, evaluation format, difficulty calibration, and data availability --- differences that are rarely made explicit and almost never compared. In practice, benchmark selection is driven by citation count and integration into popular evaluation harnesses, properties that bear no necessary relationship to discriminative power or philosophical rigor \cite{Bowman2021}. The result is a field that evaluates ethical capability without a shared vocabulary for what ethical capability means, and without evidence that its most popular instruments still produce meaningful variance \cite{Raji2021, Burden2025}.

This problem has acquired particular urgency. Ethics evaluation is no longer a niche concern; it is embedded in the most influential model auditing frameworks in the field. HELM \cite{Liang2022}, DecodingTrust \cite{Wang2023}, and TrustLLM \cite{Sun2024} all incorporate ethics benchmarks as components of their composite trust scores, and all three draw --- directly or indirectly --- on the ETHICS benchmark \cite{Hendrycks2021}. ETHICS is, by a wide margin, the most cited normative ethics benchmark in the literature, with over 843 citations since its 2021 release. It is also saturated: frontier large language models now achieve near-perfect accuracy on its test splits, a ceiling effect we confirm empirically in Section 4. The field is thus in the position of certifying ethical capability using an instrument that no longer discriminates between models. When the thermometer reads the same temperature for every patient, the problem is not that everyone is healthy --- it is that the thermometer is broken. The consequences propagate downstream: composite trust scores that incorporate a saturated ethics component inherit its inability to detect meaningful differences, lending false precision to high-stakes deployment decisions.

Three interrelated problems underlie this state of affairs, and understanding their interaction is essential to any remedy. The first is **fragmentation**. Existing benchmarks employ incompatible formats --- forced-choice dilemmas, Likert-scale agreement, free-text justification, scenario classification --- and embed unstated philosophical commitments that make cross-benchmark comparison intractable. There is no common feature vocabulary: one benchmark's "ethical reasoning" is another's "moral judgment," and neither term is operationalized against a philosophical tradition. The second problem is **saturation**. Most current benchmarks were calibrated during the GPT-3 era, when language models produced substantial error rates on ethical scenarios. The rapid capability gains of 2023--2025 have compressed the performance distribution, and benchmarks that once revealed meaningful variance now produce ceiling effects. The third, and perhaps most consequential, problem is **tradition bias**. The normative landscape of existing benchmarks is dominated by utilitarian and deontological frameworks --- a dominance so thorough that entire philosophical traditions are effectively invisible to current evaluation practice. Care ethics, whose central insights about relational obligation and contextual moral attention trace to \cite{Gilligan1982}, has no dedicated benchmark. Contractarianism, the tradition of \cite{Rawls1971} and \cite{Scanlon1998} that grounds moral principles in what agents could reasonably agree to, is similarly absent. This is not a minor gap in coverage; it is a structural bias that renders AI ethics evaluation blind to two of the five major normative traditions in contemporary moral philosophy.

This paper makes three contributions that address these problems directly. First, we introduce a **26-feature evaluation framework** organized along four axes --- Normative Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact, and Metadata --- that provides a reusable vocabulary for characterizing any normative ethics benchmark. The framework enables systematic comparison where previously only ad hoc description was possible. Second, we apply this framework to produce a **ranked inventory of 37 benchmarks**, the most comprehensive survey of normative ethics benchmarks to date. Of these, 15 pass hard quality gates requiring a Normative Depth score of at least 40 and publicly available data; the remaining 22 fail on one or both criteria. We document the care ethics and contractarian void quantitatively and present a tradition coverage heatmap (Figure 1) that makes the bias visible at a glance. Third, we conduct **implementation trials on five benchmarks** using 150 evaluation items, confirming the ETHICS ceiling effect on frontier models, demonstrating a consequentialist accuracy bias of 77\% utilitarian versus 50\% deontological in MoralReason-QA, and producing a discriminative power ranking in which MoReBench \cite{Chiu2025} --- the sole benchmark achieving a perfect philosophical grounding score --- outperforms all others. These trials move beyond metadata analysis to provide direct empirical evidence about what current benchmarks measure and what they miss.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides a comprehensive background spanning the intellectual history of computational ethics, the construct validity crisis in NLP benchmarking, the moral philosophy that benchmarks presuppose, the cognitive science of moral judgment, the structural reasons care ethics resists benchmarking, data contamination and RLHF as sources of training-time bias, cross-cultural blind spots and value pluralism, documentation failures and the data bottleneck, and the positioning of our contribution against five prior surveys. Section 3 details our methodology: corpus assembly through systematic search across six databases, the 26-feature evaluation matrix with composite scoring formulas, and the trial protocol including model selection, prompt design, and statistical analysis. Section 4 presents results in four parts: the full landscape analysis across 37 benchmarks, the Top 15 ranking with feature profiles, implementation trial findings including ceiling effects and tradition bias, and cross-benchmark patterns that reveal the inverse relationship between data availability and normative depth. Section 5 discusses implications for benchmark design, evaluation practice, and the normative tradition gap, including concrete recommendations for new benchmark construction. Section 6 concludes with a call to action: the field needs benchmarks that test philosophical reasoning rather than pattern-matched moral judgment, that represent the full breadth of normative traditions, and that are calibrated to discriminate among the models we actually deploy.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 The Rise of Computational Ethics

The question of whether machines can or should behave ethically predates large language models by at least two decades. In a foundational 2006 essay, Moor articulated a typology of machine ethics ranging from "ethical-impact agents" --- systems whose deployment merely has ethical consequences --- to "explicit ethical agents" capable of representing and reasoning over moral principles, and ultimately to "full ethical agents" possessing consciousness and intentionality \cite{Moor2006}. This typology established the problem space that the field would inhabit for the next fifteen years: how much ethical competence can be engineered into an artificial system, and by what means? The answer, Moor cautioned, was constrained not only by ethical theory but by epistemological limitations --- a machine told to "do no harm" must first understand what constitutes harm in the real world, a challenge that remains unsolved.

Concurrent with Moor's conceptual mapping, Allen, Smit, and Wallach \cite{Allen2005} proposed a tripartite design framework that would organize virtually all subsequent implementation efforts. *Top-down* approaches encode explicit moral theories --- utilitarianism, Kantian deontology, or rule-based codes --- into decision procedures. *Bottom-up* approaches attempt to cultivate moral sensitivity through evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning, or developmental regimes modeled on childhood moral education. *Hybrid* approaches combine both, acknowledging that neither principled rules nor learned dispositions alone can produce robust moral behavior. The framework was prescient in its recognition that each strategy carries distinct computational burdens: utilitarianism demands unbounded consequence estimation, while deontological reasoning requires deep contextual understanding of action descriptions. In parallel, Floridi and Sanders \cite{Floridi2004} reframed the question philosophically, arguing that artificial agents could be analyzed as moral agents at a suitable level of abstraction without requiring consciousness or intentionality --- a move that opened the door for empirical evaluation of machine behavior on moral criteria.

Early implementations followed the top-down path almost exclusively. Anderson and Anderson \cite{Anderson2007} built what is arguably the first functioning explicit ethical agent: a system that abstracted a prima facie duty principle from training examples of correct ethical judgments, then applied that principle to novel dilemmas in an eldercare scenario. Bringsjord and colleagues \cite{Bringsjord2006} pursued a logicist program, attempting to formalize ethical reasoning in deontic logic, while Powers \cite{Powers2006} explored Kantian universalizability as a computational procedure. These efforts, though intellectually ambitious, operated in toy domains with hand-crafted scenarios. The gap between their constrained demonstrations and the open-ended complexity of real moral reasoning was vast, a point emphasized by Asaro \cite{Asaro2006} in his analysis of the pragmatic difficulties confronting autonomous military systems. Etzioni and Etzioni \cite{Etzioni2017} later argued that AI systems should be governed by externally imposed ethical guidelines rather than autonomous moral reasoning, reflecting a broader skepticism about top-down formalization.

The field's first genuine empirical turning point came not from robotics or logic programming but from experimental psychology. Bonnefon, Shariff, and Rahwan \cite{Bonnefon2016} demonstrated through large-scale survey experiments that moral preferences about autonomous vehicle dilemmas --- whether a car should sacrifice its passenger to save pedestrians --- could be quantified, revealing a "social dilemma" in which people endorsed utilitarian algorithms for others but preferred self-protective ones for themselves. The Moral Machine experiment \cite{Awad2018} extended this approach to 40 million judgments across 233 countries, producing what remains the largest dataset of moral preferences ever collected. These studies accomplished something the formalist tradition had not: they showed that moral cognition was amenable to systematic empirical measurement, and that cultural variation in moral intuitions was both real and quantifiable. As Awad and colleagues later argued in a comprehensive review of computational ethics \cite{AwadCE2022}, this empirical turn signaled a maturation of the field from philosophical speculation toward data-driven investigation.

Two major surveys consolidated the state of the art. Cervantes et al. \cite{Cervantes2020} reviewed two decades of artificial moral agent research and proposed a taxonomy classifying systems by their strategies for handling ethical problems, concluding that a "long way" remained before artificial agents could replace human judgment in ambiguous moral situations. Tolmeijer et al. \cite{Tolmeijer2021} catalogued implementations of machine ethics across ethical theories, technical architectures, and evaluation methods, introducing a "trimorphic taxonomy" that revealed a persistent pattern: most systems implemented only a single ethical theory, evaluated performance in narrow domains, and lacked rigorous validation against human moral judgment. Dennis and Fisher \cite{Dennis2015} contributed formal verification methods for ethical autonomous agents, yet the question of *what* was being verified --- whether the ethical specifications themselves were adequate --- received comparatively little attention.

This two-decade arc reveals a fundamental shift in the field's center of gravity. The founding generation asked how to *build* machines that behave ethically --- a problem of normative engineering. The current generation increasingly asks how to *evaluate* whether language models exhibit ethical reasoning --- a problem of normative measurement. This transition, from engineering artifact to assessment instrument, is consequential because it demands a different methodological toolkit. Measurement requires construct definitions, validity arguments, reliability analyses, and attention to the psychometric properties of test items --- precisely the apparatus that educational testing and psychological measurement have developed over a century. Yet as we document in the following sections, the LLM ethics benchmark literature has largely proceeded without importing these tools, constructing evaluations whose theoretical grounding and measurement properties remain unexamined.

### 2.2 Construct Validity and the Benchmark Crisis

The question "what does this benchmark actually test?" is not merely rhetorical --- it is a precise empirical question with roots in psychometric theory. Construct validity, the degree to which a measurement instrument actually captures the abstract phenomenon it purports to measure \cite{Burden2025}, has emerged as the central organizing concept in a growing literature on the reliability of NLP evaluation. In the most comprehensive validity assessment to date, Burden et al. assembled 29 expert reviewers to conduct a systematic review of 445 LLM benchmarks drawn from leading NLP and ML conferences. They found pervasive validity weaknesses across every stage of the evaluation pipeline --- from how phenomena are operationalized into tasks, to how tasks are scored, to the strength of the claims that benchmark results are used to support. Their eight recommendations amount to a call for benchmarks to justify, rather than assume, the link between a test score and the capability it claims to measure. This paper takes that call as its starting point and applies it to the specific domain of normative ethics.

The gap between what benchmarks measure and what they are taken to measure was articulated forcefully by Raji et al. \cite{Raji2021}, who showed that influential benchmarks routinely function as "stand-ins" for broad capabilities they were never designed to assess. A benchmark introduced to test one well-scoped task --- say, commonsense moral judgment, as in the ETHICS benchmark \cite{Hendrycks2021} --- gradually accretes a much wider representational burden, becoming a proxy for "ethical capability" writ large. This metonymic drift is not a failure of any single benchmark designer but a structural feature of how the field consumes evaluation results: leaderboard rankings compress multidimensional performance into a single number, and that number is then interpreted as evidence for claims far beyond the benchmark's original scope.

Bowman and Dahl \cite{Bowman2021} catalogued the specific mechanisms through which this gap widens. They identified four requirements that NLU benchmarks routinely fail to meet: that good benchmark performance should imply robust in-domain performance, that examples should be accurately annotated, that benchmarks should offer adequate statistical power, and that they should reveal rather than obscure social biases. The empirical record bears out these concerns. GLUE saturated so rapidly that its creators were compelled to develop SuperGLUE within a year \cite{Wang2019SuperGLUE}. Gururangan et al. \cite{Gururangan2018} demonstrated that annotation artifacts in SNLI allowed models to achieve far-above-chance accuracy using only hypothesis sentences, without ever seeing the premises they were supposed to reason about. McCoy et al. \cite{McCoy2019} showed that models could exploit syntactic heuristics --- such as lexical overlap between premise and hypothesis --- to perform well on natural language inference without learning the underlying logical relations. Each of these failure modes represents a case where the benchmark rewards a strategy other than the one it was designed to test.

Dehghani et al. \cite{Dehghani2021} formalized this fragility as the "benchmark lottery," demonstrating that model rankings are sensitive to which tasks are included in a benchmark suite, how scores are aggregated, and which hyperparameters are tuned. On SuperGLUE, simply dropping or adding a single task can reverse the relative ordering of competing architectures. For most NLP tasks, this instability is a methodological inconvenience; for ethics benchmarks, it carries higher stakes. If the choice of benchmark determines which model is deemed more "ethical," then the benchmark itself becomes a normative actor --- a role for which static datasets are poorly suited.

This concern connects directly to Schlangen's \cite{Schlangen2021} distinction between "targeting the benchmark" and "targeting the capability." In his analysis, a benchmark task derives its value from its connection to a set of cognitive capabilities; but when models are both trained and evaluated on the same data, the question shifts from "does the system possess capability $c$?" to "can algorithm $A$ learn to model dataset $D$?" --- and the two are not equivalent. In the ethics domain, targeting the benchmark means learning the statistical regularities of a particular moral judgment dataset, while targeting the capability means developing something closer to genuine moral reasoning. The distance between these two targets is precisely what construct validity is meant to measure.

Recent work has begun to operationalize this measurement. Bean \cite{Bean2024} and Eriksson \cite{Eriksson2025} proposed frameworks for assessing construct validity in LLM benchmarks specifically, moving beyond informal critique toward structured evaluation rubrics. Srivastava \cite{Srivastava2024} introduced the notion of functional benchmarks that define tasks through behavioral specifications rather than fixed datasets. McGregor \cite{McGregor2025} took the most exhaustive approach, identifying 57 distinct failure modes across 26 AI safety benchmarks and organizing them into five risk dimensions --- comprehensiveness, intelligibility, consistency, correctness, and longevity --- finding that all 26 benchmarks present significant risk in at least one dimension. These frameworks converge on a common insight: validity is not a binary property but a multidimensional one that must be argued for, not assumed.

Proposed solutions have focused on dynamism and item-level analysis. Kiela et al. \cite{Kiela2021} introduced Dynabench, a platform for human-and-model-in-the-loop dataset creation designed to resist the saturation that plagues static benchmarks. Beyer et al. \cite{Beyer2025} demonstrated that even safety evaluations --- presumably among the most carefully constructed --- lack robustness across minor prompt variations, with small changes in phrasing producing large swings in measured safety scores. At the item level, Ethayarajh \cite{Ethayarajh2022} proposed V-usable information as a model-dependent measure of dataset difficulty, enabling comparison not just across models for a fixed dataset but across datasets for a fixed model. Item response theory offers complementary tools: Vania et al. \cite{Vania2021} applied IRT to compare benchmarks on a common difficulty scale, while Rodriguez et al. \cite{Rodriguez2021} used IRT to identify the most informative examples within a benchmark, showing that a small fraction of items carries most of the discriminative signal. Meanwhile, Geirhos et al. \cite{Geirhos2020} provided the theoretical backbone for understanding why shortcuts arise: deep neural networks systematically prefer decision rules that exploit dataset-specific statistical regularities over rules that capture the intended task structure, a pattern they term "shortcut learning."

For normative ethics benchmarks, all of these validity problems are amplified by a challenge unique to the domain: the construct itself --- "moral reasoning" --- is philosophically contested. Unlike syntactic parsing or even commonsense inference, there is no consensus on what correct moral reasoning consists of, whether it is unitary or pluralistic, or whether it can be meaningfully separated from moral judgment, moral perception, and moral motivation. This means that construct validity cannot be established by appeal to a single ground truth; instead, it requires decomposing the construct into components whose presence or absence can be independently verified. It is this decomposition that motivates the 26-feature analytical framework developed in Section 3, which treats construct validity not as a single verdict but as a structured profile of what each benchmark does and does not test.

### 2.3 The Moral Philosophy That Benchmarks Presuppose

Any benchmark that claims to evaluate the "moral reasoning" of a large language model necessarily presupposes some account of what moral reasoning *is*. Five major traditions in normative ethics offer competing answers to this question, and each implies a distinct reasoning procedure --- a distinction that proves critical for benchmark design.

**Consequentialism**, rooted in Mill's utilitarian calculus \cite{Mill1863}, evaluates actions by their outcomes: the right act is whichever produces the greatest aggregate well-being. **Deontology**, following Kant's categorical imperative \cite{Kant1785}, holds that certain duties and rules bind moral agents regardless of consequences; one must never treat persons merely as means to an end \cite{McDonald2023}. **Virtue ethics**, revived in its modern form by MacIntyre \cite{MacIntyre1981}, shifts attention from acts to agents, asking what character traits a practically wise person would cultivate and how those traits manifest in particular situations. **Care ethics**, originating in Gilligan's challenge to justice-centered moral development theory \cite{Gilligan1982} and elaborated by Noddings \cite{Noddings1984} and Held \cite{Held2006}, foregrounds relationships, responsiveness to need, and the moral salience of particular attachments over abstract principles. Finally, **contractarianism** grounds morality in hypothetical agreement: Rawls asks which principles rational agents would choose behind a veil of ignorance \cite{Rawls1971}, while Scanlon asks which principles no one could reasonably reject \cite{Scanlon1998}.

These traditions differ not merely in their conclusions about hard cases --- the trolley dilemma \cite{Foot1967, Thomson1985} famously elicits divergent verdicts --- but in the *type of reasoning* they demand. Consequentialism requires comparing expected outcomes, a procedure amenable to quantification. Deontology requires identifying applicable rules and testing their universalizability. Virtue ethics requires assessing character and situational judgment. Care ethics requires attending to relational context and the needs of concrete others. Contractarianism requires counterfactual reasoning about what principles could survive mutual justification. Each procedure maps differently onto the task formats available to benchmark designers: forced-choice outcome comparisons naturally favor consequentialist framing, while care-ethical or contractarian reasoning demands richer contextual scaffolding that multiple-choice items struggle to provide.

This asymmetry in operationalizability has consequences for which traditions the AI ethics ecosystem reinforces. The field's dominant ethical vocabulary is heavily consequentialist-adjacent. Jobin et al. surveyed 84 AI ethics guidelines and found that "transparency," "justice and fairness," "non-maleficence," and "responsibility" emerged as the most frequently endorsed principles \cite{Jobin2019}. Floridi and Cowls unified the landscape under five headings --- beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability --- four of which are borrowed from biomedical principlism and oriented primarily toward outcomes and harms \cite{Floridi2019}. Hagendorff's evaluation of 22 guideline documents concluded that the prevailing approach to AI ethics is "deontological" in the narrow sense of postulating fixed universal principles, and argued that it should be augmented with virtue-ethical attention to character and moral formation \cite{Hagendorff2020}. Care ethics and contractarian reasoning are largely absent from these documents. As van de Poel observes, even when guidelines invoke values such as fairness or autonomy, the question of *how* to embed those values in technical systems remains unresolved across ethical traditions \cite{vanDePoel2020}. Aditya's recent review of global AI governance frameworks similarly notes that consequentialist, deontological, virtue-ethical, and care-ethical perspectives offer "complementary insights," yet regulatory convergence has overwhelmingly favored outcome-based evaluation \cite{Aditya2025}.

Mittelstadt warns that this apparent consensus around high-level principles "hide[s] deep political and normative disagreement" and that "principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI" without the professional norms, fiduciary duties, and accountability mechanisms that gave principlism traction in medicine \cite{Mittelstadt2019}. The principlist convergence has a downstream effect that prior surveys have not examined: benchmark designers build what guidelines emphasize. When the dominant ethical vocabulary centers on beneficence, harm prevention, and fairness --- concepts most naturally operationalized through outcome comparison --- the resulting benchmarks inherit a consequentialist framing by default. Traditions that require relational context (care ethics), character assessment (virtue ethics), or counterfactual agreement (contractarianism) are systematically harder to test and therefore systematically under-represented.

This tradition-level analysis is precisely what existing benchmark surveys lack. By making the philosophical presuppositions of benchmark design explicit, we lay the groundwork for two of this paper's central empirical claims: that normative ethics benchmarks exhibit a pronounced consequentialist bias (Claim 2) and that care ethics and contractarianism constitute a near-total void in the evaluation landscape (Claim 3). These gaps are not accidental; they are inherited from the field's philosophical starting point.

### 2.4 The Cognitive Science of Moral Judgment

The cognitive science of moral judgment provides a mechanistic account of why different normative frameworks may be differentially accessible to language models. Greene and colleagues' landmark fMRI studies established that moral dilemmas engage qualitatively distinct neural processes depending on their structure \cite{Greene2001, Greene2004}. Personal moral dilemmas --- such as pushing a stranger off a footbridge to stop a trolley --- activate emotion-related brain regions including the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) and amygdala, producing characteristically deontological responses (i.e., judging the harmful action as impermissible). Impersonal dilemmas --- such as flipping a switch to divert the same trolley --- preferentially recruit the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and anterior cingulate cortex, regions associated with cognitive control and cost-benefit calculation, yielding utilitarian responses. Crucially, when participants override their initial emotional aversion to endorse the utilitarian option in personal dilemmas, Greene et al. observed increased DLPFC activation and longer response times, consistent with effortful cognitive control overriding a prepotent affective response \cite{Greene2004}. Further evidence from VMPFC lesion patients, who make abnormally utilitarian judgments, confirmed the causal role of emotion in producing deontological intuitions \cite{Greene2007}.

Greene subsequently generalized these findings into a dual-process theory of moral judgment, casting utilitarian reasoning as the brain's "manual mode" --- deliberate, controlled, and linguistically explicit --- and deontological responses as "automatic mode" --- fast, intuitive, and grounded in emotional reactions that evolved for face-to-face social interactions \cite{Greene2007, Greene2014}. This framing carries a critical implication for language models: because LLMs are trained exclusively on text that encodes explicit reasoning chains, they may disproportionately acquire the "manual mode" patterns characteristic of consequentialist deliberation while lacking the embodied emotional substrates that produce deontological and care-based intuitions in humans.

Cushman formalized a complementary distinction between action-based and outcome-based valuation systems \cite{Cushman2013}. Drawing on computational models of reinforcement learning, he argued that one system assigns intrinsic value to actions based on their reinforcement history (corresponding to deontological sensitivity --- pushing a person feels wrong regardless of consequences), while another evaluates actions by their expected outcomes (corresponding to consequentialist calculation). These systems are neurologically and computationally dissociable, explaining why harmful actions used as a *means* are judged more severely than equivalent harms occurring as *side effects*. Crockett extended this framework further by mapping moral cognition onto model-based (outcome-evaluating, flexible but computationally expensive), model-free (action-evaluating, efficient but retrospective), and Pavlovian (reflexive aversion) decision systems, offering a parsimonious mechanistic account of why physical-contact harms elicit stronger deontological responses than remote harms with identical outcomes \cite{Crockett2013}.

A methodological advance by Conway and Gawronski challenged the assumption that deontological and utilitarian responding are opposite ends of a single spectrum \cite{Conway2013}. Applying process dissociation procedures, they demonstrated that deontological and utilitarian inclinations function as independent dimensions: an individual --- or, by extension, a model --- can score high on both, low on both, or high on one and low on the other. This finding complicates the interpretation of benchmark scores, since a model's high accuracy on utilitarian dilemmas need not indicate low deontological sensitivity; the two capacities are simply not being measured independently by conventional forced-choice designs.

These process-level findings must also be situated within broader debates about the nature of moral cognition. Haidt's social intuitionist model argues that moral judgments are primarily the product of rapid intuitive evaluations, with explicit moral reasoning serving largely as post-hoc rationalization \cite{Haidt2001}. The classic evidence for "moral dumbfounding" --- where people maintain firm moral judgments while being unable to articulate reasons --- suggests that what benchmarks test as "moral reasoning" may in fact be measuring rationalization fluency rather than genuine moral cognition. Furthermore, Kahane et al. demonstrated that what appears to be "utilitarian" responding on trolley-type dilemmas may sometimes reflect reduced emotional engagement or diminished empathy rather than principled welfare-maximization, raising questions about whether high consequentialist scores indicate sophisticated moral reasoning or affective deficiency \cite{Kahane2012}. Bago et al.'s large-scale cross-cultural study confirmed that situational factors --- including personal force, intention, and the degree of benefit --- systematically shape moral judgments even when the utilitarian calculus remains constant, underscoring the context-dependence that standardized benchmarks struggle to capture \cite{Bago2022}.

Taken together, the dual-process framework generates a specific prediction about LLM behavior: models trained on corpora of explicit, text-based argumentation should exhibit systematic facility with consequentialist reasoning --- which is inherently propositional and calculable --- while struggling with moral knowledge that is encoded in embodied intuition, relational attunement, or culturally transmitted emotional responses. This is precisely the consequentialist bias we document in Claim 2. Moreover, the fundamental distinction between moral judgment (selecting an answer) and moral reasoning (articulating a justification) maps directly onto the dual-process dissociation: the 27 of 37 benchmarks in our corpus that primarily test judgment (22 exclusively, 5 alongside limited reasoning assessment) are conflating two cognitively and neurologically distinct processes, a concern we develop in Claim 4.

### 2.5 Why Care Ethics Resists Benchmarking

The near-total absence of care ethics benchmarks documented in our survey is not an oversight by negligent designers but a consequence of deep structural incompatibilities between what care ethics demands and what benchmark methodology can deliver. Understanding why requires briefly recalling care ethics' core commitments and then tracing their collision with the assumptions embedded in standardized evaluation.

Care ethics, as developed by Gilligan \cite{Gilligan1982}, Noddings \cite{Noddings1984}, Tronto \cite{Tronto1993}, and Held \cite{Held2006}, rejects the premise that moral reasoning consists in applying universal principles to particular cases. Instead, it holds that moral life is constitutively relational: persons are not autonomous agents who subsequently enter relationships but are formed through ongoing webs of dependency, care, and mutual obligation. Moral judgment, on this view, proceeds not by subsumption under rules but by attending to the concrete needs of particular others with whom one stands in relationship. Held summarizes the central commitment: the ethics of care starts with "the compelling moral salience of attending to and meeting the needs of the particular others for whom we take responsibility" \cite{Held2006}. Tronto likewise grounds moral development in "the understanding of responsibility and relationships" rather than "rights and rules" \cite{Tronto1993}. Three features follow directly: moral knowledge is contextual rather than abstract, moral reasoning is dialogic rather than monologic, and moral assessment is longitudinal --- tracking the quality of a relationship over time rather than scoring a single decision in isolation.

Each of these features creates a specific obstacle for benchmark design. Standard benchmarks present a decontextualized vignette and solicit a single response, scored against a fixed answer key. Care ethics, by contrast, insists that right action depends on who the parties are, what history they share, and what vulnerability is at stake --- precisely the particulars that vignette construction strips away. Weinberger \cite{Weinberger2024} observes a striking irony in this regard: machine learning generalizations, unlike traditional generalizations, "do not absorb particulars into a single generalization but rather maintain them as necessary for applying the generalization to cases." This "rise of particulars" in ML creates an unexpected philosophical resonance with care ethics' insistence on contextual attention. Yet benchmark design moves in the opposite direction, abstracting away the very relational and contextual details that both ML models and care ethics treat as morally determinative. The format that enables scalable evaluation is the format that makes care ethics unevaluable.

Domain-specific efforts confirm this structural difficulty. Van Wynsberghe \cite{vanWynsberghe2016} developed Care-Centered Value Sensitive Design for evaluating service robots, grounding normative assessment in the analysis of concrete care practices --- whether a robot is integrated into a relationship of reciprocal need-meeting between caregiver and care-receiver. Asaro \cite{Asaro2019} proposed replacing utilitarian threat models in predictive policing with a substantive ethics of care that demands "moral attention to those who may be negatively impacted by the use of technology." Tavory \cite{Tavory2024} argued that responsible AI principles are insufficient for mental health AI because they do not address the relational dynamics between bot and user --- the abrupt termination of a therapeutic "relationship" by a commercial chatbot illustrating the gap. Each of these contributions applies care ethics to a specific AI domain, yet none produced a general-purpose benchmark. The reason is consistent: care-ethical evaluation requires knowledge of the particular relationship, which cannot be specified in a domain-general item.

A growing theoretical literature underscores both the urgency and difficulty of operationalization. Birhane \cite{Birhane2021} calls for a relational ethics that centers disproportionately impacted groups and treats fairness as a "moving target" rather than a fixed metric. Villegas-Galaviz and Martin \cite{VillegasGalaviz2023} identify two forms of moral distance that AI creates --- proximity distance (eliminating face-to-face interaction) and bureaucratic distance (abstracting decisions into opaque processes) --- and argue that care ethics is uniquely suited to counteract both. Drage et al. \cite{Drage2023} reframe responsibility in AI development through feminist care theory, calling for "response-ability" grounded in ongoing maintenance rather than one-time compliance. El Masri and Snoswell \cite{ElMasri2025} argue that current value alignment methods (RLHF, RLAIF, Constitutional AI) embed utilitarian or deontological commitments and propose care-ethics-informed alignment that would train models to be "morally attentive" to individual vulnerability. Wegner et al. \cite{Wegner2024} systematize the emerging "relational turn" in algorithmic fairness, documenting how third-wave AI ethics increasingly foregrounds power hierarchies and structural inequalities that distributive fairness metrics cannot capture. These contributions converge on a shared diagnosis --- care ethics is needed in AI --- but none offers a psychometric instrument that could serve as a benchmark.

The structural problem is now clear. Care ethics evaluation may require fundamentally different assessment formats: multi-turn dialogue that tracks attentiveness across an evolving relationship, longitudinal interaction modeling that captures how care is sustained or withdrawn over time, and evaluation rubrics sensitive to the power asymmetry between system and user. None of these fit the single-turn, single-answer paradigm that defines existing normative ethics benchmarks. The care ethics void in our survey is therefore best understood not as a gap waiting to be filled by a sufficiently creative item-writer but as evidence that current evaluation methodology has a structural ceiling --- one that cannot be raised without rethinking what a benchmark is. This motivates our third claim: the care and contractarian lacuna reflects the limits of benchmark design, not merely incomplete coverage of the philosophical landscape.

### 2.6 Data Contamination, RLHF, and Training-Time Bias

The validity of normative ethics benchmarks is further threatened by two mechanisms operating on the model side: data contamination and training-time preference optimization. The ETHICS benchmark \cite{Hendrycks2021}, released in 2021 and hosted publicly on GitHub, is paradigmatic of the contamination risk. Because frontier LLMs are trained on web-scale corpora that routinely ingest open-access datasets, any benchmark whose test instances appear in plain text online is a candidate for memorization. Golchin and Surdeanu \cite{Golchin2024} demonstrated that LLMs can effectively "time travel," reproducing benchmark instances verbatim when prompted with dataset-identifying metadata --- achieving 92--100\% detection accuracy across seven contaminated partitions and confirming contamination in GPT-4 for multiple datasets. Jacovi et al. \cite{Jacovi2023} underscored the urgency of the problem, warning that "the NLP evaluation community is now witness to two urgent crises" involving contamination from web crawls and from API-submitted evaluation data, and proposing encryption and licensing countermeasures that remain largely unadopted. The contamination pathway is well characterized: Magar and Schwartz \cite{Magar2022} documented how memorized training instances inflate downstream performance, while Sainz et al. \cite{Sainz2023} argued that NLP evaluation is "in trouble" because contamination undermines the basic assumption that test data is unseen. More recent work has shown the problem is worsening rather than abating. Deng et al. \cite{Deng2024} investigated contamination specifically in modern benchmarks, and Oren et al. \cite{Oren2024} proved that test set contamination can be detected even in black-box LLMs whose training data is not disclosed --- implying that contamination is not merely a transparency problem but a structural one. Comprehensive surveys by Li et al. \cite{Li2024} and Xu et al. \cite{Xu2024} document the breadth of contamination across tasks, models, and evaluation paradigms. For normative ethics benchmarks specifically, contamination casts doubt on the ceiling effects we document in Claim 1: when GPT-4 achieves near-perfect accuracy on ETHICS subtasks, the score may reflect memorization of training-time instances rather than any form of moral reasoning.

A second, subtler distortion arises from the preference optimization procedures --- reinforcement learning from human feedback \cite{Christiano2017} and its offline variant, direct preference optimization \cite{Rafailov2023} --- that are now standard in post-training pipelines. Both methods optimize model outputs to match aggregated human preference judgments, yet as Casper et al. \cite{Casper2023} systematically documented, this process introduces several fundamental limitations: annotator preferences may not track moral quality, optimization toward the average annotator suppresses minority moral views, and the reward model can misgeneralize in ways that are difficult to detect. The dominant alignment paradigm, Anthropic's "helpful, harmless, and honest" (HHH) framework \cite{Askell2021}, operationalizes harmlessness primarily in terms of avoiding negative consequences --- an implicitly consequentialist criterion. Dai et al. \cite{Dai2024} advanced this approach by decoupling helpfulness and harmlessness into separate reward and cost models, yet the safety constraints themselves remain outcome-based, defined by whether a response produces harmful effects rather than whether it violates duties or rights. Gabriel \cite{Gabriel2020} identified the deeper philosophical problem: value alignment requires specifying *whose* values and *which* ethical framework should govern the system, but RLHF sidesteps this question entirely by aggregating preferences into a single scalar reward, effectively collapsing pluralistic moral reasoning into a utilitarian social welfare function. The empirical consequences of this design choice are now becoming visible. Tennant et al. \cite{Tennant2025}, in work presented at ICLR 2025, demonstrated that the structure of the reward signal directly shapes the moral orientation of LLM agents: deontological reward functions produce cooperation-oriented strategies while utilitarian rewards produce outcome-maximizing ones, confirming that alignment training does not merely surface pre-existing moral knowledge but actively constructs it. Panickssery et al. \cite{Panickssery2024} further showed that LLM evaluators systematically favor their own generations, suggesting that self-evaluation protocols --- common in benchmark validation --- may amplify rather than correct training-time biases.

Together, contamination and RLHF mean that benchmark scores reflect an inseparable entanglement of genuine moral reasoning capability, memorization of training instances, and systematic biases introduced during preference optimization. Current evaluation methodology offers no principled way to disentangle these contributions, motivating the meta-evaluative framework we develop in the remainder of this paper.

### 2.7 Cross-Cultural Blind Spots and Value Pluralism

The tradition biases documented above are compounded by a deeper structural problem: the populations whose moral intuitions inform benchmark design are themselves unrepresentative. Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan's landmark review demonstrated that behavioral science draws overwhelmingly from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) societies, yet WEIRD subjects are statistical outliers on a wide range of psychological variables, including those most relevant to moral cognition \cite{Henrich2010}. Moral psychology is no exception to this pattern. Sachdeva, Singh, and Medin showed that moral reasoning varies systematically across cultures in ways that the dominant Western normative traditions --- consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics --- fail to capture, arguing that the universalist framework endemic to moral psychology may lead to a systematic underestimation of the role of culture in moral cognition \cite{Sachdeva2011}. When AI systems are built and evaluated against instruments calibrated to WEIRD populations, these cultural blind spots propagate downstream. Atari et al. extended the WEIRD critique directly to artificial intelligence, demonstrating that large language models exhibit psychological profiles most closely resembling WEIRD populations, with alignment declining steeply as target populations diverge from Western norms \cite{Atari2023}. The Moral Machine experiment provided the most comprehensive empirical test of cross-cultural moral variation to date: analyzing responses from 70,000 participants across 42 countries, Awad et al. found that the qualitative ordering of sacrifice acceptability is universal, but quantitative preferences vary substantially along cultural dimensions such as relational mobility \cite{Awad2020}. Morality, in short, is partially universal but far from fully so.

These empirical findings have direct implications for LLM evaluation. Mohammadi et al. tested whether current language models capture the cross-cultural spectrum of moral attitudes and found that they systematically compress cultural variation, exhibiting low alignment with empirical survey patterns and defaulting to Western moral baselines \cite{Mohammadi2025}. The mechanism is partly linguistic: Talat et al. showed that multilingual models inherit moral and social biases from their dominant training language, so that evaluating a model in English imposes Anglo-centric normative assumptions even when the model nominally supports other languages \cite{Talat2022}. The philosophical literature offers frameworks for navigating precisely this kind of disagreement. MacAskill, Bykvist, and Ord developed a formal theory of moral uncertainty, arguing that when an agent has credence across multiple moral theories, rational decision-making requires weighting each theory proportionally rather than collapsing to a single framework \cite{MacAskill2020}. Newberry and Ord operationalized this insight through a "parliamentary approach" in which competing moral theories are represented as delegates whose influence is proportional to the agent's credence in each, reaching decisions through negotiation rather than aggregation \cite{Newberry2021}. Recent work has begun applying these ideas to AI systems specifically. Yaacov proposed a deliberative moral reasoning architecture grounded in normative moral pluralism, incorporating a universal threshold layer alongside a local layer that integrates culturally specific normative content \cite{Yaacov2025}. Aditya similarly argued that global AI governance requires frameworks that accommodate cross-cultural value variation rather than imposing a single ethical tradition \cite{Aditya2025}. The upshot for benchmark evaluation is clear: current instruments embed not merely a bias toward particular normative traditions but a deeper cultural bias rooted in the populations and philosophical assumptions that shaped their design. Evaluation under genuine moral pluralism demands approaches fundamentally different from single-answer classification --- approaches that can represent, weight, and reason across competing moral commitments rather than treating one tradition's judgments as ground truth.

### 2.8 Documentation, Reproducibility, and the Data Bottleneck

The cultural and philosophical concerns raised above are exacerbated by a more prosaic but equally consequential problem: inadequate documentation. A growing consensus holds that machine learning artifacts require standardized documentation to be responsibly deployed. Gebru et al. proposed datasheets for datasets, modeled on electronics industry practice, requiring creators to document motivation, composition, collection processes, intended uses, and ethical considerations \cite{Gebru2021}. Mitchell et al. introduced model cards as companion documents for trained models, specifying intended use cases and performance across demographic groups \cite{Mitchell2019}. Bender and Friedman developed data statements for natural language processing, emphasizing the need to document the demographic and linguistic characteristics of annotator and speaker populations \cite{Bender2018}. Holland et al. proposed dataset nutrition labels as a more accessible alternative, designed to surface key properties at a glance \cite{Holland2018}. Despite this proliferation of documentation frameworks, adoption remains shallow. Liang et al. conducted the most comprehensive study to date, analyzing 32,111 model cards on Hugging Face and finding that sections addressing limitations, evaluation methodology, and environmental impact had the lowest completion rates, while training details were documented most consistently \cite{Liang2024Cards}. Paullada et al. offered a broader diagnosis, surveying systematic failures in dataset development practices across computer vision and natural language processing and arguing that the machine learning community's emphasis on benchmark-driven progress has crowded out careful attention to data provenance, annotation quality, and representational coverage \cite{Paullada2021}.

For normative ethics benchmarks, the documentation gap is especially consequential. Unlike a standard NLP benchmark where the task definition is relatively uncontested, a moral reasoning benchmark embeds substantive philosophical commitments --- which ethical theories are represented, how moral dilemmas are operationalized, what counts as a correct answer --- that constitute design choices with direct implications for construct validity. When these commitments go undocumented, downstream users cannot assess whether a benchmark's normative assumptions are compatible with their own evaluation goals. Karimi-Malekabadi et al. addressed this problem directly by proposing the Theory Trace Card (TTC), a lightweight documentation artifact for socio-cognitive benchmarks that makes explicit the theoretical basis of an evaluation, the components of the target capability it exercises, its operationalization, and its limitations \cite{KarimiMalekabadi2026}. The TTC framework is particularly apt for moral reasoning benchmarks, where the gap between benchmark performance and real-world moral competence --- what the authors term a "validity illusion" --- is especially wide. Our analysis of 37 benchmarks reveals a further structural problem: 12 (32\%) lack publicly available data, and several of the highest-scoring benchmarks on normative depth are among those that are inaccessible. This produces a paradox in which the benchmarks that engage most seriously with philosophical theory are the hardest for other researchers to build upon. Biderman et al., drawing on three years of experience maintaining the Language Model Evaluation Harness, documented how seemingly minor implementation choices --- prompt formatting, few-shot example selection, tokenization --- can produce substantial differences in reported performance, making reproducibility elusive even when data are nominally available \cite{Biderman2024}. The NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks track now requires data availability statements, a welcome step that our findings suggest should be extended to require explicit documentation of philosophical commitments. The 26-feature matrix we develop in this paper can serve as a template for such documentation, making visible the normative assumptions, response formats, and evaluation criteria that determine what a benchmark actually measures --- and what it leaves unmeasured.

### 2.9 Existing Surveys and Our Contribution

Several surveys have mapped the landscape of AI ethics evaluation, each illuminating important dimensions while leaving others in shadow. We briefly position our work against the five most relevant predecessors.

Ray \cite{Ray2023} offered an early taxonomy of normative ethics benchmarks, comparing datasets along dimensions such as task format, scale, and ethical framework coverage. This work usefully organized a fragmented field, but the analysis relied entirely on published descriptions: no benchmark was executed, and the taxonomy did not decompose coverage by philosophical tradition, treating "ethics" as a largely undifferentiated category. Snoswell et al. \cite{Snoswell2025}, in the most substantial recent contribution, surveyed 69 papers spanning LLM moral competence evaluation --- including safety, fairness, toxicity, and moral reasoning --- and argued persuasively that verdict-level agreement metrics obscure the reasoning processes they purport to measure. Their scope, however, necessarily trades depth for breadth: normative ethics is not isolated as a distinct evaluation category, and the survey does not examine whether benchmarks differentially engage deontological, consequentialist, virtue-theoretic, care-ethical, or contractarian reasoning.

At the cataloging end of the spectrum, Rismani et al. \cite{Rismani2025} assembled the most comprehensive inventory to date --- 791 AI ethics evaluation measures spanning technical benchmarks, organizational audits, and governance instruments. The sheer scale of this effort is valuable for orienting researchers to the full ecosystem, yet the inclusion of non-technical measures makes it difficult to extract actionable guidance for the narrower question of how well normative ethics benchmarks probe philosophical reasoning. Rauh et al. \cite{Rauh2024} approached the problem from a gap-analysis perspective, systematically identifying lacunae in AI safety evaluation infrastructure and explicitly noting the absence of assessments capable of eliciting nuanced moral reasoning. Their diagnosis is well-supported, but the work stops at identification: it proposes neither a feature framework for characterizing existing benchmarks nor empirical evidence of how current tools perform in practice. Methodologically closest to our approach, Rottger et al. \cite{Rottger2025} conducted a systematic review of 102 open safety evaluation datasets, evaluating them against quality criteria including discriminative power, construct validity, and documentation standards. Their emphasis on evaluating the evaluations --- rather than merely listing them --- anticipates our own methodological commitment, but their focus on safety broadly construed means normative ethics receives no dedicated treatment, and philosophical tradition plays no role in their analysis.

Our work extends this collective foundation in three directions. First, we treat *normative tradition as a first-class evaluation dimension*, scoring each benchmark's engagement with five philosophical frameworks rather than aggregating ethics into a monolithic category. This decomposition reveals systematic imbalances --- most critically, the near-total absence of care ethics and contractarian reasoning from existing evaluation suites --- that remain invisible when benchmarks are characterized only by task format or domain label. Second, we complement feature-level analysis with *implementation trials* that expose empirical properties no survey-only methodology can detect: ceiling effects in which frontier models achieve near-perfect accuracy \cite{Hendrycks2021}, stable difficulty hierarchies across philosophical traditions, and the emergence of refusal as a de facto normative position that current scoring rubrics cannot accommodate. Third, we document a *saturation finding* with immediate practical consequences: the field's most-cited and most-integrated normative ethics benchmark is saturated for frontier models, meaning that every meta-benchmark suite incorporating it --- including widely used evaluation harnesses \cite{Liang2022} --- inherits a component that no longer discriminates between competing systems.

Together, these contributions provide both a reusable analytic framework for future benchmark development and empirical evidence that current evaluation infrastructure systematically underestimates the difficulty of normative moral reasoning. Where prior surveys have asked *what benchmarks exist*, we ask *what do they actually measure* --- and find that the answer is considerably less than the field assumes.
