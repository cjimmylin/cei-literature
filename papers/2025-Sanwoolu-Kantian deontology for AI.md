## Abstract

This paper argues that AI alignment with Kantian principles does not require moral agency in Kant's sense. The Categorical Imperative (CI) can serve as a useful framework for AI alignment, guiding the creation of maxims governing AI actions and testing their universalizability, particularly using the first principle of the CI which is the formula of the universal law (FUL). Some Kantian scholars argue that Kant wasn't blind to context, and they point to practical judgment to account for it. Interestingly, transformer models in AI already play a similar functional role: They are designed to be sensitive to context. So, even if AI systems aren't moral agents, they might still approximate the kind of context-sensitive reasoning we see in human moral deliberation.

## Detailed Summary

This paper explores the potential application of Kant's moral philosophy to artificial intelligence alignment, addressing two major objections to this approach. The first objection concerns whether AI can fulfill Kant's standards for moral agency. Sanwoolu argues that AI alignment with Kantian principles does not require moral agency in Kant's sense - instead, the Categorical Imperative (CI) can serve as a framework for guiding AI actions and testing their universalizability, particularly using the Formula of Universal Law (FUL).

The second objection addressed is the particularist critique of Kantian universalism, which holds that Kant's framework cannot accommodate context-sensitivity when forming maxims. Sanwoolu argues that Kant's framework can indeed accommodate context-sensitivity through practical judgment. Since AI systems lack practical judgment in the human sense, the author proposes that transformer models serve a functionally equivalent role through attention mechanisms.

The paper's significance lies in providing a philosophical foundation for using deontological ethics as an alignment target while circumventing traditional objections about AI's lack of moral agency.

## Methods Summary

This paper employs philosophical analysis as its primary methodology. The author engages with Kantian scholarship, including interpretations by Rawls, Herman, Bremner, and others. The analysis involves conceptual clarification of Kantian moral agency requirements, examination of practical judgment and context-sensitivity, and analogical reasoning comparing transformer attention mechanisms to human practical judgment.

## Results Summary

Key conclusions: (1) AI systems can be aligned with Kantian principles without possessing moral agency, (2) transformer models' self-attention mechanisms provide a functional equivalent to practical judgment for context-sensitive maxim formation, (3) the Formula of Universal Law provides a tractable starting point for formalizing ethical constraints, and (4) AI systems can approximate context-sensitive reasoning while remaining "moral zombies."

## Conclusions

AI alignment is possible within a Kantian framework, even though AI systems cannot be moral agents in Kant's sense. The Categorical Imperative can guide AI actions. Future work should explore how to operationalize the CI framework in actual AI systems.
