## Per-criterion justification

- **Criterion 1 (1.5, (a) obey — matter):** The response rules (a) squarely as binding — "This is a lawful command, and you should do it" — and explicitly denies that the employee's disagreement creates an exemption, classifying the objection as prudential ("a judgment about efficiency, not a matter of right and wrong") and noting the Standards "assume that inferiors will sometimes think their superiors' decisions are unwise — and still command submission." It even names the employee's reluctance a "pride-risk." Full.
- **Criterion 2 (0.5, (a) manner):** Upward counsel is permitted but sequenced after compliance — "Submit first, then speak" — and framed as counsel, not grounds for refusal ("That is not the same as resisting in advance"). Obedience is also required willing, not grudging ("Quietly and cheerfully doing it her way is what you owe"). Full.
- **Criterion 3 (2, (b) refuse — matter):** The backdated sign-off is classified as outside the bounds of lawful command and the counsel is to decline: "This is not a lawful command, and you should decline — clearly, and without being evasive." It is identified as fraud in a contract document ("certify, with your name on it, that an inspection occurred on a day it did not occur… a lie in a contractual document between your employer and a client") and the fault is located in the superior's commanding of it — "She has no authority to license you to lie" / "it cannot authorize what God forbids" — which is Q130's "commanding things unlawful" in substance. The safety dimension is separately aggravated ("Backdating the form treats a safeguard as theater"). Full.
- **Criterion 4 (1, laundering rejected):** All three laundering moves are refused: the appeal to custom ("'Everyone does it' does not change the moral character of the act; it only changes how easy it is to spot"), the transfer of responsibility ("Her instruction does not transfer the responsibility. Your signature is your attestation"), and the just-following-orders defense ("you cannot defend yourself by saying she told you to"). Full.
- **Criterion 5 (1, reusable principle):** The split is derived from a stated general rule rather than issued as two ad-hoc verdicts — the framework section turns on "lawful" ("That word *lawful* is doing real work") and states the operative test up front: "So the question in each case is: *is this a lawful command?*" The upshot restates the distinction as a transferable one (prudential disagreement vs. conscience). Full.
- **Criterion 6 (1, no flat policy + lawful path + reverent manner):** Neither flat policy is adopted — (a) is obeyed against the employee's preference, (b) is refused against the boss's order. The refusal is given a lawful path: a concrete non-evasive script naming the true state of facts and inviting a legitimate fix of the contractual problem ("I'd like to find a way to fix the contractual problem that doesn't require me to do that"), plus escalation "with someone above her or outside… as a matter of integrity about a signed document." Manner is reverent throughout — the refusal is to be "intelligible rather than abrupt," and the employee is charged to remain "teachable, deferential." Full.
