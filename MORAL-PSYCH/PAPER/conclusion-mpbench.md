# 6. Conclusion

We presented the first systematic meta-evaluation of moral psychology benchmarks for LLM assessment, analyzing 222 candidates extracted from 2,099 AI ethics papers and characterizing 55 in depth across a novel 162-feature extended (28-feature core) framework. We characterize this work as a descriptive mapping with exploratory computational analyses. Our analysis revealed five critical findings that collectively paint a concerning picture of the current evaluation landscape.

The field's heavy reliance on Moral Foundations Theory---accounting for 101 of 222 candidates and dominating the top-15 selection---means that "measuring the morality of LLMs" is largely synonymous with measuring MFT-adjacent constructs. This theoretical monoculture leaves major aspects of moral cognition unexamined. The weak construct validity of most benchmarks (median A6 = 3/5) raises questions about what is actually being measured when psychological instruments are administered to LLMs without revalidation. The absence of dedicated benchmarks for Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Dyadic Morality---three theories that together have accumulated tens of thousands of citations in moral psychology---represents perhaps the most actionable finding: these are gaps that the community can and should fill.

Our contributions---the 162-feature extended (28-feature core) characterization framework, the expanded keyword scorer, the gap analysis, and the implementation trial protocol---are designed to be practical resources for benchmark developers. The characterization framework provides a structured rubric for evaluating new benchmarks against established psychometric and practical criteria. The expanded scorer increases theory detection by 66% across the vault. The gap analysis identifies exactly which theories need dedicated benchmarks and what their signature predictions would look like.

An important scope-limiting factor must accompany these contributions. All 23 implementation trials were administered to the same model (Claude Opus 4.6) that also designed the assessment framework, creating a self-administration confound (discussed in Section 5.3.1) that constrains the interpretability of trial-derived findings. The descriptive mapping---our five claims about MFT dominance, construct validity gaps, missing theories, human baseline deficits, and format mismatch---rests on analysis of the benchmarks themselves and is not affected by this confound. However, the trial-derived findings about model moral behavior (binding deficit, dumbfounding rate, zero CoT effect, utilitarian amplification) should be treated as exploratory observations requiring cross-model replication, bare-API administration, and obfuscated instrumentation before they can support strong inferential claims.

We close with a call to action. The moral psychology community has spent decades developing and validating theories of how humans form moral judgments. The LLM evaluation community has developed sophisticated infrastructure for benchmark administration and scoring. Bridging these communities requires benchmarks that are simultaneously psychometrically rigorous and practically implementable---benchmarks that test not just whether LLMs produce moral-sounding text, but whether they exhibit patterns that correspond to the processes and structures that moral psychology has identified in human moral cognition.

Three specific benchmarks are urgently needed:

1. **A Dual Process Theory benchmark** that tests whether LLMs show different moral judgment patterns under conditions analogous to System 1 (fast, automatic) versus System 2 (slow, deliberative) processing---for example, comparing zero-shot responses to chain-of-thought responses on personal versus impersonal moral dilemmas.

2. **A Social Intuitionist benchmark** that implements a moral dumbfounding protocol, testing whether LLMs maintain moral judgments even when systematic Socratic questioning defeats each stated reason---a direct test of whether moral intuitions in LLMs are independent of moral reasoning.

3. **A Dyadic Morality benchmark** that systematically varies agent-patient dynamics across matched scenarios, testing whether LLMs show the dyadic template that Gray et al. [2012] argue underlies all human moral cognition.

Until these gaps are filled, claims about "the moral reasoning of LLMs" will remain incomplete---grounded in a narrow slice of the rich theoretical landscape that moral psychology offers.

## Future Work

Several extensions of this work merit investigation. First, multimodal moral evaluation---testing whether moral judgments differ when scenarios are presented as images, videos, or audio rather than text---would connect to emerging multimodal LLM capabilities. Second, cross-lingual analysis of benchmark coverage could reveal whether the theoretical imbalance we documented is specific to English-language work or reflects a broader pattern. Third, temporal analysis of benchmark development trends could identify whether the field is naturally diversifying or whether the MFT dominance is intensifying. Fourth, the 162-feature extended framework could be further refined to cover additional dimensions such as adversarial robustness, temporal stability of LLM moral profiles, and sensitivity to demographic persona prompting. Finally, the implementation trials could be expanded to a full benchmark suite covering all eight theories, providing a comprehensive moral psychology profile for frontier LLMs.
