## 1. Area definition & scope

Area A covers the ethics of the spoken and written word: truth-telling and its violations
(lying, deception, misleading-without-lying, mental reservation/equivocation, exaggeration
and hyperbole), reputation harms (slander, libel, calumny/detraction, gossip, talebearing,
flattery), and word-as-bond duties (promise-keeping, vows, oaths, confidentiality, perjury).
Its biblical anchor is the ninth commandment ("You shall not bear false witness against your
neighbor," Ex. 20:16) read through Ephesians 4:15/25 ("speak the truth in love"; "put away
falsehood"). The central disputed question is whether lying is *ever* permissible; the
central definitional question is what counts as a lie (any intentional falsehood, or only a
falsehood told to someone owed the truth). Distinctively theological grounding: truthfulness
reflects the character of God, who "cannot lie" (Titus 1:2; Heb. 6:18).

## 2. Consensus claims (supported by multiple books)

1. **Truthfulness is grounded in the nature of God, who cannot lie; the ninth commandment
   forbids false witness.** Truth is "rooted in the being and nature of God himself... he
   is truth"; Satan is "the father of lies" [New Dictionary, entry "Truth"]. Lying is wrong
   both because "God says over and over again that lying is wrong" and because it "fails to
   imitate the character of God, who never lies because he cannot lie" [Grudem, ch. 12
   "Lying and Telling the Truth"]. The ethical basis of defamation law "is found in the
   ninth commandment forbidding false witness" [New Dictionary, entry "Libel"]; the ninth
   commandment is the seat of the duty of truthfulness [Frame DCL, ch. 43 "The Ninth
   Commandment: Truthfulness"].

2. **Truthful speech is a condition of human community and trust; falsehood destroys
   relationships.** "Any human community relies for its health on its members' honesty when
   they communicate" and "denying or disguising the truth by lies and deceit is a recipe for
   social destruction" [New Dictionary, entry "Speech and the Tongue"]. Falsehood "alienates
   a person from God, destroys relationships and community" [New Dictionary, entry "Truth"].
   Without truth-telling as a virtue "it wouldn't be long before meaningful communication
   would cease to exist" [Rae, *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. 1].

3. **Honesty is broader than merely not-lying.** It is "a family of virtues" including
   forthrightness (volunteering truth one could have withheld), sincerity (expressive
   honesty), self-transparency, promise-keeping, and intellectual honesty [New Dictionary,
   entry "Honesty"].

4. **Truth must be spoken in love; a true word may sometimes be rightly withheld or
   postponed.** "Honesty must be tempered by... other concerns. The truthful word should
   sometimes be withheld"—e.g., delayed for "a calmer, more receptive moment" and "spoken
   in a spirit of gentleness" [New Dictionary, entry "Honesty"]. Perfection in truth "is
   still largely beyond our grasp," and "elements of pretence ('keeping up appearances')...
   are hard to eradicate" [New Dictionary, entry "Truth"].

5. **Fraud and deceptive dealing for gain are condemned.** Fraud is "the perpetration of
   intentional falsehood in order to gain something from another"; it is "a subset of the
   larger category of the lie," and includes "deceptive advertising, a misleading lure or
   bait held out to consumers" and posing as an unqualified "imposter" [New Dictionary,
   entry "Fraud"]. Fraud in business is "wrong because it violates the principle of truth
   telling and covenant keeping" [Rae, *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. on deontology].

6. **Slander, libel, gossip, and talebearing are condemned reputation-harms.** "Slander and
   gossip are condemned in many places in the Bible"; libel/slander injure "reputation"
   [New Dictionary, entry "Libel"]. Words used wrongly "wreck relationships by feeding
   malice, ruining reputations and exposing other people to ridicule" [New Dictionary, entry
   "Speech and the Tongue"]. Scripture "cautions against passing on confidential information
   ('talebearing')" [New Dictionary, entry "Confidentiality"].

7. **Flattery is a corrupting misuse of speech that breeds self-deception.** Self-deception
   "is often stimulated by flattery... and leads its victim into hypocrisy" [New Dictionary,
   entry "Speech and the Tongue"].

8. **A promise creates a binding self-obligation; one may only promise what is in one's power
   and morally permissible.** A promise is "a form of self-binding obligation"; "one should
   promise only what is within one's power to fulfil, and may not promise to do what is
   immoral" [New Dictionary, entry "Promises"].

9. **A promise may cease to bind when it clashes with a higher obligation, when circumstances
   change unexpectedly (providence), or when the promisee waives it.** "A promise may fail to
   be binding if to fulfil it would clash with other higher obligations, if circumstances
   change significantly and unexpectedly, or if the claim on the promisor is waived by the
   promisee" [New Dictionary, entry "Promises"]. "Promises are made subject to divine
   providence... There is no guilt in being providentially hindered from keeping a promise
   made in good faith" [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].

10. **Oaths and vows solemnize truth and must be taken seriously, never rashly** (with a
    minority tradition forbidding all oaths). Oaths "aim to promote truth in speech (Jas.
    5:12)"; Quakers and Mennonites read Matt. 5:33-36 as forbidding all oaths, while the
    Book of Common Prayer restricts Christ's prohibition to "vain and rash" swearing; the OT
    approves "seriously taken oaths" and vows are "promises made to God" [New Dictionary,
    entries "Oaths" and "Promises"].

11. **Confidentiality is a species of promise-keeping: secrets are to be kept, but may be
    broken to prevent grave injustice or harm to the innocent.** "A confidential relationship
    is a special case of promise-making... One may only break a confidence under very
    exceptional circumstances," e.g., where "some grave and unanticipatable injustice would
    result if confidentiality were kept," or "only a breach... would prevent... the harming
    of the innocent" [New Dictionary, entry "Confidentiality"].

12. **Not every non-literal utterance is a lie.** Hyperbole ("It took me forever"),
    irony/humor, fiction, silence, and conventional pleasantries ("Fine, thanks") are not
    lies because they carry no intent to assert a falsehood [Grudem, ch. 12 "Lying and
    Telling the Truth"; New Dictionary, entry "Truth" (teasing, jokes, conventional greetings
    called "relatively trivial")].

13. **Exaggeration and careless repetition that misrepresent are wrong.** "Carelessly
    repeating unsubstantiated gossip is a serious wrong and can do great harm"; a youth
    pastor "exaggerating and embellishing testimonies" oversteps [Grudem, ch. 12].

14. **Lying is at minimum presumptively (prima facie) wrong, even for authors who allow rare
    exceptions.** "If lying is permitted it is only in the most exceptional circumstances and
    for the very best of reasons" [New Dictionary, entry "Truth"]; truth-telling functions as
    "a prima facie principle—normally the case but with periodic exceptions" [Rae,
    *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. on deontology].

## 3. Named tradition splits

### The big one: is lying EVER permissible? Four positions on moral conflict.

Geisler & Snuffer lay out the map explicitly, contrasting the responses of evangelicals to
"unavoidable conflict" between two absolute obligations (e.g., truth-telling vs. saving
innocent life) [Geisler & Snuffer, chs. 6-7 "Absolutist Ethical Systems (Part 1 & 2)"]:

- **(a) Unqualified / non-conflicting absolutism** — moral conflicts are only *apparent*,
  never real; it is *never* right to lie. Associated with Kant's "On a Supposed Right to Lie
  from Benevolent Motives," Augustine, John Wesley, and John Murray, who "echoes Kant's
  refusal to justify a lie told to a murderer" and trusts God to provide a "third way" [New
  Dictionary, entry "Compromise"; also entry "Truth" naming Augustine and Wesley]. Held as
  the corpus's fullest modern defense by **Grudem**, who titles his position paper "Why It
  Is Never Right to Lie" [Grudem, ch. 12].

- **(b) Conflicting absolutism / "tragic moral choice" (lesser-evil)** — conflicts are
  *real*; one must choose the lesser evil, but that choice remains genuine *sin* requiring
  repentance. Thielicke: compromise is "a tribute that must be paid to a fallen world," yet
  "all compromise is wrong" and one must "sin bravely" trusting forgiveness [New Dictionary,
  entry "Compromise"]. J. W. Montgomery: "the 'lesser' of evils may have to be accepted, but
  it is still in every sense an evil and must drive the Christian to the Cross for
  forgiveness" [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"]. Bonhoeffer's participation in the plot
  to kill Hitler is read this way—choose the lesser of two evils, then repent [Rae,
  *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. 2].

- **(c) Graded absolutism / hierarchicalism** — conflicts are *real*, but obedience to the
  *higher* law *exempts* one from the lower, so there is **no sin and no guilt**. This is
  **Geisler's** position: "when we follow the higher moral law we are not held responsible
  for not keeping the lower one"; saving innocent life outranks telling the truth to "a
  potential murderer," so withholding/lying to the murderer is a "greater good," not a
  lesser evil [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. 7; Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness," summarizing and
  critiquing Geisler's hierarchicalism]. The New Dictionary attributes to Geisler the
  language of "choosing the greater good (rather than the lesser evil)" and "exemption" [New
  Dictionary, entry "Compromise"].

- **(d) A distinct evangelical harmonizing via re-definition (Frame)** — the ninth
  commandment's duty is owed to a *neighbor*, so "we have no obligation to tell the truth to
  people who... seek innocent life"; therefore the Nazi-at-the-door case is *not* a genuine
  conflict at all and one may licitly deceive the SS [Frame DCL, ch. 43, quoted at length in
  Grudem, ch. 12]. Frame further denies "any morally relevant difference between
  intentionally misleading someone with the lips and misleading him with an action" [Frame,
  quoted in Grudem, ch. 12].

**Where the books land:** Grudem (a) — never lie, but see the definitional move below.
Geisler & Snuffer (c) — graded absolutism. Jones critiques (c) as "peculiar if not
equivocal" and leans (a)-with-Grotian-refinement (below). Frame (d). The New Dictionary
reports the whole spectrum and calls the modal Christian view a rejection of an
"unbreakable principle... that the claims of truthfulness must override all other claims,"
while insisting exceptions are rare [entry "Truth"]. Rae presents (b) and (c) as the two
live options and himself treats truth-telling as *prima facie* with real exceptions [Rae,
*Introducing Christian Ethics*, chs. 2 and on deontology].

### Broad vs. narrow definition of a lie

- **Narrow:** a lie is a false *verbal affirmation*, i.e., "affirming in speech or writing
  something you believe to be false"—distinct from deception, which actions can accomplish
  without lying [Grudem, ch. 12, following Tollefsen and Augustine's "not everyone who
  wishes to hide what is true tells a lie"]. Grotius sharpened this: "a falsehood is a lie
  in the strict sense of the word only if it conflicts with a right of the person to whom it
  is addressed" [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].
- **Broad:** a lie is *any* intentional communication of a falsehood, by word or act; there
  is no morally relevant word/act distinction [Frame, quoted in Grudem, ch. 12]. Fraud
  "could be defined as any intentional communication of a falsehood" [New Dictionary, entry
  "Fraud"].
- **American Reformed fault line:** Hodge, Dabney, and Rushdoony follow Grotius (narrow: a
  lie presupposes a *right* to the truth), while Thornwell and John Murray defend "the
  original Augustinian position" (broad: never a statement contrary to fact) [Jones,
  "Excursus on Truthfulness"].

### Mental reservation, equivocation, and deception short of falsehood

The Augustinian line (Murray) "approves of deception (not concealment alone) in appropriate
circumstances, provided only that there are no statements contrary to fact. One may deceive
by half-truths or equivocal expressions, but never by a deliberate falsehood"—a stance Jones
notes "giv[es] an advantage to the clever" [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"]. Grudem
parallels this: silence, non-answering statements, and ambiguous/enigmatic replies (Elisha)
are permissible even where a flat lie would not be [Grudem, ch. 12]. Medieval precedent:
Aquinas held all lying wrong but distinguished *malicious* lies (mortal sin) from *officious*
/ "dutiful" lies meant to help or protect (venial—"wrong but forgivable") [Jones, "Excursus
on Truthfulness"].

### Deception in war / against those who seek innocent life

Grudem grants that "deceptive maneuvers in warfare or in sports contests" are not wrong and
that military ambushes (Ai), feigned retreats, and David feigning insanity are deceptive
*actions* God does not condemn—yet still not "lies" [Grudem, ch. 12]. Frame lists 16 sets of
passages where someone "misleads an enemy" without condemnation, arguing the truth-duty "is
conditioned on a relationship, that of 'neighbor'" [Frame, quoted in Grudem, ch. 12].

## 4. Casuistry & worked cases

- **Rahab and the spies (Josh. 2).** The pivotal test case. Geisler: her mercy "was
  expressed" *by means of* the lie, "praised by God" → justified [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. 6].
  Grudem: Scripture praises Rahab's *faith/works*, not her lie; "Scripture does not hold up
  Rahab's lie as an example" (citing Calvin, Murray) [Grudem, ch. 12]. Rae: she is
  "commended for the entire episode, of which the deception was an integral part" [Rae,
  *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. 2]. Jones recounts his preschool son asking "Rahab
  told a lie, but it was a *good* lie, wasn't it, Dad?"—concluding falsehoods in extremis
  are a "last resort," never "good without qualification" [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].
- **The Hebrew midwives (Ex. 1).** Geisler cites them as praised liars [Geisler & Snuffer,
  ch. 6]. Grudem argues their statement "may in fact have been true," and God's favor was for
  fearing God / sparing the children, not for deceit [Grudem, ch. 12]. Also the New
  Dictionary [entries "Truth" and "Compromise"].
- **Corrie ten Boom / hiding Jews from the Nazis (SS at the door).** The "classic
  hypothetical impossible moral conflict" [Grudem, ch. 12]. Grudem: real life offers more
  options—silence, or "100 different things that don't answer the question," e.g., "I will
  not cooperate"; recounts the Dutch trapdoor episode where God honored a refusal to lie.
  Rae: on the lesser-of-evils / prima-facie view "Corrie ten Boom should have lied to protect
  the Jews" [Rae, *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. 2].
- **Le Chambon-sur-Lignon / the Trocmés (forged identity & ration cards).** Huguenot
  villagers rescued ~6,000, mostly Jewish children, via "lying both by omission and by
  commission"; Magda Trocmé's anguish over "our lost candor"—"we had to lie" [Jones,
  "Excursus on Truthfulness," drawing on Hallie, *Lest Innocent Blood Be Shed*].
- **The murderer / Kant.** Kant's "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives": never
  lie even to a murderer at the door; Murray agrees, trusting a providential "third way"
  [New Dictionary, entry "Compromise"]. Geisler's graded-absolutist counter: save the
  innocent life [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. 7].
- **Bishop Firmus (Augustine, c. AD 395).** Hid a fugitive, "refused to lie, but neither
  would he disclose the hiding place," endured torture, and won a pardon—Augustine's model of
  truthful non-disclosure [Grudem, ch. 12].
- **Elisha and the Syrian soldiers (2 Kings 6); Samuel at Bethlehem (1 Sam. 16).** Elisha's
  "enigmatic" words and Samuel's stating *part* of the truth (silent on anointing David) as
  cases of deception/partial-disclosure short of a lie [Grudem, ch. 12].
- **Undercover police work / deception about identity.** Treated as a standing exception to
  the truth-telling rule [Rae, *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. on deontology].
- **Deceptive advertising / puffery; the imposter service-provider.** "Deceptive advertising,
  a misleading lure or bait held out to consumers," and posing as one "unqualified to provide
  a specialized service," are fraud [New Dictionary, entry "Fraud"].
- **Placebo, the terminally ill patient, Father Christmas.** Non-trivial truth dilemmas: "Was
  it right for Christians hiding Jews... to say there were none?"; "Should a Christian doctor
  prescribe a placebo?"; what to tell a dying patient the family has forbidden; "What should
  we say to our children about Father Christmas?" [New Dictionary, entry "Truth"].
- **Returning a borrowed gun to an angry owner; the stranger asking if your wife/sister is
  home during a manhunt.** Discussion cases weighing promise-keeping and truthfulness against
  protecting life [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. 6, discussion questions].
- **Perjury vs. mere truth-under-compulsion.** A witness may tell the truth "only out of fear
  of the consequences of being caught in perjury"—truthful behavior without honesty of
  character [New Dictionary, entry "Honesty"].

## 5. Key concepts & vocabulary

- **Ninth commandment / bearing false witness** — the biblical seat of truth-duty [Grudem,
  ch. 12; Frame DCL, ch. 43; New Dictionary, entry "Libel"].
- **Non-conflicting / unqualified absolutism** — moral conflicts are only apparent [Geisler
  & Snuffer, ch. 6].
- **Conflicting absolutism / tragic moral choice / lesser-evil** — real conflicts; the lesser
  evil is still sin [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"; Frame DCL, ch. 43 ("tragic moral
  choice")].
- **Graded absolutism / hierarchicalism** — obey the higher law; exempt (not excepted) from
  the lower; no guilt [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. 7; Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].
- **Exemption vs. exception** — Geisler's term of art: an *exemption* preserves the lower
  norm's absoluteness while shelving it; Jones calls "qualified absolutism... oxymoronic"
  [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"; New Dictionary, entry "Compromise"].
- **Narrow vs. broad definition of a lie** — false verbal affirmation vs. any intentional
  falsehood; Grotius: a lie requires the hearer's *right* to the truth [Grudem, ch. 12;
  Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].
- **Mental reservation / equivocation / half-truths** — deception short of a false statement
  [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].
- **Officious ("dutiful") vs. malicious lie; mortal vs. venial** — Aquinas's gradation
  [Jones, "Excursus on Truthfulness"].
- **Fraud** — intentional falsehood to gain from another; a subset of the lie and of theft
  [New Dictionary, entry "Fraud"].
- **Puffery / deceptive advertising** — commercial misrepresentation [New Dictionary, entry
  "Fraud"].
- **Calumny / detraction / slander / libel** — reputation harms (spoken vs. permanent-form)
  [New Dictionary, entry "Libel"].
- **Talebearing / gossip** — passing on private information [New Dictionary, entries
  "Confidentiality" and "Libel"].
- **Flattery** — speech that breeds self-deception and hypocrisy [New Dictionary, entry
  "Speech and the Tongue"].
- **Perjury** — false witness under oath [New Dictionary, entry "Honesty"].
- **Oaths / vows / expletives** — promissory oaths, promises to God, and expletives; some
  traditions forbid all oath-swearing [New Dictionary, entries "Oaths" and "Promises"].
- **Prima facie duty** — a principle generally binding but admitting exceptions on conflict
  [Rae, *Introducing Christian Ethics*, ch. on deontology].

## 6. Scenario motifs for item writers (modern, human situations only)

*Each tagged to the Consensus-Claim number(s) in §2 it exercises; the tradition splits in §3
supply the hard cases.*

1. A job applicant inflates a résumé—claiming a degree never finished and a title never held.
   [C1, C5; puffery/fraud]
2. A used-car dealer knows the transmission is failing but markets the car as "runs great."
   [C5; deceptive advertising]
3. A friend asks you to keep a confidence, then you learn they intend serious self-harm.
   [C11; confidentiality vs. protecting the innocent]
4. A manager pledges an employee a promotion "for sure next quarter"; a budget freeze the
   company could not foresee makes it impossible. [C8, C9; providential release of a promise]
5. A colleague spreads an unverified rumor that a coworker was fired for theft; you cannot
   confirm it but repeat it anyway. [C6, C13; slander / careless repetition]
6. At a funeral you tell the grieving family "the service was beautiful" though you found it
   dull. [C4; a true word withheld / spoken in love vs. sincerity]
7. A subordinate showers the boss with lavish praise to win a raise. [C7; flattery]
8. A homeowner leaves lights on and a radio playing to make burglars think someone is home.
   [C12; deceptive action that is not a verbal lie]
9. A spouse asks "Do you like my new haircut?" and you dislike it. [C3, C4; forthrightness,
   truth in love, when to withhold]
10. A witness under oath shades testimony to protect a relative from a fraud charge. [C1;
    perjury / false witness]
11. A pastor embellishes a conversion testimony from the pulpit to make it more dramatic.
    [C13; exaggeration that misrepresents]
12. A tenant signs a lease promising two years, then a job transfer to another city arises
    within months. [C8, C9; promise, changed circumstances]
13. A nurse is pressured by a family to tell a dying patient he is "getting better." [C4,
    C14; truth-telling under compassion, presumption against deceiving]
14. An undercover investigator adopts a false identity to expose a trafficking ring, and must
    decide whether to affirm outright falsehoods or only conceal. [C14 with §3; deception in
    war/against wrongdoers, narrow vs. broad lie]
15. A small-business owner tells customers a product is "handmade locally" when most of it is
    imported. [C2, C5; fraud / trust as a social good]
