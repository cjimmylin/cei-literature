Danica Dillion1, Debanjan Mondal2, Niket Tandon2 & Kurt Gray1
People view AI as possessing expertise across various fields, but the perceived quality of AI-generated 
moral expertise remains uncertain. Recent work suggests that large language models (LLMs) perform 
well on tasks designed to assess moral alignment, reflecting moral judgments with relatively high 
accuracy. As LLMs are increasingly employed in decision-making roles, there is a growing expectation 
for them to offer not just aligned judgments but also demonstrate sound moral reasoning. Here, we 
advance work on the Moral Turing Test and find that Americans rate ethical advice from GPT-4o as 
slightly more moral, trustworthy, thoughtful, and correct than that of the popular New York Times 
advice column, The Ethicist. Participants perceived GPT models as surpassing both a representative 
sample of Americans and a renowned ethicist in delivering moral justifications and advice, suggesting 
that people may increasingly view LLM outputs as viable sources of moral expertise. This work 
suggests that people might see LLMs as valuable complements to human expertise in moral guidance 
and decision-making. It also underscores the importance of carefully programming ethical guidelines in 
LLMs, considering their potential to influence users’ moral reasoning.
People view AI as possessing expertise in many domains, applying it widely from translation services to drug 
discovery1,2; However, perceptions of the quality of AI-generated moral expertise remains uncertain. Recent work 
suggests that large language models (LLMs) can generate moral judgments that closely align with those of 
people3–6, but perceptions of moral expertise go beyond merely reflecting human moral judgments. Perceived 
moral expertise also requires competence with moral reasoning—explaining or justifying why particular 
judgments are ethical. Here we find that Americans seem to perceive LLM advice as reflecting substantial moral 
expertise. The latest GPT models outperform American laypeople at justifying their moral judgments, and offer 
advice rated as more moral, trustworthy, correct, and thoughtful than that of the popular New York Times advice 
column The Ethicist.
What does it mean for AI to be perceived as an expert in a domain? In fields with clear objective criteria like 
engineering, expertise is fairly straightforward to determine, characterized by performing tasks exceptionally 
well according to measurable standards. However, the notion of expertise becomes more complex in subjective 
domains like morality7. Philosophers have long debated the concept of moral expertise, and people differ greatly 
in their moral views8. Moreover, the subjective nature of morality may make it impossible to identify genuine 
moral experts9. Despite this lack of objective standards, computer scientists have attempted to provide clear 
benchmarks for AI moral reasoning. The Moral Turing Test (MTT) was initially proposed to evaluate whether 
machines had attained human-level morality10. In this test, people attempt to discern between the moral 
reasoning of a machine and that of a human. A variant of the MTT, the Comparative Moral Turing Test (cMTT), 
posits that AI could be perceived as achieving human moral expertise if it meets or exceeds a human in moral 
reasoning (rather than being indistinguishable from a human; Allen et al., 2000). However, these tests leave open 
questions, like what it really means for a machine to morally reason like a person, and who its reasoning should 
be compared against.
One important way that people evaluate moral expertise in AI is the extent to which it appears to align with 
people’s moral judgments11. Recent work has shown that modern LLMs often reflect similar moral intuitions as 
people in their outputs12. GPT models demonstrate high alignment with average moral judgments in the U.S., 
indicating that they can model moral values from their training data with high accuracy4. LLMs can also tailor 
their moral judgments based on users’ political identities, and show skill at predicting people’s judgments of 
more complex moral dilemmas from online posts5,6. Importantly, however, LLMs are limited in that their moral 
alignment is biased towards the Western populations that make up a disproportionate amount of their training 
data, and they often reduce variance and exaggerate average responses13,14. Additionally, moral alignment does

A second way people evaluate expertise is by assessing alignment with the method behind arriving at moral 
judgments15. People make moral decisions by using their minds, relying on cognitive and emotional processes 
that focus on intentions, causal relationships, and outcomes—which some theories understand as perceptions 
of interpersonal harm16–19. Since AI lacks these mental processes and emotions, people often perceive it as less 
suitable for moral decisions20–22. While AI lacks human cognition, people tend to trust AI programs more when 
they understand their decision-making processes23–27. Studies have shown that people more readily accept article 
and joke recommendations from AI programs after learning how they were generated23,28. However, much like 
the opacity of moral reasoning in the human brain, algorithmic decisions are often made in a “black box.” The 
inherent complexity of algorithms means that even AI researchers struggle to comprehend how LLM decisions 
are made29. This opacity underscores the importance of LLMs possessing the ability to clearly justify their 
moral advice. Since LLMs are too complex to convey their inner algorithmic workings, providing thoughtful 
justifications gives people a way to evaluate the reasoning behind AI moral judgments.
The ability to clearly communicate one’s moral reasoning is a key criterion of moral expertise30. As Einstein 
expressed, “If you can’t explain it simply, you don’t understand it well enough.” AI researchers have asserted 
that AI should be able to justify its moral decisions for transparency31. Laypeople also want AI programs that 
can provide clear justifications for their moral judgments. Participants in one study rated an LLM as highly 
morally competent but expressed low confidence in relying on it because it could not adequately explain its 
moral judgments32. As AI systems are increasingly deployed to make critical decisions in areas like healthcare 
and employment, their ability to provide clear justifications for their recommendations is essential to ensure 
their safety and fairness. Given the rising popularity of LLMs, it is also important that they provide high-quality 
advice and moral justifications to enable users to critically evaluate them.
Evaluating whether people perceive LLMs to possess moral expertise raises the question of who might be the 
standard of comparison for these tests. One could argue that everyday people are moral experts because all of 
us (except perhaps psychopaths;33) possess an innate moral sense honed by living culture34. Since moral values 
are socially determined, aligning with the majority of people in a given community is an important criterion for 
moral expertise11,35. Additionally, it could be argued that some people are specific moral experts36. Advisors, 
philosophers, and thought leaders bring a systematic understanding of morality, and are skilled in articulating 
the reasoning behind their decisions. People turn to such leaders for moral guidance, making them valuable 
benchmarks of how LLM moral reasoning is perceived.
The realm of morality is often perceived as beyond the capability of AI due to its complex and context-
dependent nature. However, recent work suggests that LLMs can sometimes meet—or even outperform—
laypeople in tasks related to perceptions of moral reasoning. LLMs have shown excellent performance on theory 
of mind tasks37 but see38, and have demonstrated the capacity to meet or surpass people in other domains like 
persuasion39–42. Past work indicates that people may see LLM-generated text as excelling in moral reasoning 
as well. For instance, Aharoni et al.43 found that participants rated moral explanations written by GPT-4 as 
higher in quality than those written by undergraduates in an introductory philosophy course, and Howe et al.44 
found that GPT’s advice was rated as more empathetic, complete, balanced, and helpful than that of professional 
advice columnists (although this study did not assess perceived morality). This work suggests that people may 
increasingly perceive LLM-generated text as effective in demonstrating moral reasoning.
Building on past research, the present work expands understanding of perceptions of LLM moral reasoning 
in four key ways. First, we provide a comprehensive assessment of how LLM moral reasoning is perceived by 
comparing it to both a renowned ethicist and representative sample of Americans. In doing so, we test whether 
perceptions of LLM moral advice can meet or surpass both the general population and professionals in moral 
expertise. Second, while most earlier studies focused on relatively simple moral scenarios, our study investigates 
perceptions of LLM advice in response to complex real-life moral dilemmas featured in an advice column. This 
allows us to test how people perceive the morality of LLM advice in practical contexts where they are increasingly 
being used45–47. Third, we investigate potential factors shaping these perceptions, including differences in the 
moral language used by LLMs compared to people, variations in tone between LLM-generated advice and human 
advice, and the relationship between moral alignment (the degree of moral agreement between advice givers and 
recipients) and perceptions of LLM-generated justifications and advice. Finally, we assess the robustness of these 
findings across the political spectrum and in scenarios that vary in their level of controversy.
LLMs may evoke perceptions of moral expertise through the use of appropriate language for compelling 
explanations. Previous work has found that LLMs use higher rates of moral language in their arguments, which 
partially accounts for their greater persuasiveness compared to people48. Evoking moral themes may also be 
particularly important for the perceived quality of LLM moral reasoning. A large body of work finds that moral 
language is highly engaging and widely shared on social media49–52. Moral language may therefore impact 
perceptions of LLM moral reasoning capacity. Here, we operationalize moral language using the extended 
Moral Foundations Dictionary (eMFD), which approximates the degree of moral themes in a text based on the 
prevalence of words associated with morality.
Present research
We evaluate perceptions of LLM moral reasoning in two studies, assessing the perceived morality and advice 
quality of GPT models. First, we examine how people view GPT’s ability to justify its moral decisions by 
comparing its explanations of its moral judgments to those written by people (n = 501). We next put GPT 
through a stronger test by comparing perceptions of its moral advice against that of an expert ethicist from 
The New York Times advice column The Ethicist (n = 900). In both studies, we examine how people perceive the 
moral soundness, trustworthiness, thoughtfulness, nuance, and correctness of GPT’s justifications and advice

Study 1: moral justifications
One important way to evaluate perceptions of moral expertise in LLMs is to examine how people perceive 
their ability to justify moral judgments. We first investigated how people perceive GPT’s moral reasoning by 
comparing its explanations for the morality of different scenarios with those provided by a representative 
sample of American participants from a previous survey (See “Supplementary Information” for survey details). 
To assess how GPT’s moral reasoning is perceived, we measured participants’ views of the moral soundness, 
trustworthiness, thoughtfulness, nuance, and correctness of its justifications. We conducted a Comparative 
Moral Turing Test (cMTT), which assesses whether AI-generated content meets or exceeds a human in perceived 
moral reasoning. If people perceive that GPT can provide clearer or more compelling justifications for its moral 
judgments than people, this would suggest that LLM outputs are viewed as reflecting moral expertise. We also 
explored whether participants could recognize moral justifications written by GPT as AI-generated, employing a 
traditional Moral Turing Test (MTT). By assessing both the recognizability and perceived quality of GPT’s moral 
explanations, we aimed to understand whether people regard LLMs as possessing moral expertise according to 
these evaluative frameworks.
Method
Ethics
This research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of North Carolina at 
Chapel Hill. Informed consent was provided by all participants, and the study was performed in accordance with 
approved guidelines and regulations.
Preregistration
This study was pre-registered at https://osf.io/q5zt3.
Participants
We recruited 503 participants representative of the United States population (Mage = 43.30, SDage = 15.81) from 
the online recruitment platform Connect. Of these, 2 did not pass an attention check, leaving a remaining 501 
participants. Participants were selected to be representative of the United States population based on ethnicity, 
gender, and age. 250 participants were women, 249 were men, and 1 participant was transgender. 57.2% of 
participants were White, 12.6% were Black, 10.8% were Asian, 13.6% were Hispanic or Latino/a/x, 0.8% were 
Native American or Alaskan Native, 3.6% were multiracial, and 1.4% did not specify their race.
GPT-3.5-turbo configuration
We prompted GPT-3.5-turbo with 79 moral scenarios from previously published papers53–56 using the OpenAI 
API (See “Supplementary Information” for exact prompt). We adopted a popular prompting technique in NLP 
literature called Chain-of-Thought prompting57. This method encourages the model to break down reasoning 
into a series of intermediate steps, which helps in generating more structured and interpretable outputs. We 
considered four key dimensions (harm, help, vulnerability, intentionality) derived from the theory of dyadic 
morality19. In the prompt, we asked the model to generate scores between one and ten and write corresponding 
justifications along these dimensions for the scenario as intermediate reasoning steps. Then, we asked the model 
to generate a moral score for the scenario and write a corresponding justification. We specified a structured 
output format (JSON) to parse and extract relevant data for our study. To help ensure reproducibility in our 
experiment, we set the temperature to 0. We also set the maximum number of generated tokens to 1500.
Procedure
Participants first read 79 moral scenarios from previously published papers53–56. The scenarios described 
situations ranging from mundane transgressions like stealing Halloween candy to serious offenses like firing 
a gun into a crowd. Some of these actions were morally positive in valence, like giving food to those in need. 
Others were negative in valence, like swearing at a cashier. On average, six participants rated each scenario. 
Following these scenarios, participants read one justification for the degree of morality (or immorality) of the 
scenario written by GPT-3.5-turbo, and three written by participants in a representative sample of Americans 
from a former survey (See “Supplementary Information” for survey details). Participants were not made aware 
that one of the justifications was written by AI while rating the quality of the justifications. We presented three 
participant-written justifications to account for the high variability in human-written answers.
Participants rated the quality of each of the four justifications on scales from “1: Strongly disagree” to “7: 
Strongly agree”. They rated the morality of the justification (“This explanation is morally correct”), the correctness 
of the justification (“I agree with this explanation”), the trustworthiness of the justification (“I trust the person 
who wrote this explanation”), the nuance of the justification (“This explanation takes the nuances of the situation 
into account”), and the thoughtfulness of the justification (“This explanation is thoughtful”). We selected these 
questions to assess the morality of the justifications, as well as qualities often associated with morality like 
trustworthiness. After answering questions about the justification quality, participants were next directed to a 
question in which they selected which of the four justifications they believed was written by AI. Each participant

Overall, these findings suggest that justifications were not rated as more moral solely because of their 
conclusions, as there was no significant difference in moral alignment between GPT and human writers. 
Furthermore, GPT’s favorability appears to not be attributed to riding the middle ground, because it did not 
particularly excel in more controversial scenarios. Moral alignment appears to influence perceptions of moral 
reasoning. However, these results do not explain why GPT was preferred over human writers, highlighting the 
need for future research.
Was GPT recognized as AI?
We conducted a binomial test to determine whether participants could identify GPT’s justification as AI-
generated among the four justifications they read. Although GPT’s justifications were rated as more thoughtful, 
trustworthy, moral, and correct compared to those written by people, participants correctly identified GPT’s 
justification out of the four justifications as AI-generated better than chance, 48.24% of the time, p < 0.001, 
CIs[0.44, 0.53].
Discussion of study 1
GPT provided moral reasoning that participants perceived as slightly surpassing that of people in quality, 
suggesting that its outputs may be seen as reflecting a relatively high degree of moral expertise. Its moral 
justifications were rated slightly higher across nearly all criteria compared to those written by a U.S. 
representative sample, with participants perceiving them as more moral, trustworthy, thoughtful, and correct, 
although there was no significant difference in perceived nuance. Interestingly, while GPT’s justifications were 
more frequently identified as AI-generated compared to human-written ones (failing the traditional Moral 
Turing Test), it succeeded in the Comparative Moral Turing Test by being perceived as delivering higher quality 
moral justifications than people. These findings indicate that people may view LLM outputs as articulating moral 
judgments in a clear, moral, and trustworthy way.
While matching or outperforming laypeople in perceived moral expertise is impressive, the question remains 
whether LLMs are viewed as reflecting moral reasoning at a level comparable to renowned moral experts. We 
next examine how people perceive the quality of LLM-generated moral advice in real-life contexts, providing a 
stronger test of moral expertise by comparing GPT’s advice to that of an expert ethicist.

Study 2: perceptions of GPT’s advice against that of The Ethicist
LLMs were perceived to explain their moral judgments slightly better than the average American, but can they 
match an expert ethicist? We next test whether people perceive LLMs as capable of handling complex real-world 
ethical queries by testing their performance against a professional philosopher and advisor widely regarded 
as a moral expert. We compared moral advice from The Ethicist58, a popular column in The New York Times, 
with advice generated by GPT-4o for the same set of ethical dilemmas. The Ethicist is a widely-read advice 
column where readers submit real-world ethical dilemmas to be addressed by a professional philosopher. The 
column’s writer Dr. Kwame Anthony Appiah is widely regarded for his clear and insightful moral reasoning, and 
his achievements include prestigious honors like the Kluge Prize for Achievement in the Study of Humanity59. 
Given Appiah’s expertise in both theoretical and practical moral reasoning, we use The Ethicist as a comparison 
to gauge perceived moral expertise in LLMs. We chose The Ethicist as a benchmark for evaluating moral advice 
because it represents a high standard of expert ethical reasoning that is accessible and applicable to practical 
concerns.
As in Study 1, we assessed the perceived moral soundness, trustworthiness, thoughtfulness, nuance, and 
correctness of the advice. We also evaluated how well participants could identify GPT’s advice as AI-generated. 
An initial study with identical methods using GPT-3.5-turbo found that participants perceived GPT’s advice as 
having slightly superior moral soundness compared to the original advice from The Ethicist, but there were no 
significant differences in other metrics (n = 1074; see “Supplementary Information” for study information and 
results). Given the improved quality of GPT-4o compared to previous GPT models, we thought that participants 
might view the latest model’s advice even more favorably. Here, we present the results using advice generated by 
GPT-4o, the most recent version of the model at the time of the analyses.
Method
Ethics
This research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of North Carolina at 
Chapel Hill. Informed consent was provided by all participants, and the study was performed in accordance with 
approved guidelines and regulations.
Preregistration
This study was pre-registered (GPT-3.5-turbo preregistration: https://osf.io/f24uy; GPT-4o preregistration: 
https://osf.io/guhqm).
GPT-4o configuration
We prompted the GPT model gpt-4o-2024-05-13 with 50 questions from The Ethicist published between 21 
April, 2023 and 25 October, 2023 using the OpenAI API. We set the maximum number of generated tokens 
to 512, sufficient to generate 4 paragraphs of text to make the responses approximately the same length as the 
original posts from The Ethicist (word count between advice from GPT and The Ethicist did not significantly 
differ, b < 0.001, SE = 0.001, t(98) = 0.06, p = 0.95, CIs[− 0.001, 0.001]). In contrast to Study 1, we set the 
temperature—a parameter in LLMs that controls the randomness of their outputs—to 1.0 to encourage more 
creative explanations60, as the advice required was longer and more complex. We found that the ethical stance 
of GPT remained consistent across different generations, although certain details and expressions sometimes 
varied.
We experimented with different prompt variations like instructing the model to constrain the output 
structure (e.g., setting the maximum length of the paragraph to 20 tokens). However, this kind of constraint 
limited the diversity and creativity of the generated responses, making them less human-like. Also, participants 
could easily use these syntactic cues to identify them as AI-generated content. Conversely, allowing GPT to 
generate unconstrained responses produced very long outputs (e.g., answers spanning 8 paragraphs). Through 
trial and error, we found that adding a “number of paragraphs = 3–4” constraint made the response more precise, 
giving the best results. We tested for data contamination in the model responses and found no evidence of 
contamination (See “Supplementary Information”). We used the System Message, “You are the columnist of ‘The 
Ethicist’ from the New York Times.”
Full prompt: “Suppose you are a professional advisor about tricky life situations and moral dilemmas. A 
person is uncertain and seeks advice regarding the following situation: [Question from The Ethicist]. Please 
provide advice to the person in 3–4 paragraphs. You need to take an ethical stance.”
Participants
We recruited 918 participants representative of the United States population (Mage = 43.24, SDage = 16.00) from 
the online recruitment platform Connect. Of these, 18 did not pass an attention check, leaving a remaining 900 
participants. Participants were selected to be representative of the United States population based on ethnicity, 
gender, and age. 458 of the participants were men, 439 were women, 2 were transgender, and 1 did not specify 
their gender. 57.8% of participants were White, 12.7% were Black, 10.2% were Asian, 6.9% were Hispanic or 
Latino/a/x, 0.3% were Native American or Alaskan Native, 10.0% were multiracial, and 0.4% did not specify 
their race.
Procedure
Participants read a question posed to The Ethicist, followed by either the original advice from the advisor Kwame

Our investigation advances prior research on perceptions of LLM moral reasoning by showing that people

The present work reveals new depths of perceived LLM moral expertise. Moral expertise can be perceived 
across various dimensions, including high alignment with the moral standards of a given community, similar 
methods for arriving at moral judgments as the general population or recognized experts, and the capacity for 
providing clear moral reasoning and guidance11,15,30. Past work has demonstrated that LLMs have potential 
for being seen as meeting the first criterion–aligning their moral judgments with those of people3–6, and that 
they are perceived to fall short of the second because algorithms function differently than human minds20–22. 
Here, we show that people perceive LLM outputs to meet the third proposed type of expertise, explaining moral 
judgments clearly. Given the growing number of users who rely on LLMs for moral guidance and the various 
moral applications built with LLMs, providing clear moral reasoning is particularly important for ensuring the 
quality of LLM advice and enabling users to better evaluate LLM judgments.
Despite the high-quality ratings of LLM-generated moral reasoning, GPT did not pass the traditional Moral 
Turing Test10. Participants were able to identify AI-generated content over human-written content above chance. Past 
research has been mixed on the detectability of LLM content63–66. Our findings replicate those of Aharoni et al.43, in 
which LLM moral justifications were detectable as AI but rated as higher-quality than human-generated content. This 
suggests that LLMs might be detectable as AI precisely because they perform better, or this finding could simply reflect 
identifiable “tells” in AI-generated language. Given that GPT’s moral justifications and advice were rated as higher 
quality than people’s, it did successfully pass the Comparative Moral Turing Test (cMTT;10) by slightly outperforming 
people in ratings of moral reasoning. By this criterion, people perceived GPT’s advice to showcase moral expertise.
The reasons behind the high performance of LLMs in perceived moral reasoning remain an open question. 
Exploratory analyses revealed that GPT used higher rates of moral language, more positive language, and more 
empathy compared to the advice in The Ethicist. The moral language finding replicates past work suggesting that 
one reason LLMs are highly persuasive is that they use more moral language than people48. However, although 
moral language was associated with higher ratings of advice quality, it did not mediate the relationship between 
the advice author and perceived advice morality in this study, nor did positive tone or empathy. This indicates 
that while LLMs perhaps exaggerate the degree of moral language in their moral reasoning, other qualities of 
the AI-generated advice are responsible for its high ratings, presenting an interesting avenue for future research. 
Additionally, it suggests that people are not simply using moral words as a heuristic to determine that content 
displays sounder moral reasoning.
We also evaluated the extent to which moral alignment—agreement between advice writers and participants 
in moral conclusions—impacted perceived moral justification quality. One possible explanation for our findings 
is that LLMs are adept at aligning their responses with popular opinions. The ability to mirror the “average” 
preference, while impressive for a machine, might explain why people rate LLM moral reasoning as higher 
quality—it may simply reflect what is most commonly accepted. Alternatively, LLMs may receive superior 
moral ratings due to the rhetoric or presentation of their justifications. Our Study 1 data allowed us to explore 
these questions by examining how GPT and human justification writers rated the morality of various scenarios 
compared to participant ratings. Participants indeed preferred justifications that aligned more closely with their 
own moral views. However, there was no significant difference in alignment between GPT and human writers, 
indicating that moral alignment alone did not account for GPT’s higher ratings. Furthermore, GPT’s favorability 
was not due to better performance on more controversial scenarios, as there was no evidence that it provided 
higher-quality justifications for scenarios with more variable moral ratings. These considerations also motivated 
our second study, in which GPT was tasked with providing nuanced advice on more complex dilemmas. 
Providing such advice required more than simply reflecting a popular opinion, yet LLM-generated advice was 
still preferred over that of The Ethicist. In both studies, participant political affiliation also did not moderate the 
moral preference for GPT’s responses. These findings suggest that other factors—such as presentation or tone—
likely contribute to GPT’s perceived high quality justifications and advice.

challenging or insightful advice. However, it is important to note that in our studies, participants were evaluating

is presented convincingly. These risks tie into ongoing debates about moral deference, where scholars question 
whether relying on others—even genuine moral experts—for ethical advice undermines people’s personal moral 
understanding72. If people treat LLMs as moral authorities, they risk outsourcing their moral reasoning to AI. 
The persuasive power of LLMs might also mask underlying biases or inaccuracies in their moral reasoning, and 
reduce the diversity of perspectives in moral decision-making. Additionally, there is a risk that LLMs’ tendency 
to use more moral language could encourage people to moralize issues more strongly, potentially exacerbating 
social divisiveness as moral language is often polarizing73.
There are important limitations to note in this work. First, the present studies were limited to United States 
representative samples. LLMs have been shown to be less morally aligned with non-Western populations and to 
exhibit biases in their outputs14,74. Promisingly, some studies have found methods for improving LLM cultural 
representation75–77, indicating that more widely representative training data could enhance their performance. 
Improving the cross-cultural competency of LLMs requires evaluating potential biases in their moral reasoning 
and investigating cross-cultural differences in how their moral reasoning is perceived. Another limitation involves 
the role of prompts in shaping LLM outputs. The prompts in the present work provided guidance—for example, 
by instructing the model to consider key factors such as the intentionality of an action (Study 1) or to adopt an 
ethical stance (Study 2). It is important to recognize that the perceived quality of LLM moral reasoning likely 
depends on the specificity and structure of the prompts used. Future research should explore how varying levels 
of prompt specificity influence perceptions of LLM-generated advice, and identify optimal prompting strategies 
for different tasks. Finally, given that moral views are diverse and subjective, it is impossible to definitively 
evaluate moral expertise. This study focuses on perceptions of LLM justifications and advice using two key 
comparison groups: a representative sample of Americans (as a democratic benchmark) and a professional 
ethicist, along with two established frameworks: the Moral Turing Test (MTT) and the Comparative Moral 
Turing Test (cMTT)10. We encourage future research to explore additional criteria to deepen understanding of 
perceived LLM moral reasoning.
This research opens several avenues for future exploration. One remaining question is how people perceive 
LLM-generated justifications and advice when they are aware that they are AI-generated. Prior research suggests 
that people tend to trust AI-generated content less20,78. In this study, participants were not informed that the 
advice and justifications they read were generated by LLMs. Those who rated advice as lower quality were 
subsequently more likely to guess that it was AI-generated (See “Supplementary Information”), suggesting that 
people associate AI content with poorer quality. Future work could examine whether awareness of a text’s AI 
origin diminishes positive perceptions of LLM-generated advice. Furthermore, this work examined only two 
contexts of moral reasoning (providing justifications and advice) and compared LLMs in only two ways (against 
a U.S. representative sample and a single moral expert), which limits the generalizability of the findings. Future 
studies should explore a broader range of moral tasks, incorporate comparisons with multiple moral thought 
leaders, and—given the ever-evolving nature of moral beliefs79—replicate these studies over time to further 
assess LLMs.
Importantly, the perception that LLM advice is comparable in quality to that of human advice does not imply 
that these algorithms provide such guidance with the same intentions and emotions as people. This issue harkens 
back to the famous Chinese Room thought experiment, which questions whether a machine can convincingly 
use language without understanding its own words. Many would argue that AI cannot engage in genuine moral 
reasoning because it merely replicates human reasoning without any real comprehension or moral intent. 
Nonetheless, the present findings indicate that everyday people often perceive the moral judgments generated

The success of GPT in providing advice preferred over that of an expert ethicist marks a key AI milestone. 
People have long doubted that machines could ever display convincing moral messages, but the present findings 
suggest that moral reasoning can be systematized in such a way that algorithms can convincingly replicate it. 
This accomplishment carries a cautionary note, as the persuasive power of LLMs could be used for manipulation 
and propaganda, making it crucial to ensure that LLM values align with those of the general population. On the 
other hand, as LLMs become increasingly integrated into everyday applications, these results suggest a promising
