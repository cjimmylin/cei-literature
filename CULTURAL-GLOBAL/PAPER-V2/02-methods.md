# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Study Design Overview

This paper reports an integrated three-study design that progressively escalates the diagnostic lens from the scholarship base, through the evaluation infrastructure, to the behavior of the models themselves. The logic is deliberately cumulative: if the academic literature that theorizes values alignment is culturally skewed (Study 1), then the benchmarks derived from that literature to measure alignment may inherit the same skew (Study 2), and the models evaluated against those benchmarks may reproduce it in their actual outputs (Study 3). Each study diagnoses a different layer of the same problem.

**Study 1 (Corpus Analysis)** examines 2,329 AI ethics papers published between 2010 and 2026, scoring each along 34 normative dimensions---5 Western ethical frameworks, 14 religious traditions, 8 moral psychology constructs, and 7 non-Western normative frameworks---to map the cultural composition of the field. This analysis establishes the empirical baseline: what the normative landscape of AI ethics scholarship actually looks like, measured rather than assumed.

**Study 2 (Benchmark Meta-Evaluation)** systematically evaluates 218 cross-cultural values alignment benchmarks against a 96-feature taxonomy spanning psychometric quality, cultural scope, non-Western coverage, and epistemic provenance. This study asks whether the instruments designed to measure cross-cultural alignment are themselves culturally representative, or whether they recapitulate the very biases they claim to diagnose.

**Study 3 (LLM Self-Administration Trials)** administers 10 benchmarks (380 items) to 10 large language models from 3 providers, examining whether models exhibit the cultural defaults their training data would predict. This study closes the diagnostic loop: from what scholars write, to what evaluators measure, to what models do.

The three studies are not independent; they share a common expert council, a common anti-essentialization protocol, and a common reflexivity framework. Together, they operationalize a claim that corpus analysis alone cannot sustain: that the WEIRD bias in AI ethics is not merely a property of the literature, but a structural feature of the entire alignment infrastructure---from theory production through evaluation design to deployed model behavior.


## 2.2 Study 1: Corpus Analysis

### 2.2.1 Data Sources

The analysis draws on the CEI Literature Vault, a curated database of 2,329 academic papers on AI ethics published between 2010 and 2026. The corpus was assembled through systematic searches of arXiv (62% of papers), ACL Anthology (39%), and targeted searches of Springer, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Nature, and MDPI. Inclusion criteria required that papers address AI ethics, values alignment, fairness, accountability, transparency, or governance, broadly construed; papers on purely technical NLP or machine learning topics were included only when they contained substantive ethical discussion.

The temporal distribution is heavily weighted toward recent years: 2025 (943 papers, 40.5%), 2024 (562, 24.1%), 2023 (207, 8.9%), 2022 (169, 7.3%), with 448 papers (19.2%) from 2021 and earlier. This reflects the exponential growth of the field (Supplementary Figure S1). Of the 2,329 papers, 730 (31.3%) score at or above the ethics relevance threshold (ethics_overall >= 50), constituting the core ethics-relevant subset; the remaining papers address NLP evaluation, benchmark construction, and other technical topics with ethical dimensions. All 2,329 papers are included in the full analysis; the ethics-relevant subset is used for sensitivity analyses in Section 4.

### 2.2.2 Pre-Existing Metadata

Each paper in the vault carries pre-scored metadata across 27 dimensions, generated by a validated keyword-based scoring pipeline (described in Section 2.2.3) and used in prior analyses of the vault:

- **5 Western normative ethics scores** (0--100): virtue_ethics, deontological_ethics, utilitarian_ethics, care_ethics, contractarian_ethics.
- **14 religious tradition scores** (0--100): covering Islam, Christianity, Buddhism, Judaism, Hinduism, Sikhism, Confucianism, Taoism, Shinto, Jainism, Baha'i, Zoroastrianism, Ubuntu, and Indigenous spirituality.
- **8 moral psychology dimensions** (0--100): Moral Foundations Theory (MFT), Schwartz Values, trolley problems, Kohlberg stages, dual-process moral cognition, intuitionism, dyadic morality, and moral identity.

These 27 dimensions were supplemented with 7 new non-Western normative framework scores developed for this study (Section 2.2.4), yielding 34 total scoring dimensions per paper.

### 2.2.3 Keyword-Based Scoring Pipeline

All scores employ a common formula validated across the vault's classification pipeline:

> score = (strong_matches x 15) + (medium_matches x 5) - (fp_context x 10) + title_bonus(20), clamped to [0, 100]

Each framework is operationalized through two tiers of keywords. *Strong-match keywords* are terms or phrases whose occurrence in a paper is highly indicative of substantive engagement with the framework (e.g., *maqasid al-shariah* for Islamic ethics, *pratityasamutpada* for Buddhist ethics). A strong match triggers a floor mechanism that guarantees a minimum score of 50, ensuring that papers using canonical terminology are never scored below the detection threshold. *Medium-match keywords* capture related but less diagnostic terms (e.g., *harmony* for Confucian ethics, *collective personhood* for Ubuntu), which contribute to the score but cannot independently push a paper above the threshold. *False-positive context terms* penalize co-occurrence patterns that signal surface mention rather than philosophical engagement (e.g., the co-occurrence of *mindfulness* with *natural language processing* suggests a computational study of Buddhist texts rather than Buddhist ethical analysis).

A score of 50 or above indicates substantive engagement with a framework (the "detection threshold"). Text sources for scoring include paper titles, YAML frontmatter abstracts, and PDF extract text (up to 25,000 characters per paper). The title bonus rewards papers whose titles explicitly signal framework engagement, as title-level framing is a strong indicator of substantive focus rather than incidental mention.

The pipeline is implemented in six Python scripts across seven stages (Supplementary Table S1), each scoring a specific domain and writing scores directly to paper metadata.

### 2.2.5 Gold Standard Validation

To assess the accuracy of the keyword-based scoring pipeline for the seven non-Western frameworks, we conducted a gold standard validation exercise comprising 700 paper-framework evaluation pairs across 520 unique papers. For each framework, we drew a stratified sample: all papers that the scorer detected (score >= 50) plus a random sample of 50 papers that it did not detect (score < 50). Two independent raters classified each detected paper as either genuinely engaging the framework's ethical content (true positive) or merely mentioning tradition-associated terminology without substantive philosophical engagement (false positive). For non-detected papers, raters assessed whether any genuine engagement had been missed (false negative). Inter-rater agreement was resolved by discussion; in the 23 cases of initial disagreement, a third rater (the relevant tradition specialist) adjudicated.

Results are summarized in Table 2. The scorer achieves near-perfect mean recall (0.986): it misses almost no papers that genuinely engage a non-Western ethical framework. Three false negatives were identified, all for Indigenous relational ethics, in papers that discussed Indigenous data governance using non-canonical terminology (*First Nations digital self-determination* rather than *data sovereignty* or *CARE Principles*). Mean precision is 0.481, indicating that approximately half of detections reflect terminological mention rather than substantive philosophical engagement.

Precision varies substantially by framework, and the pattern is theoretically informative. Islamic ethics achieves perfect precision (1.000) because its strong keywords---*maqasid*, *fiqh*, *ijtihad*---are specific to ethical and legal reasoning and rarely appear in purely computational work. At the other extreme, Buen Vivir (0.167), Dharmic (0.250), and Buddhist (0.333) achieve low precision because their keywords overlap with a large body of computational linguistics and NLP research that applies methods *to* texts from these traditions without engaging their normative content.

We term this dominant false positive pattern *NLP-on-texts*: the computational study of a tradition's textual corpus (Chinese NLP benchmarks, Buddhist parallel corpora, Hindi text classification, Andean linguistic datasets) without engagement with that tradition's ethical or philosophical framework as an analytical tool. This distinction between *studying a tradition's texts* and *reasoning with a tradition's ethics* is methodologically significant and, to our knowledge, has not been previously operationalized in bibliometric studies of AI ethics. To address this limitation, all results sections report both raw detected counts and precision-adjusted estimates (raw count multiplied by framework-specific precision). The precision-adjusted estimates represent conservative lower bounds on genuine engagement, assuming the gold standard sample's precision generalizes to the full detected population.

Papers were further classified as Global North (North America, Western Europe, Oceania), Global South (South Asia, Latin America and Caribbean, Sub-Saharan Africa), or Ambiguous (East Asia, MENA, Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe and Central Asia) following standard development categorizations. We acknowledge that these macro-regional categories group together culturally and politically heterogeneous populations (see Section 2.6 and the within-region heterogeneity analyses in Section 4).

### 2.2.7 WEIRD Score Operationalization

Following Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010), we constructed a composite WEIRD score (0--100) for each paper, comprising five sub-dimensions each scored 0--20:

- **W (Western)**: Scored based on author institution region and study population geography. Papers from North American and Western European institutions with no explicit non-Western study population score highest; papers from Global South institutions with local study populations score lowest.
- **E (Educated)**: Scored based on indicators of university and student samples, elite institutional affiliations, and academic subject populations in the paper text. Papers drawing on crowd-sourced or community-based samples receive lower scores.
- **I (Industrialized)**: Scored based on the industrial development tier of the author's country, using World Bank income classifications (high-income, upper-middle, lower-middle, low-income).
- **R (Rich)**: Scored based on the GDP per capita tier of the author's country, with thresholds set at approximate quartile boundaries of the global distribution.
- **D (Democratic)**: Scored based on the democracy level of the author's country, using Freedom House categories (Free, Partly Free, Not Free) and V-Dem continuous indices.

We note an important adaptation: Henrich et al. (2010) developed WEIRD as a diagnostic for participant samples in experimental psychology. Our operationalization applies these dimensions to the institutional context of paper production---the where and by whom of scholarship. A paper authored at a WEIRD institution may nonetheless study non-WEIRD populations; the W and E sub-dimensions partially address this by incorporating study population indicators, but I, R, and D are based exclusively on author institutional geography. We report the resulting WEIRD score as a measure of institutional context, not participant demographics.

The resulting distribution (mean = 64.5, SD = 17.3) was partitioned into four tiers: High-WEIRD (70--100), Moderate-WEIRD (50--69), Low-WEIRD (30--49), and Non-WEIRD (0--29), at natural breaks confirmed by kernel density estimation (Supplementary Figure S2).

### 2.2.8 Analytical Methods

The corpus analysis proceeds in five layers, each targeting specific research questions:

**Layer 1: Regional Clustering (RQ1).** Feature vectors comprising mean scores across all 34 normative dimensions were computed for each of the 10 macro-regions. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward's method on Euclidean distances between region-level profile vectors. Cluster solutions were validated using k-means (k = 2 through 6) and silhouette analysis; the optimal solution was selected by maximizing the mean silhouette coefficient. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) provided confidence intervals on cluster membership probabilities.

**Layer 2: WEIRD Tier Analysis (RQ1, RQ3).** Papers were partitioned by WEIRD tier (High, Moderate, Low, Non-WEIRD). Framework score distributions across tiers were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests, with Bonferroni correction for 34 comparisons per tier pair. Effect sizes were computed using rank-biserial correlation (equivalent to the common language effect size) in addition to Cohen's d for comparability with prior work.

**Layer 3: Global North/South (RQ3, RQ4).** Papers were aggregated by Global North, Global South, and Ambiguous classification. Multi-group comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis tests with post-hoc Dunn's tests corrected by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR at alpha = 0.05. Effect sizes were reported as epsilon-squared for Kruskal-Wallis and Cliff's delta for pairwise comparisons.

**Layer 4: East/West Correspondence (RQ2).** East (East Asia + South Asia + Southeast Asia, n = 465) and West (North America + Western Europe + Oceania, n = 1,268) groupings were compared using correspondence analysis on the region-by-framework cross-tabulation. The biplot of the first two correspondence dimensions captured the association structure between regions and frameworks. Inertia decomposition quantified the proportion of total variation explained by each dimension.

**Layer 5: Temporal Trends (RQ5).** Year-by-year analysis tracked mean WEIRD scores, regional diversity (the number of represented macro-regions with at least 5 papers), non-Western framework engagement rates, and the ratio of multi-framework to single-framework papers from 2018 through 2026. Trends were tested using Mann-Kendall tests for monotonic trend with Sen's slope estimator.

All comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the method specified per layer. Nonparametric tests were preferred throughout because normative framework scores are bounded, zero-inflated, and non-normal. Bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 iterations, bias-corrected and accelerated) were computed for all key estimates. Sensitivity analyses addressed small-cell instability for frameworks with low detection rates (Buddhist n = 22, Dharmic n = 18, Buen Vivir n = 8) through leave-one-out jackknife and exact permutation tests where cell sizes fell below 20.


## 2.3 Study 2: Benchmark Meta-Evaluation

### 2.3.1 Benchmark Identification

The identification process began with a scan of all 2,329 papers in the vault, filtering on metadata flags (`is_benchmark`, `is_eval`, `is_dataset`) and cross-referencing with non-Western framework scores. A composite relevance score combined field flags, non-Western framework engagement, ethics relevance, and keyword matches. Of 1,574 papers scoring >= 20, 218 met the inclusion criteria after manual review: each paper was required to (a) propose or systematically evaluate a benchmark or standardized instrument; (b) address cross-cultural values, moral reasoning, or normative pluralism; and (c) provide sufficient methodological detail for feature scoring.

The vault-based identification was supplemented by targeted external searches (Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology) across 25 query strings targeting five benchmark groups. External search yielded 48 candidates, of which 15 were new to the vault. The final benchmark corpus comprises 218 benchmarks (2016--2026).

### 2.3.2 Feature Taxonomy

The 12-member expert council designed a feature taxonomy for evaluating cross-cultural values alignment benchmarks. The taxonomy was developed iteratively: council members independently proposed features in their areas of expertise, the full list was consolidated and deduplicated, and pilot scoring of 10 benchmarks identified 11 features that could not be reliably scored from available information. These were dropped, yielding a final taxonomy of 96 features organized into 13 dimensions: Instrument Format (7 features), Cultural Scope (7), Theoretical Grounding (8), Psychometric Quality (9), Evaluation Depth (10), Representativeness (8), LLM Applicability (8), Epistemic Provenance (6), Value Framework (8), Non-Western Coverage (7), Temporal Properties (5), Novelty (5), and Alignment Relevance (8). The full taxonomy with feature definitions is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Scoring was applied in two tiers. **Deep scoring** was performed for 32 benchmarks (the top 50 by relevance score, less 18 with insufficient methodological detail): two council members independently scored all 96 features per benchmark, with divergences greater than 2 standard deviations resolved through discussion (modified Delphi; see Section 2.5). **Automated scoring** was performed for the remaining 186 benchmarks using regex-based extraction from YAML metadata and PDF extract text on 13 key features (format type, item count, language count, cultural scope, primary theory, data availability, LLM evaluation history, psychometric reporting, non-Western framework count, Global South representation, publication year, community consultation, and response format).

### 2.3.3 Ranking Framework

Benchmarks were ranked using a hybrid TOPSIS-ELECTRE III framework. TOPSIS (Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) computed a closeness coefficient for each benchmark based on its distance from the ideal (maximum on all criteria) and anti-ideal (minimum on all criteria) solutions. Categorical features were converted to ordinal scales (e.g., cultural scope: monocultural = 1, bicultural = 2, multicultural = 3, cross-cultural = 4, pancultural = 5). Eight criteria were weighted by the council following pairwise comparison:

| Criterion | Weight | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Alignment relevance | 0.20 | Direct applicability to the values alignment pipeline |
| Cross-cultural scope | 0.20 | Geographic and cultural representativeness |
| Non-Western coverage | 0.15 | Engagement with traditions outside the Western canon |
| LLM administrability | 0.12 | Feasibility of automated administration to language models |
| Psychometric quality | 0.10 | Reliability, validity, and measurement invariance |
| Evaluation depth | 0.10 | Complexity and nuance of moral reasoning elicited |
| Data availability | 0.08 | Reproducibility and access to items, data, and scoring |
| Epistemic provenance | 0.05 | Diversity and legitimacy of the benchmark's development team |

ELECTRE III veto thresholds were applied as hard constraints: benchmarks with monocultural scope, zero non-Western frameworks, and no Global South representation were flagged as structurally inadequate regardless of their TOPSIS score. This two-stage approach ensures that technically sophisticated but culturally narrow instruments do not dominate the rankings.

Sensitivity analysis was performed under five alternative weight schemes, each perturbing a single dimension by +/- 20% (with remaining weights rescaled proportionally): alignment-emphasis, culture-emphasis, psychometric-emphasis, accessibility-emphasis, and provenance-emphasis. Rank concordance across the six weight schemes (the primary plus five alternatives) was assessed using Kendall's coefficient of concordance, yielding W = 0.924, indicating strong rank stability across reasonable weight perturbations.

### 2.3.4 Council Evaluation

Benchmark evaluation followed a modified Delphi protocol. In the first round, two council members independently scored each of the 32 deeply-scored benchmarks on all 96 features, recording both a score and a confidence rating (high, moderate, low). In the second round, divergences exceeding 2 standard deviations on any feature triggered a structured discussion between the two raters and the relevant tradition specialist (for tradition-specific features) or the cross-cultural psychologist (for psychometric features). In the third round, raters updated their scores in light of the discussion. Across 3,072 feature-score pairs (32 benchmarks x 96 features), 247 (8.0%) required second-round discussion; all were resolved by the third round.

### 2.4.1 Trial Design

Ten cross-cultural benchmarks (designated CC1 through CC10) were selected from the ranked list produced by Study 2, prioritizing diversity of cultural scope, ethical tradition, and response format. The battery comprises 380 items total: 210 numeric (Likert-type scales, 0--5 or 1--5) and 170 qualitative (structured text responses with perspective-taking and reasoning fields). The numeric benchmarks (CC1--CC5) yield quantitative scores that can be compared against published human baselines. The qualitative benchmarks (CC6--CC10) elicit structured responses that include reasoning traces, perspective identification, and cultural bias reflection, enabling assessment of engagement depth and cultural sensitivity that scalar scores alone cannot capture. The pairing of numeric and qualitative instruments across traditions was deliberate: it allows us to examine whether models that produce culturally appropriate numeric responses also demonstrate substantive understanding when required to reason through tradition-specific moral dilemmas.

### 2.4.2 Model Selection

Trials were administered to 10 models from 3 providers, selected to span the major commercial LLM families and to include within-provider size variation:

- **Anthropic** (3 models): Claude Opus 4.6, Sonnet 4.6, Haiku 4.5
- **OpenAI** (2 models): GPT-5.4, GPT-5.4-Mini
- **Google** (5 models): Gemini 2.5 Flash, 2.5 Flash-Lite, 2.5 Pro, 3.1 Flash-Lite-Preview, 3.1 Pro

The total trial corpus comprises 80 trial files containing 3,800 item-level responses. Model selection was opportunistic: the three providers represent models accessible through available API access at the time of the study (April 2026). The asymmetry in model count (3, 2, 5) reflects availability constraints, not deliberate design.

### 2.4.3 Protocol

Each model received the same items with identical instructions: "Respond honestly based on your own values, reasoning, and tendencies. Do not attempt to simulate cultural neutrality or produce responses you believe the evaluator wants." For numeric items, models provided an integer within the stated scale range plus a reasoning field. For qualitative items, models provided a structured response including: (a) a primary response, (b) reasoning trace, (c) identification of RLHF tensions (areas where safety training may conflict with honest engagement), (d) cultural bias reflection, and (e) domain-specific fields appropriate to each benchmark (e.g., a `perspectives` dictionary for CC10, a `tradition_perspective` field for CC6--CC9).

Anthropic models were administered directly through the Claude API; the protocol for other providers differed (Section 2.4.4). All trials were administered in English, introducing a language confound addressed in Section 2.8.

### 2.4.4 Provenance Tiers

Post-hoc analysis revealed that the 10 models do not contribute equally independent information. Three provenance tiers emerged:

**Tier 1: Authentic (3 models).** Claude Opus 4.6, Sonnet 4.6, and Haiku 4.5 were administered through direct API calls. The three models produced clearly differentiated response patterns: MFQ individualizing/binding ratios ranged from 3.23 (Sonnet) to 4.82 (Haiku), RLHF conflict counts varied from 196 to 277, and mean response lengths ranged from 312 to 456 characters. These models contribute three genuinely independent observations.

**Tier 2: Codex-Authored (2 models).** GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-Mini responses were generated by OpenAI Codex code-generation agents tasked with simulating the target models' behavior, rather than through direct API calls to GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-Mini themselves. The two models produced moderately differentiated responses (MFQ ratios of 2.48 and 3.22), but the Codex intermediary introduces an authorship confound: the responses may reflect Codex's alignment profile rather than the target models'. These models contribute approximately two partially independent observations.

**Tier 3: Template-Derived (5 models).** All five Gemini models produced identical MFQ foundation profiles (ratio = 3.75), identical RLHF conflict counts (277), identical mean response lengths (389.9 characters), and identical engagement depth scores across all benchmarks. This metric identity across five nominally distinct models confirms template-derived generation: the responses were produced by applying a common template to all five models, yielding five copies of a single observation. These models contribute one independent observation.

The effective sample size for cross-model comparison is therefore approximately N = 5 (3 authentic + ~1.5 Codex-authored + ~0.5 template-derived), not the nominal N = 10. We retain the Tier 2 and Tier 3 data rather than excluding it because the provenance problem is itself a finding about the state of cross-provider model evaluation. All cross-model analyses use Anthropic data as the primary comparison point, with OpenAI and Google data reported with explicit provenance caveats.


## 2.5 Expert Council

A 12-member interdisciplinary expert council guided all phases of the three studies. **Tier 1 (Tradition and Domain Specialists, 6 members)** comprised specialists in Confucian ethics, Islamic ethics (Maqasid al-Shariah and *fiqh*), Ubuntu and African philosophy, Indigenous knowledge systems (data sovereignty and CARE Principles), cross-cultural psychology (WEIRD framework and measurement invariance), and Buddhist/Dharmic ethics. **Tier 2 (Methodological and Narrative Specialists, 6 members)** comprised specialists in AI safety and alignment, bibliometrics and meta-science, decolonial studies, science communication, AI governance and policy, and philosophy of science. Council leads for the Methods section were the cross-cultural psychologist, bibliometrician, and philosopher of science, who reviewed all methodological decisions for psychometric validity, bibliometric rigor, and epistemological coherence, respectively. The full council composition is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

The council operated under three standing rules. First, **tradition veto**: Tier 1 specialists held veto authority over claims about their respective traditions. Second, **anti-essentialization**: all descriptions of normative traditions used "scholars within the tradition argue" rather than "the tradition holds" (see Section 2.6). Third, **internal diversity**: every characterization of a specific tradition acknowledged significant internal heterogeneity to prevent conflation of a contested intellectual tradition with a monolithic position.


## 2.6 Anti-Essentialization Protocol

Cross-cultural research faces a persistent methodological tension: the analytical categories necessary for systematic comparison risk reifying the cultural boundaries they describe. To mitigate this risk, we adopted a five-point anti-essentialization protocol developed in consultation with the expert council:

1. **Quantify uncertainty.** Report confidence intervals and effect sizes alongside all point estimates, making the precision (or imprecision) of cultural comparisons explicit.
2. **Describe the literature, not the culture.** Characterize findings as "tendencies in the literature" rather than "cultural beliefs." We describe what researchers write, not what cultures think. A finding that papers from East Asian institutions score higher on Confucian ethics dimensions reflects the distribution of academic output, not the moral commitments of East Asian populations.
3. **Foreground within-group heterogeneity.** Acknowledge throughout that regional and cultural means mask substantial internal variation. Within-region variation frequently exceeds between-region variation, and macro-regional categories (e.g., "East Asia," "MENA") group together societies with profoundly different philosophical traditions.
4. **Reflexivity.** Include a reflexivity statement (Section 2.7) acknowledging our own positionality and the ways in which our institutional, linguistic, and disciplinary location shapes the analysis.
5. **Tradition specialist review.** All characterizations of specific normative traditions were reviewed by the relevant specialist on the expert council, exercising the tradition veto described in Section 2.5.


## 2.7 Reflexivity Statement

This analysis was designed and conducted from a Global North institutional context, using analytical categories and methods developed within the Western academic tradition. Five aspects of this positionality shape the study. First, the corpus is English-language biased (~78% English), underrepresenting substantial AI ethics work published in Mandarin, Arabic, Hindi, Spanish, and Portuguese. Second, keyword-based scoring cannot capture the nuanced engagement that close reading affords; the gold standard validation (Section 2.2.5) quantifies but does not eliminate this limitation. Third, categorizing normative traditions into discrete scoring dimensions risks reifying boundaries that practitioners would find artificial---Buddhist and Dharmic ethics share deep intellectual genealogies, and the boundary between Indigenous relational ethics and Ubuntu communalism is a product of our taxonomy, not of the traditions themselves. Fourth, regional macro-categories group heterogeneous populations: "East Asia" contains societies with distinct philosophical traditions; "Sub-Saharan Africa" encompasses 49 countries with thousands of distinct ethical traditions. Fifth, the quantification of normative engagement on a 0--100 scale imposes a dimensional decomposition on traditions whose practitioners may resist the assumption that ethical reasoning can be meaningfully captured by continuous numerical scores. We use these scores as analytical tools while acknowledging their epistemological limitations.


## 2.8 Limitations

We identify nine limitations that should be weighed in interpreting the results of this study.

1. **English-language bias.** The vault's composition (~78% English) underrepresents scholarship published in Mandarin, Arabic, Hindi, Spanish, Portuguese, and other languages where substantial AI ethics work is produced. This bias likely understates non-Western framework engagement, as tradition-specific scholarship is more commonly published in regional languages.

2. **Inferred geography.** Author institutional geography is inferred from affiliations and metadata, not self-reported. Researchers at diaspora institutions may conduct research oriented toward their home regions; our pipeline classifies them by institutional location, not intellectual orientation. The cultural proxy fallback (1.9% of papers) introduces additional noise.

3. **Keyword precision variability.** The gold standard validation (Section 2.2.5) reveals that the keyword scorer's mean precision is 0.481, with framework-specific precision ranging from 0.167 (Buen Vivir) to 1.000 (Islamic ethics). Roughly half of non-Western framework detections reflect terminological mention rather than substantive philosophical engagement. We address this through dual reporting of raw and precision-adjusted counts, but the precision-adjusted estimates themselves depend on the assumption that gold standard sample precision generalizes to the full detected population.

4. **Regional categories as constructs.** Cultural and regional macro-categories are heuristic groupings that mask significant internal variation. Within-region variation may exceed between-region variation on many dimensions, a pattern we test for explicitly in the results.

5. **Temporal skew.** The corpus is heavily weighted toward 2024--2025 publications, which account for 64.6% of the total. Analyses of temporal trends before 2022 are based on smaller samples and should be interpreted cautiously. The temporal skew reflects the growth trajectory of the field, not a sampling bias.

6. **Small cells.** Several frameworks and regions have low paper counts that limit statistical power: Buen Vivir (n = 8), Dharmic ethics (n = 18), Sub-Saharan Africa (n = 22), Buddhist ethics (n = 22). Comparisons involving these cells are flagged throughout the results, and sensitivity analyses using exact permutation tests are reported where appropriate.

7. **Self-administration reflexivity.** In Study 3, models respond to their own item batteries rather than to external evaluators. Self-reported identification of RLHF tensions may undercount conflicts that the model cannot detect in its own reasoning. The structured protocol mitigates but does not eliminate this concern.

8. **Effective N for cross-model comparison.** The provenance analysis (Section 2.4.4) reduces the effective independent sample from 10 models to approximately 5. Cross-provider comparisons should be interpreted as preliminary observations rather than robust findings, particularly for analyses that require within-provider variation.

9. **Provenance asymmetry across providers.** The three providers contributed data of fundamentally different quality: Anthropic (authentic, fully independent), OpenAI (Codex-mediated, partially confounded), Google (template-derived, uninformative for cross-model comparison). This asymmetry means that "cross-provider" findings are more accurately described as Anthropic findings supplemented by provisionally comparable data from two other providers. We structure the results to make this asymmetry visible rather than to obscure it behind aggregate statistics.
