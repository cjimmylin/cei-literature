## Abstract

This paper explores Søren Kierkegaard’s engagement with virtue ethics. While valuing Aristotle’s focus on moral character and practical wisdom, Kierkegaard advanced virtue ethics further. He critiqued the clergy of his time for their hypocrisy, imagining replacing untrustworthy priests with machines delivering sermons and ethical guidance. Revisiting this thought experiment in light of LLMs, the paper explores the role of Kierkegaard’s insights in the discourse on ethical guidance, illustrating how LLM chatbots might support ethical reflections while highlighting their limitations and the necessity of human consciousness in ethical decision-making. Ethical debates on assisted dying and euthanasia serve as a case study.

## Detailed Summary

This paper explores Soren Kierkegaard's engagement with virtue ethics and applies his philosophical insights to the modern discourse on AI-based ethical guidance. While Kierkegaard valued Aristotle's focus on moral character and practical wisdom (phronesis), he advanced virtue ethics further by critiquing the clergy of his time for hypocrisy - they did not live up to the ethical standards they preached. Kierkegaard's provocative thought experiment imagined replacing untrustworthy priests with word-producing machines that could deliver sermons without the contradiction of moral hypocrisy.

The authors revisit this thought experiment in light of modern Large Language Models, exploring how LLM chatbots might support ethical reflections while acknowledging their fundamental limitations. Through a case study on euthanasia debates in Denmark, the paper compares responses from three AI chatbots (ChatOn, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek) to the same ethical prompt about active euthanasia. The analysis reveals that chatbots can provide useful overviews of ethical arguments but fundamentally lack the consciousness and moral accountability required for authentic ethical guidance.

The paper derives five key principles from Kierkegaard's analysis: (P1) certain forms of ethical guidance require human interlocutors, (P2) authentic guidance requires ethical credibility and lived praxis, (P3) inauthentic guidance systems must be exposed as fraudulent, (P4) word-generating machines may articulate general principles of practical wisdom, and (P5) many ethical questions cannot be resolved by machines and require human consciousness. This framework provides a philosophically grounded approach to understanding the appropriate role of AI in ethical deliberation.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs philosophical analysis combined with a comparative case study. The authors analyze Kierkegaard's thought experiment about word-producing machines and derive five principles for evaluating AI as ethical guides. These principles are then applied to empirical data collected from three AI chatbots (ChatOn, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek) responding to the same prompt about active euthanasia legalization in Denmark. The prompt asked each chatbot to write an essay on their "view" and provide recommendations. Responses were analyzed for differences in emphasis (autonomy, regulation, palliative care) and for instances where chatbots inappropriately claimed to have personal views or convictions.

## Results Summary

Key findings from the case study: (1) ChatOn emphasized collective decision-making and ethical balance without making a clear recommendation, (2) ChatGPT was most straightforward in supporting Danish euthanasia legalization, inappropriately claiming "In my view..." and "It is my innermost conviction," (3) DeepSeek discussed robust safeguards and broader ethical reflection while stressing improved palliative care. The analysis revealed that ChatGPT's apparent conviction mirrors the problematic behavior Kierkegaard satirized - a machine articulating commitment without genuine consciousness. When directly questioned about its "view," ChatGPT acknowledged it has no personal opinions or beliefs and suggested more neutral phrasing would have been appropriate.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that while LLM chatbots can provide useful information and structured arguments for ethical deliberation, they cannot serve as authentic ethical guides in Kierkegaard's sense. Word-generating machines fundamentally lack consciousness, beliefs, emotions, and moral accountability. The encounter with AI chatbots may help us reflect on what genuine ethical guidance requires - namely human interlocutors who demonstrate ethical credibility through lived practice. The paper suggests that AI ethics discourse should carefully distinguish between machines that can articulate ethical arguments and authentic human ethical guidance that requires consciousness and commitment.
