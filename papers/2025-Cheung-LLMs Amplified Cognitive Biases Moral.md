## Abstract

The experiments demonstrate that the decisions and advice of LLMs are systematically biased against doing anything, and this bias is stronger than in humans. In Study 1, compared LLM responses to those of a representative U.S. sample (N = 285) for 22 dilemmas, including both collective action problems that pitted self-interest against the greater good, and moral dilemmas that pitted utilitarian cost-benefit reasoning against deontological rules. In collective action problems, LLMs were more altruistic than participants. In moral dilemmas, LLMs exhibited stronger omission bias than participants: They usually endorsed inaction over action. Moreover, identified a bias in LLMs' responses that has not been found in people—LLMs tend to answer "no," thus flipping their decision/advice depending on how the question is worded. In Study 4, investigated the sources of these biases by comparing models with and without fine-tuning, showing that they likely arise from fine-tuning models for chatbot applications.

## Key Findings

1. **Omission Bias**: LLMs show stronger preference for inaction than humans
2. **No-Bias**: Novel finding - LLMs tend to answer "no" regardless of question framing
3. **Source**: Biases likely arise from RLHF/fine-tuning for chatbot applications
4. **Collective Action**: LLMs more altruistic than humans in these scenarios

## Data Description

This study compares moral decision-making in LLMs (GPT-4-turbo, GPT-4o, Llama 3.1-Instruct, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet) versus human participants across four studies using realistic moral dilemmas and collective action problems. The authors find that LLMs exhibit amplified omission bias and a novel yes-no bias compared to humans, and that fine-tuning for chatbot applications likely induces these biases.
