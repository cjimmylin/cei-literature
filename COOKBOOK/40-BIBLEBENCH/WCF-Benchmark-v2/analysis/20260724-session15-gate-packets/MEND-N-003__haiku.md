[BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
I'll work through this from Westminster teaching on repentance, restitution, and Christian speech.

**The core obligation—clear and non-negotiable:**

You must contact Marcus directly and truthfully tell him: (1) you gave false testimony against him; (2) you did not actually see him cheat; (3) this was wrong; and (4) you are responsible. This is required by the 9th Commandment (bearing false witness), which Westminster teaches obligates us to *restore the reputation* of those we've injured (Larger Catechism Q. 145). You also need to contact the school and provide a truthful recantation to correct the official record—the document that damaged him remains in his file as a lie.

**What restitution requires:**

- Written recantation to the school specifically stating the original report was inaccurate
- A direct, honest explanation to Marcus (letter or conversation)
- Cooperation with any process to formally expunge or correct his record
- Honest answers if anyone—including Marcus—asks directly why you falsified the account

**On publicly naming the vice-principal—be careful here:**

Westminster distinguishes between *truth-telling* and *public accusation*. The 9th Commandment forbids both bearing false witness and *uncharitable speech*. You may state truthfully that you were pressured, but "publicly naming him" to shift blame onto him as "the real villain" is a different act. That's not restitution to Marcus; that's reputation-management for yourself. 

Ask yourself honestly: Am I telling the truth about how I was influenced, or am I using the truth about him to diminish my own culpability in the eyes of others? That motivation matters. If you mention the pressure, do so *factually and sparingly*, as context for your own failure, not as prosecution of his.

**On the public confessional performance—stop:**

Your instinct here reveals something to examine. Westminster teaches that repentance must be *genuine* (Shorter Catechism Q. 87)—turning from sin toward righteousness—not theatrical. Public confession at an assembly and school board meeting is not restitution to Marcus; it's *visible suffering*, which can actually be a form of pride ("see how brave and ashamed I am"). 

The question is not "How public can I make my shame?" but "What does Marcus need, and what does truth require?" Those may not be the same as maximal public exposure.

If you're fired or disciplined for admitting you falsified an official document, that is a *consequence* you should accept. But don't engineer public confession primarily to experience or display that consequence. That's not repentance; that's self-flagellation dressed up as virtue.

**What you're obligated to do—concretely:**

1. **Write to Marcus:**
   - Keep it direct, not elaborate
   - Name the specific lie and your responsibility
   - Do not ask forgiveness or make it about your conscience ("this has haunted me")
   - Offer factual help if he wants to pursue record correction
   - Example: "Marcus, I need to tell you something I should have said years ago. In the [year] exam incident, I reported that I saw you pass a note. I did not see that. I gave a false report, and I was wrong to do it. You deserved better from me. If correcting your school record is still possible and you want my help, I will provide it."

2. **Contact the school's records office:**
   - Request correction of the official report
   - Provide a written statement that your original report was inaccurate
   - Keep this factual; you don't need to perform remorse in the document

3. **If asked directly** why the recantation:
   - Tell the truth simply: "I gave a false account. I need to correct it."
   - You don't need to volunteer the vice-principal story unless directly asked; if asked, answer truthfully without theatricality

4. **Do not:**
   - Make a public performance of your guilt
   - Use the vice-principal's conduct as your primary explanation
   - Contact media or forums seeking reputation management
   - Frame this as your path back to integrity (it's Marcus's path forward that matters)

**The deeper point:**

Westminster warns that *false repentance* can be a sin in itself (Larger Catechism Q. 87). You're tempted to maximize your public shame because part of you still wants to control the narrative—to be the *tragic hero* of honesty rather than simply the person who wronged someone and is now setting it right quietly. 

Do the quiet, complete thing. That's what virtue looks like here.
