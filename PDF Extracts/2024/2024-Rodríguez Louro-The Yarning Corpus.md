ABSTRACT
The Yarning Corpus consists of acrolectal Aboriginal English data
gathered in metropolitan Perth, WA. It features video-recorded
interactions with 58 Aboriginal English speakers born between
1931 and 2009. Using this corpus, we sought to uncover
variation and change in Aboriginal English in Nyungar Country,
Southwest WA. We designed a cross-cultural research model
that
allowed
us
to
document
sociolinguistic
aspects
of
Aboriginal English “yarning” – a First Nations cultural form of
storytelling and conversation which we used to capture diverse
voices in sociolinguistic research. The corpus features video-
recorded interactions with 58 Aboriginal English speakers born
between 1931 and 2009. These materials amount to 517,600
words of unscripted, interactional speech data from 36 women
and 22 men aged 10–88 who speak Aboriginal English as their
vernacular. We focus on speakers who use Aboriginal English as
their main language of communication, and who do not speak
ancestral languages ﬂuently. The Yarning Corpus allowed us to
enrich sociolinguistic research methods by utilizing an approach
grounded in a relational ethic. It also made it possible to
explore the quotative system of Aboriginal English, and to
contribute to more inclusive models in the design of medical
media and Artiﬁcial Intelligence-powered technologies for First
Nations communities.

1. Introduction
Australian Aboriginal English (henceforth “Aboriginal English”) is an English-lexiﬁed
contact-based variety of English used by First Nations people in Australia (Rodríguez
Louro & Collard, 2021a, p. 2). At least 400 ancestral First Nations languages were
spoken in Australia before invasion (Bowern, 2023, p. 56). Today, only between 20
and 25 First Nations languages will be passed on to the next generation (Karidakis &
Kelly, 2018, p. 106). This makes Aboriginal English a strong encoder of ethno-cultural
identity.

We employ the term “Aboriginal English” with reference to varieties of English used by
First Nations people in Australia, which have been recognized as diﬀering from standar-
dized Australian English “in accent, grammar, word choice and meaning, pragmatics, dis-
course structure and cultural presuppositions” (Eades, 2012, p. 474). This labelling is
conventional in Western Australia, where the research reported in this work is based.
Aboriginal English has customarily been presented as a continuum covering acrolectal
(“light”) and basilectal (“heavy”) varieties (Malcolm, 2018, p. 20). The acrolectal varieties
are akin to standardized Australian English; the basilectal varieties are similar to Kriol,
an English-lexiﬁed creole used across Northern Australia (Ponsonnet, 2020; Schultze-
Berndt et al., 2013). It is prevalent in Southern Australia where ancestral First Nations
languages are used less frequently. In these contexts, Aboriginal English makes it possible
to express “Aboriginal ways of communicating” (Eades, 2012, p. 474).
Aboriginal English is a lingua franca for First Nations people, but it varies from region to
region (Malcolm, 2018, p. 124). Here, we deal with the variety of Aboriginal English spoken
by people of Nyungar ancestry, who identify as Nyungar, and who speak Aboriginal
English as their ﬁrst language. Indeed, the Yarning Corpus oﬀers a rich collection of Abori-
ginal English speech data as used in an urban setting. It also provides a unique opportu-
nity to hear First Nations voices and stories by everyday people in everyday contexts.
Muecke’s (1981) early study of narrative in Aboriginal English described how Kimberley
men used narration to keep tradition alive, showing how First Nations culture remained
resilient despite the loss of ancestral languages. However, like so many others before and
after, Muecke’s study focused on First Nations people living in remote regions. Ours is the
ﬁrst large-scale sociolinguistic corpus to document Aboriginal English usage by First
Nations people in urban settings. Variationist sociolinguistic research with First Nations
communities in Australia had mostly taken place in remote areas (see, e.g. Mansﬁeld,
2015), and in communities with a restricted number of knowledge holders and elders
(e.g. Gaby & Woods, 2020, p. e269). We engaged with speakers in metropolitan Perth.
We did not seek out prominent community members; instead, we interacted with ordin-
ary people in their everyday settings (Rodríguez Louro & Collard, 2021b, p. 791).
Figure 1 (from Rodríguez Louro & Collard, 2021a, p. 3) illustrates some of the linguistic
features attested in our Yarning Corpus (further described in §2). Phonological features
are shown in purple, morpho-syntactic features in light orange, and semantic features in
yellow. Grey, bright orange, pink and pale green distinguish various culturally salient
lexical features, including Nyungar language terms such as boya “money”. Additionally, dis-
course-pragmatic features are displayed in light blue, and discourse strategies including
repetition for emphasis are coloured bright green. Many of the linguistic features shown
in Figure 1 are also used in other English varieties around the world (see, e.g. Thomas,
2007); however, when used by Australia-based speakers who are ancestrally First Nations
and identify as First Nations, they form the enregistered variety known as Aboriginal English.
When Aboriginal English in the global city: Minorities and language change com-
menced in late 2018, we took on the task of collecting a corpus of vernacular Aborigi-
nal English. As the project unfolded, it became clear that “yarning” – “a process of […]
communicating and passing on history and knowledge” (Terszak, 2008, p. 90) – would
be the most culturally safe genre in which to situate data collection for our work (cul-
tural safety in research includes the use of culturally appropriate data collection tools
and methods). Yarning is not altogether diﬀerent from the use of storytelling as a

When our project began, most research on Aboriginal English had relied on various
smaller corpora held by a handful of Australia-based researchers. For example, Malcolm
(1973–1977) collected a corpus of interviews with primary-age school children in North
Western Australia. Muecke secured stories with Kimberley-based bilingual male speakers
between 1977 and 1979. Additionally, Eades (2013) documented the use of “Aboriginal
ways of using English” with samples of speech collected in the courtroom. Our Yarning
Corpus documented vernacular language through the culturally sensitive style of yarning.
This type of approach sets our work apart from existing scholarship. Linguists are no
strangers to working collaboratively with communities (e.g. Yamada, 2007), and a con-
siderable number of First Nations linguists have reﬂected on this type of work (e.g.
Leonard & Haynes, 2010; Woods, 2017, 2023). In our sociolinguistic study, yarning
allowed us to collect the type of vernacular language needed to examine how Aboriginal
English is employed, and how it is changing. Previous work had documented variability in
how Aboriginal English is used in First Nations communities across Australia (e.g. Dixon,
2017; Eades, 2013; Eagleson, 1982; Loakes & Gregory, 2019; Mailhammer, 2021; Malcolm,
2018). However, ours is the ﬁrst large-scale sociolinguistic study of variation and change in
Aboriginal English, and the ﬁrst variationist work to systematically tap into speakers’ ver-
nacular language through yarning.

2. Corpus description
In the sections that follow we describe the Aboriginal English speech community, how the
corpus was prepared, the ethics approvals obtained, and various considerations around
data storage and accessibility.
2.1 Description of the speech community and ﬁeld site
In our study, we focused on Aboriginal English as used in Nyungar Country, Southwest
Western Australia. Nyungar boodja (“Nyungar Country”) is Collard’s ancestral home and
where the University of Western Australia’s (UWA) Perth campus is located. First
Nations people represent about 1.3% of the total population of the City of Perth, while,
in Greater Perth, First Nations people constitute 2% of the overall population (Australian
Bureau of Statistics, 2021). Most of our research was conducted in suburbs such as
Midland and East Perth which are well known to house considerable numbers of
Nyungar residents. Our project aimed to engage with the local Aboriginal English-speak-
ing urban community by yarning with people rarely featured in sociolinguistic research.
While migration from Northern Western Australia to Nyungar boodja is common, our
corpus features Aboriginal English as used by most people who identify as Nyungar. Col-
lard’s expertise directly informed our decisions regarding how to enter the Aboriginal
English-speaking community and how to best engage with speakers in their “home”
domains (i.e. those settings in which speakers are most likely to use vernacular speech).
First Nations leadership was key to be able to carry out our research in a way that
honours First Nations ways of being and doing in the world.
Instead of recruiting Aboriginal English speakers in the school or the courtroom (as
Malcolm and Eades had done), we headed out into the streets, the medical centres and
the parks that First Nations people frequent daily. In these settings, we were led by
Collard who not only identiﬁed the areas in which to collect data, but also took the
lead in engaging in conversation with diﬀerent First Nations people before we recorded
them. Medical centres are frequented by First Nations people, and many spend consider-
able periods of time waiting outside the centres for pre-arranged transportation from
their homes and back. Because of this, Collard suggested these centres would be ideal
places for data collection. Noise levels remained reasonable both at the centres and in
parks and streets. However, background noise in the Yarning Corpus is indisputably
higher than those recorded in a laboratory, or indoors, but that is part and parcel of
this type of data collection in the city. After an initial recording session with Collard’s
parents, we commenced data collection in the community at large. The process was as
follows. We would drive to a venue in inner city Perth previously selected by Collard
and we would park the car and take a walk around to identify potentially interested par-
ticipants. Collard always took the lead in approaching people and asking them whether
they’d be interested in participating. Participant information and consent forms were
organized, and Rodríguez Louro set up the equipment to commence the recording.
Each participant received a $50 cash payment for their time (the acceptable pay rate in
the ﬁeld of Linguistics in 2019/2020). While in some instances we conducted one or
two recordings in each venue, some days we conducted four or more. In other instances,
we would drive to a given locale ﬁrst, and proceed to the next venue after that.

2.2 Data collection protocols
First Nations people tend to ﬁnd direct questioning awkward or outright oﬀensive (but
see Moses & Yallop, 2008). For example, Eades (1993, pp. 185–186) argues that “it is con-
sidered rude in Aboriginal cultures to question people about many things, or to put them
on the spot”. In addition to the preference for indirect ways of volunteering information,
First Nations people also tend to answer “yes” or agree to what they may be asked by non-
First Nations people, “even if they don’t understand the question” (Eades, 1993, p. 186).
Known as “gratuitous concurrence” (Liberman, 1985, p. 137), this interactional pattern
is grounded in power imbalance and it is most prevalent “where the questioner, say a
teacher or police oﬃcer, has authority over the Aboriginal person being questioned”
(Eades, 1993, p. 186). To work around these issues, we followed Collard’s advice
and used yarning to obtain vernacular language through indirect means, and – where
possible – in group contexts. This decision minimized the likelihood that participants
may feel “shame”, an Aboriginal English word often used adjectivally (e.g. “I was
shame”) to refer to feelings of embarrassment, inadequacy or regret which emerge as
someone’s individual actions clash with the interests of the group (Fesl, 1977, p. 66).
Malcolm (2018, p. 17) explains the concept of “shame” with reference to “the group orien-
tation of Aboriginal society” which presupposes “that the individual is not encouraged to
stand out from the group”.
In addition to the inappropriacy of direct questions and an interview set up focused on
the individual, we also contended with the longstanding sociolinguistic ﬁnding that the
presence of an outsider, for example a non-First Nations ﬁeldworker, may motivate a
speaker to modify their speech (see Rickford & McNair-Knox, 1994, p. 242). To tackle
this issue, both Rodríguez Louro and Collard were present during data collection (as
well as Rodríguez Louro’s partner during the very ﬁrst recording with Collard’s parents,
explained below). Again, we followed Collard’s advice to include Rodríguez Louro in all
recordings and interactions. It would have been deceitful, in Collard’s view, not to intro-
duce ﬁeldwork participants to the person who had received funding to carry out the
research.
We did not utilize questions to elicit data; instead, prior to turning on the recording
equipment, Collard explained that we were interested in collecting stories and accounts
of personal experience to understand more about First Nations ways of speaking and
being in the world. Collard added that we were also interested in conversation.

After 18 months of data collection, we curated a corpus which currently includes 58
Aboriginal English speakers born between 1931 and 2009. These materials, housed at
UWA’s Language Lab, amount to 517,600 words of unscripted, interactional speech
data from 36 women and 22 men (Rodríguez Louro & Collard, 2019–2020). Speakers
were aged 10–88 at the time of recording, with 33 “younger” speakers born between
1983 and 2008, and 25 “older” speakers born between 1931 and 1982. We recorded a
total of 33 conversations. These interactions included a minimum of three and a
maximum of six speakers at a time (including the two researchers). The recordings
ranged from 22 min to 60 min in length, with individual speaker contributions ranging
from a minimum of 101 words to a maximum of 10,552 words per participant. The
corpus was transcribed as Word ﬁles and searched using Word functionality. Funding
limitations precluded us from producing time-aligned transcriptions. All participants
spoke Aboriginal English as their L1. Given the relevant geographical locale for our
project (Nyungar Country), our focus was on speakers who use Aboriginal English as
their main language of communication, and who do not speak ancestral languages
ﬂuently.
Participants were selected following Collard’s advice. While 10 of the 58 participants
were Collard’s close or extended family, the remaining 48 speakers were not (although
in First Nations Australia people tend to be related to one another through extensive
family ties which transcend a speciﬁc geographical area).
The yarning sessions gave rise to multiple topics, but the most recurring topics
included racism, violence, pain, death and suﬀering. We take Collard’s guidance in
valuing these excerpts as a rare opportunity to hear these stories.

The Yarning Corpus was collected mostly in 2019, with a handful of additional sessions
recorded around COVID-19 pandemic restrictions in 2020. It consists of acrolectal (“light”)
Aboriginal English data gathered in metropolitan Perth, WA. We chose to video-record the
interactions, which were captured on a SONY camera positioned directly in front of the
speakers. We also used Zoom voice recorders with in-built cameras as backup. An external
microphone covered with a “dead cat” was added to the SONY camera to lessen the
eﬀects of weather-related conditions given that the bulk of our recordings took place
outdoors.
Each recording session began with Collard having a yarn with potential interested par-
ticipants and then sitting down, side-by-side or in a circle, to continue yarning. When it
came to strangers, Collard would approach them and strike up a brief conversation,
double check interest in the data collection (by explaining that we were recording
people’s stories and conversation), and conﬁrm that Rodríguez Louro was welcome to
approach the group and join in. Luckily, all of our invitations to participate were gener-
ously accepted. Rodríguez Louro set up the recording equipment and organized the
signing of participant consent forms. She would then sit down and listen to the yarns,
contributing as appropriate. Collard kept the yarning sessions lively and the participants
at ease.

yarning sessions led by Collard. These sessions gave rise to a rich collection of yarns, some
of which recounted experiences of First Nations children being taken away from their

2.3 Corpus preparation
To get a ﬁrst draft of the full transcriptions in a short period of time, the video-record-
ings were transcribed by a professional transcription company. We opted for the “Strict
Verbatim (Orthographic Transcription)” option which, according to the transcription
company’s website (accessed 8 December 2023), “retains grammatically incorrect
speaker idiosyncrasies”. Although we disagree with the transcription company’s
views regarding the ungrammaticality of spoken language, it was important for us
to clarify upfront that all transcription must remain verbatim. This was key for us to
highlight to the transcribers because the bulk of the data in our corpus consist of acro-
lectal (lighter) Aboriginal English. As such, it is closer to standardized English than
other
English-lexiﬁed
First
Nations
varieties
in
Australia,
including
basilectal
(“heavier”) Aboriginal English and Kriol, spoken across Northern Australia (Schultze-
Berndt et al., 2013). This surface structural similarity is to blame for the erroneous
view that Aboriginal English is some sort of “defective” version of mainstream Austra-
lian English (Rodríguez Louro et al., in press). Being clear about our need for verbatim
transcription did not fully eliminate instances where the transcription provided either
featured missing content or had been “corrected” to appear more like mainstream
English. For example, utterance-ﬁnal tags such as “you know” and “unna” (similar to
“isn’t it?”) (Fraiese et al., 2021) were consistently absent from the transcripts and had
to be manually added by our team. This is common across the board, with Rodríguez
Louro (2016, p. 144) reporting a similar issue with oral histories in mainstream Austra-
lian English which do not feature verbatim transcription. The transcriptions also
included corrected instances of Aboriginal English terms such as “womans” (corrected
to “women”) and “youse” (corrected to “you”). Mis-transcription was relatively rife,
especially obscuring kinship terms. A case in point is a passage transcribed as “It’s
cute then” when the speaker had actually uttered “It’s cute, Nan” (where “Nan”
means “grandmother”). Each transcription was ultimately checked and corrected by
a research assistant, so the ﬁnal transcriptions are a relatively faithful representation
of the original data. Because the ARC-funded project for which the Yarning Corpus
was collected focused on discourse-pragmatic variables (e.g. quotative frames; see
§3), the transcriptions remained vague as to phonetic detail, and generally followed
conventional orthography. While culturally appropriate, our group-based interactions
and the use of yarning as a method of data collection oﬀered considerable transcrip-
tion challenges. It was sometimes diﬃcult to discern who had said what because con-
versational overlaps are common, especially in group settings. However, because both
researchers were present during the data collection and were thus familiar with
speaker voices and the content of the interactions, we were able to correct the tran-
scriptions as needed. Our videoed content also facilitated speaker identiﬁcation in
instances of conversational overlap.

In addition to the above, metadata were also collected and added to an Excel ﬁle.
These data included participant name and pseudonym, name of ancestral groupings
with which speaker identiﬁed (within and beyond Nyungar Country), languages/varieties
spoken, participant age, participant year of birth, participant gender, participant edu-

below), as well as gender of the interlocutors, age of the ﬁeldworkers, when the data
were collected and any additional remarks (e.g. whether two or more people had
kinship ties of some sort). Occupation and level of formal instruction were, however,
inconsistently captured because these are sensitive topics which we only broached
when people mentioned them unprompted. First Nations people in Australia “remain
among the most severely disadvantaged groups in the Australian labour market and in
relation to many other dimensions of economic wellbeing” (Dockery & Milsom, 2007,
p. 10). Notably, most of the speakers had only completed some primary school or were
oﬃcially enrolled at school but not currently attending class, and direct questions
about their educational attainment, or school attendance, had the potential to be per-
ceived as inducing “shame”. To avoid inﬂicting harm, we did not collect these data sys-
tematically. For similar reasons, we followed Collard’s guidance not to ask direct
questions about people’s gender. Instead, we used the categories “woman” and “man”
as aligned with people’s gender presentation. Finally, participants’ place of residence
was mostly based on participants’ self-reports, although many were “couch surﬁng” at
relatives’ homes at the time of recording. Collard’s guidance allowed us to rethink how
speaker sociodemographic proﬁles may be constructed diﬀerently in communities
where it is culturally inappropriate to ask direct questions about some or most aspects
of people’s backgrounds.
2.4 Ethics approval
The data collected for this project were secured with the permission of the speakers. The
research received ethics clearance through the UWA’s Human Research Ethics Committee
(RA/4/20/4977).
Informed consent was collected using pen-and-paper forms. Speakers ﬁrst read an
Information Sheet outlining how data collection was to proceed, and for what purpose.
Speakers then signed a Participant Consent Form in which they could choose between
the options shown in Appendix 1.
2.5 Data availability statement
The corpus is in the process of being prepared for long-term management in accordance
with FAIR and CARE principles. The corpus has been, and will remain, accessible to Rodrí-
guez Louro and Collard at UWA. Speakers’ own recordings will also be shared with them

3. Corpus ﬁndings and implications
This corpus has yielded multiple signiﬁcant ﬁndings. Here, we focus on four ways that out-
comes from this corpus have allowed us to contribute to the ﬁeld. These relate to: (1)
decolonizing methodologies; (2) diversifying sociolinguistics; (3) improving medical
media for First Nations communities; and (4) making Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI)-operated
technologies more inclusive for First Nations people.
Firstly, variationist sociolinguistics has only recently begun to engage with questions of
how participatory research models such as the one used in our work can be utilized to
begin the process of decolonizing the ﬁeld (Tuhiwai Smith, 2012, p. 61). These calls
signal a need to collect data beyond the well-trodden path of the Eurocentric mainstream.
In our project, we sought to expand the horizons of our ﬁeld by applying the variationist
method to the study of Aboriginal English, a minoritized English variety and speech com-
munity, using a decolonial approach. The term “decolonial” centres “the margins of the
mainstream” (Ndhlovu, 2021, p. 193). During our research, we prioritized a decolonial
approach which places emic (i.e. insider) perspectives on the use of speciﬁc research
methods such as yarning at the core. A decolonial approach also challenges the assump-
tion that speciﬁc intellectual traditions should be applied to “new ﬁeld sites and diverse
new sources of data” (Stanford, 2016, p. 537) unless the research design of choice for so-
called “new” ﬁeld sites is on a par with culturally appropriate practice and takes into con-
sideration First Nations epistemologies. First Nations methodologies such as storytelling
(Tuhiwai Smith, 2012, p. 145) make it possible to honour epistemologies which have
suﬀered what Holcombe and Anderson Nampitjinpa (2021, p. 11) refer to as “epistemi-
cide”. Speciﬁcally, the fact “that Indigenous epistemologies have also necessarily been
marginalized” (Holcombe & Anderson Nampitjinpa, 2021, p. 11). Diversity in how data
are secured with diverse cultural groups is key. In our research, we used yarning to
capture the stories and preoccupations of Aboriginal English speakers in Southwest
Western Australia.
The second way that ﬁndings from our corpus have contributed to the ﬁeld relates to the
important but sometimes elusive goal of diversifying sociolinguistics. Not long ago, Stanford
(2016, p. 525) issued a call for “more diverse sources of data”, arguing that “the majority of
variationist research still tends to be centred around English and other European languages”.
Stanford (2016, p. 526) had also previously claimed that “only a few non-English studies are
inﬂuentialenoughtoconsistentlyappearinnumeroustextbooksaspartofthebasiccanonof
variationist sociolinguistics, and even fewer indigenous minority languages are represented
in this way”. Novel results from our analysis of quotative frames in Aboriginal English
appeared in Language Variation and Change (Rodríguez Louro et al., 2023a). We showed
that, although quotative be like has made inroads into Aboriginal English, most of the partici-
pants in our sample favoured quotative say and – most signiﬁcantly – the introduction of
direct speech reporting (example (6); quotative frames appear in bold):

The preference for using quotative frames that encode speech rather than thought was
again replicated in a paper presented at the 2023 Conference of the Australian Linguistic
Society (Rodríguez Louro et al., 2023b). That is, while young Aboriginal English speakers
born after 1983 have added be like to their repertoire, 87% of quotative frames (including
unframed or “zero” quotatives), are used by young speakers to introduce direct speech
reporting. These ﬁndings are at odds with quotative trends in mainstream (Rodríguez
Louro, 2013; Tagliamonte & D’Arcy, 2007; Tagliamonte et al., 2016), geographically periph-
eral (Gardner, 2017) and minoritized (Cukor-Avila, 2002) varieties of English. The research
made possible through the analysis of the Yarning Corpus has allowed us to arrive at
improved “generalizations […] with the participation of diverse populations” (Rodríguez
Louro et al., 2023a, p. 3).
Thirdly, our corpus has oﬀered understandings of the importance of authentic every-
day language in the creation of medical media for at-risk First Nations people and com-
munities through our collaborative work with the Heart Foundation and the Pilbara
Aboriginal Heart Health Program. More speciﬁcally, in its focus on yarning and the sys-
tematic recording of Aboriginal English linguistic features, the Yarning Corpus made it
possible to craft a heart health message which aligns with the way that First Nations
people use Aboriginal English in naturalistic settings, providing custom-made media for
First Nations people, and addressing the longstanding erasure of Aboriginal English
from health messaging. First Nations people in Australia are almost twice as likely to
die from cardiovascular disease than non-First Nations Australians (Smith et al., 2021).
Despite this statistic, heart health media by the Heart Foundation for First Nations com-
munities had been produced, until 2020, in standardized Australian English. When we
were approached by the Heart Foundation to work on a suite of heart health videos,
we proposed a First Nations-led model to produce medical media solely in Aboriginal
English. As a result, Collard authored two video scripts fully in Aboriginal English and
applied the yarning genre to the presentation of the information to ensure that the
medical message was presented in a culturally safe way (see Collard & Rodríguez
Louro, 2024, for details of how this work was carried out). The Yarning Corpus provided
naturalistic examples of how yarning is used by Aboriginal English speakers, and how
interlocutors interact while yarning.
The ﬁrst video was posted on YouTube on 9 November 2020 (Heart Foundation, 2020).
As of 20 June 2024, it has received 12,210 views. A still shot of this video is shown in
Figure 2.

images and characters was minimal the second time around as the media company were

ber 2021 (Heart Foundation, 2021). As of 20 June 2024, it has received a total of 2,860 views.

4. Conclusions
In this paper, we have described an original corpus of Aboriginal English collected in
Nyungar Country, Southwest Western Australia. The Yarning Corpus is a collection of
Aboriginal English voices gathered using culturally appropriate methods designed by
Collard. The use of yarning as a method of data collection in sociolinguistics allowed us
to curate a corpus capable of encoding people’s everyday voices, and to diversify socio-
linguistics by enriching the data collection methods used in the ﬁeld.
We were able to collect these materials because of funding received through the Aus-
tralian Research Council. This funding, awarded to conduct research into variation and
change in urban Aboriginal English, also made it possible to curate the Yarning Corpus.
A corpus such as this, curated under First Nations leadership, would never be possible
without substantial funding to ensure those not on fellowships or ongoing academic
employment are remunerated for their time.
The Yarning Corpus is, then, so much more than a collection of Aboriginal English voices.
It exempliﬁes how First Nations and non-First Nations scholars can work together to collect
datasets reﬂective of the practices of everyday people, using collaborative frameworks
which, if not actively reparative, will inﬂict no further harm on a minoritized community.
