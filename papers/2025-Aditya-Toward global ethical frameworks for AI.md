# Toward global ethical frameworks for AI: Aligning Artificial Intelligence with human values and progress

## Authors

[[Shivam Aditya]]

## Links



- **Journal:** https://journalwjaets.com/

## Abstract

This article addresses the challenge of establishing ethical governance for AI across social, economic, and political domains. It highlights **virtue ethics** as a critical theoretical framework for **aligning** artificial intelligence with human values and progress. The paper explores the multifaceted nature of AI alignment, including the technical challenges of reward modeling and the sociocultural dimensions of **value pluralism**. It advocates for embedding human-aligned **virtues** into AI systems to ensure they contribute to **human welfare** and **flourishing** in an increasingly automated world.

## Detailed Summary

This article examines the challenge of establishing global ethical governance frameworks for artificial intelligence systems as they increasingly influence decision-making across social, economic, and political domains. The paper analyzes AI alignment from both technical dimensions (explainability challenges, value alignment algorithms, safety mechanisms) and sociocultural dimensions (cultural variations in ethical priorities and value pluralism). The author proposes that effective AI governance must integrate technical standards with substantive ethical principles and inclusive deliberative processes.

The article provides a comprehensive literature review of AI ethics theoretical foundations, tracing the evolution from Norbert Wiener's cybernetics ethics through computer ethics, information ethics, to contemporary AI-specific frameworks. It examines how consequentialist, deontological, virtue ethics, and care ethics approaches each offer complementary insights for AI governance. The current regulatory landscape is analyzed through comparative examination of the EU AI Act (risk-based, precautionary), US sectoral approaches (innovation-friendly), Chinese centralized oversight (security-focused), and Global South capacity-building efforts.

A key contribution is the proposed three-tiered governance framework consisting of: (1) a universal oversight structure with local, national, and international coordination similar to climate governance; (2) transparency and accountability mechanisms with risk-calibrated explainability requirements; and (3) an ethical AI certification program with differentiated standards across sectors. The paper evaluates future implications across economic dimensions (labor market transformation, business model adaptation), security dimensions (dual-use concerns, international cooperation), and social justice dimensions (bias mitigation, distributional justice, accessibility).

## Methods Summary

The paper employs a conceptual analysis and policy synthesis methodology, drawing on existing literature in AI ethics, governance studies, and comparative policy analysis. The author reviews theoretical foundations from multiple ethical traditions and examines current regulatory approaches across jurisdictions (EU, US, China, Global South). The governance framework is developed through synthesis of bioethics governance parallels, stakeholder analysis, and consideration of implementation challenges including regulatory arbitrage, power imbalances, and the "pacing problem" of technology outpacing regulation.

## Results Summary

The analysis identifies several critical gaps in current AI governance: 67% of high-stakes AI applications lack comprehensive ethical review processes; regulatory fragmentation creates arbitrage opportunities; a "responsibility gap" leaves accountability unclear between developers, deployers, and users. The comparative analysis reveals significant variation in national approaches, from EU's precautionary pre-market testing to US post-deployment monitoring. The proposed certification framework distinguishes between high-risk applications (requiring comprehensive causal explanations), medium-risk (counterfactual explanations), and low-risk (transparency reports). Cost-benefit analysis suggests ethical AI implementation offers positive long-term returns through reduced liability, improved trust, and enhanced talent recruitment.

## Conclusions

The paper concludes that AI governance cannot be reduced to technical standards alone - fundamental challenges stem from deeper questions about human values, societal priorities, and power distributions. Key recommendations include: developing risk-tiered regulatory frameworks for policymakers; implementing ethics-by-design approaches for developers with diverse teams and comprehensive documentation; expanding interdisciplinary research for academia; and engaging in governance discussions for civil society. The author emphasizes the need for adaptive regulatory mechanisms that evolve alongside technological capabilities while balancing innovation with precaution.
