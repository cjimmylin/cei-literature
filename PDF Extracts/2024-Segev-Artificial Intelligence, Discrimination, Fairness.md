Abstract
Should the input data of artificial intelligence (AI) systems include factors such as 
race or sex when these factors may be indicative of morally significant facts? More 
importantly, is it wrong to rely on the output of AI tools whose input includes fac­
tors such as race or sex? And is it wrong to rely on the output of AI systems when it 
is correlated with factors such as race or sex (whether or not its input includes such 
factors)? The answers to these questions are controversial. In this paper, I argue for 
the following claims. First, since factors such as race or sex are not morally sig­
nificant in themselves, including such factors in the input data, or relying on output 
that includes such factors or is correlated with them, is neither objectionable (for 
example, unfair) nor commendable in itself. Second, sometimes (but not always) 
there are derivative reasons against such actions due to the relationship between 
factors such as race or sex and facts that are morally significant (ultimately) in 
themselves. Finally, even if there are such derivative reasons, they are not neces­
sarily decisive since there are sometimes also countervailing reasons. Accordingly, 
the moral status of the above actions is contingent.

1  Introduction
The extensive, and expanding, reliance on artificial intelligence (AI) systems as a 
basis for practical decisions1 triggers both optimism and concern, inter alia, regarding 
the implications of such systems in terms of discrimination, unfairness, and related 
moral concerns. While some believe that actions that rely on AI tools may mitigate 
objectionable discrimination or unfairness, for example, since they are less suscep­
tible to irrationality, prejudice, contempt, and hate,2 others fear that reliance on such 
tools would intensify discrimination or unfairness.3 Of course, both the optimists 
and the pessimists may be right if their claims are suitably qualified: AI systems may 
have both positive and negative effects in terms of discrimination, fairness, and other 
moral concerns. For example, AI tools may reduce certain forms of discrimination 
or unfairness, while exacerbating others.4 The questions are therefore twofold. First, 
how the use of AI systems may affect discrimination, fairness, and other moral con­
cerns, for better or worse? Second, what are the implications of the answer to this 
question regarding how AI systems should be used?
More specifically, there are two sets of questions regarding the relationship 
between AI systems and discrimination. First, should the input data of AI systems 
include factors such as race and sex when they may be indicative of morally signifi­
cant facts? More importantly, is it wrong to rely on the output of AI tools whose input 
includes factors such as race or sex? Second, is it wrong to rely on the output of AI 
systems when it is correlated with factors such as race or sex (whether or not its input 
includes factors such as race or sex)? I will refer to these questions as the “input” and 
“output” questions respectively.5

The answers to the input and output questions are controversial. Purging factors 
such as race or sex from the input or output of AI systems may distort their results 
when these factors are correlated with morally significant facts. Accordingly, some 
claim that such factors should be included in the input data of AI systems and that the 
output of such systems should be relied upon even if it is correlated with such factors. 
However, it is often assumed that factors such as race or sex should not affect the 
allocation of benefits and burdens even if they are correlated with morally significant 
facts.6 Indeed, discrimination is often legally prohibited even when there is a reason 
to discriminate (such that not discriminating involves a morally significant cost). 
Accordingly, some insist that the input data of AI systems should not include factors 
such as race or sex, even when they are indicative of morally significant facts, and 
that an output that correlates with such factors should be discarded (or interfered with 
such that this is no longer the case).7
The input and output questions are important in themselves (given the extensive 
reliance on AI systems). In addition, they are related to other questions. One example 
is the question of whether there should be a right to a “human decision”.8 To answer 
this question we need to know what, if any, are the deficiencies involved in AI sys­
tems. Another example is the common claim that transparency regarding how AI 
systems operate is important.9 To determine which facts should be transparent, we 
need to know which facts are morally objectionable.10 These answers to the input and 
output questions are also relevant to the question of what should be the legal status of 
the relevant actions (although this does not depend only on these answers).11

or commendable in itself. Second, sometimes (but not always) there are derivative 
reasons against these actions due to the relationship between factors such as race or 
sex and facts that are morally significant (ultimately) in themselves. Finally, even if

2  What’s (Not) Unique?
It is often assumed that the AI context is unique in terms of concerns about discrimi­
nation and fairness.12 This assumption is usually not explicit,13 but it is implied by the 
extensive literature that focuses on fairness and discrimination in the AI context and 
explicates these concerns in unique ways.14 Specifically, fairness in the AI context is 
often associated with various statistical measures that evaluate the predictions of AI 
systems.15 However, since the AI context is clearly unique in some respects but not 
in others, we should consider not if but how the AI context is unique in the relevant 
respects. The answer to this question is important since it should determine the focus 
of the inquiry. When the AI context is not relevantly unique, considering the moral 
status of the relevant actions separately from that of other actions is not only unneces­
sary but may also misdirect the inquiry and ignore important lessons learned in other 
contexts.16 Conversely, when the AI context is relevantly unique, discussions in other 
contexts may be deceptive.
Given the prevalence of the assumption that the AI context is unique in terms of 
concerns about discrimination and fairness, it is especially important to highlight 
how the AI context does not seem to be relevantly unique. For example, the unique 
explication of fairness in the AI context may be partly due to the volume of data 
processed by AI systems17 and the fact that it is often possible to quantify the degree 
to which their predictions are accurate (including concerning factors such as race

3  The Input Question
Should the input data of AI systems include factors such as race and sex, and is it 
wrong to rely on the output of AI systems whose input includes such factors, when 
they are indicative of morally significant facts (such as medical conditions, qualifica­
tions, or recidivism)? The first step towards answering these questions is to consider 
whether the actions that they refer to are morally objectionable in themselves or only 
if and when other factors are present. It is sometimes assumed that these actions are 
morally objectionable in themselves, at least in the sense that there is a pro tanto 
reason against them.32 This assumption may also underlie the common legal doctrine 
of “direct discrimination” (or “disparate treatment”), which prohibits certain actions 
that are based on factors such as race or sex.33 Accordingly, it is not surprising that 
this assumption has practical implications. Indeed, some policy-makers refrain from 
taking actions that may bring about optimal states of affairs (for instance, by prevent­
ing suffering) because they believe that this assumption is correct.
Consider, for example, the deliberations of administrators who considered using 
an AI tool to address a major source of waste in healthcare institutions: “no-shows”, 
namely, patients who do not arrive, at all or on time, for their appointments without 
prior notification.34 Overbooking – scheduling more than one patient in the same time 
slot – is often the most effective way of addressing this problem, and one efficient 
way to determine which patients are most likely to miss their appointments, and 
accordingly to decide how to overbook, is based on the predictions of AI systems. 
The above administrators nevertheless decided against using such a system due to 
the concern that it would involve objectionable discrimination (inter alia) because 
the input of the relevant AI tool included personal characteristics such as ethnicity, 
financial class, religion, and body mass index.35
This response is controversial (and similar AI tools that help construct overbook­
ing systems are sometimes used by other institutions).36 However, it appears to be 
common and, as noted above, it may be motivated by the view that including factors 
such as race and sex in the input data of AI systems (or, more generally, considering 
such factors) is objectionable in itself. Still, in what follows, I argue that this view is 
misguided. More specifically, I argue that including factors such as race and sex in 
the input data of AI systems, and relying on an output that is the result of an input that

The main argument for this conclusion rests on the proposition that facts such 
as race and sex are not morally significant in themselves. While it is controversial 
which facts are important in themselves, it seems clear and widely accepted that 
facts such as race and sex are not of this type. This is apparent once we compare such 
facts to facts that are plausibly and often considered important in themselves. For 
example, when allocating scarce resources, the fact a resource would benefit one per­
son more than another is plausibly and commonly considered morally significant in 
itself (namely, as a pro tanto reason to give the resource to the former rather than the 
latter). Similarly, the fact that one person is worse off than another, or more virtuous 
than another, may be important in itself in this regard (as well as in other contexts). 
In contrast, facts such as the race or sex of the potential beneficiaries do not seem 
to be important in themselves in this regard – the fact that the relevant persons are 
black, white, men, or women, are not in themselves reasons for or against a certain 
allocation. In itself, the fact that the person who would get the relevant resource is 
black or white, for instance, is neither here nor there. Indeed, it seems that facts such 
as race or sex are never morally significant in themselves (and not only regarding 
allocative decisions).
This proposition entails, specifically, that including facts such as race or sex in the 
input data of AI systems, or relying on an input that includes such facts, is neither 
objectionable nor commendable in itself. This conclusion is compatible with the pos­
sibility that these actions are sometimes derivatively objectionable. However, this 
is the case only when additional facts, which are morally significant in themselves, 
apply. This may be the case, for instance, when considering facts such as race or sex 
(or including such facts in the input data of an AI system) results in treatment that 
is undeserved all things considered (since it does not properly reflect the balance of 
facts that are morally significant in themselves such as who would gain the greater 
benefit, who is worse off, or who is more praiseworthy). Accordingly, the ultimate 
objection, in this case, is the skewed allocation and not the fact that the input includes 
facts such as race or sex. In contrast, when considering factors such as race or sex 
leads to an allocation that properly reflects the balance of facts that are morally sig­
nificant in themselves, this objection to acting based on an input that includes factors 
such as race or sex does not apply. Moreover, in this case, there is a reason in favor 
of doing so.
Of course, since factors such as race or sex are not morally significant in them­
selves, they should not be considered or included in the input data of AI tools if the 
relevant agent knows all the facts that are morally significant in themselves. In this 
case, referring to factors such as race or sex is irrational (and sometimes also blame­
worthy). Moreover, the irrationality of the agent in such a case is likely to distort her 
action, for instance, when, as a result of the mistake, a resource is given to someone 
who should not get it (given the balance of the facts that are morally significant in 
themselves) or vice versa. However, even in this case, when the agent’s mistake dis­
torts her action, the distortion is due to the skewed allocation and not to the mistake 
itself. Indeed, as noted above, while the agent’s mistake is likely to distort her action, 
this is not necessarily the case. If, by coincidence, the resource is given to the person

It may be thought that the conclusion that acting based on input data that includes 
factors such as race or sex is not objectionable in itself is incompatible with the 
common view regarding the moral status of discrimination (namely, the assumption 
that it is objectionable in itself). However, salient normative accounts of discrimina­
tion in fact hold that discrimination – in the relevant sense of acting based on input 
data that includes factors such as race or sex – is objectionable only if it involves 
additional elements, for example, when a discriminatory action is harmful or disre­
spectful (in some sense).38 Thus, these theories do not consider discrimination (in the 
relevant sense) to be objectionable in itself and are accordingly in line with the above 
conclusion.39
As noted above, the proposition that acting based on an input that includes fac­
tors such as race or sex is not objectionable (or commendable) in itself is compatible 
with the existence of derivative reasons against doing so. But this proposition is also 
compatible with the existence of derivative reasons in favor of doing so. Specifically, 
if an agent does not know facts that are morally significant in themselves, and fac­
tors such as race or sex are the best evidence for such facts, there may be a deriva­
tive reason for the agent to consider such factors. For example, considering the race 
or sex of patients may be indicative of their medical condition. Accordingly, such 
consideration may enable providing them with the most effective treatment. In such 
a case there is accordingly a derivative reason to consider such factors and doing so 
does not seem objectionable in itself. In contrast, when factors such as race or sex are 
not (the best available) evidence for facts that are morally significant in themselves, 
not only is there no derivative reason to consider these factors but there is usually a 
derivative reason against doing so since this is likely to distort actions that are based 
on such consideration.
Thus, there are cases in which there are no reasons for or against including factors 
such as race or sex in the input data of AI tools, cases where there are only reasons 
for doing so, cases in which there are only reasons against doing so, and, finally, cases 
where there are both reasons for and reasons against doing so. Such conflicting rea­
sons may exist, for instance, when considering facts such as race or sex (or including 
these facts in the input data of AI systems) promotes well-being in one respect (for 
example, by overbooking appointments in a way that saves resources that are used to 
promote well-being) but also intensifies false stereotypes and thereby triggeres harm­

When there are such conflicting reasons, the overall conclusion depends on their 
relative weight.42 Accordingly, even when there are derivative reasons against includ­
ing factors such as race or sex in the input data of AI systems, these reasons are not 
necessarily decisive since they may be defeated by countervailing reasons. Thus, the 
answer to the question of whether doing so is wrong all things considered is contin­

sex is not objectionable (nor commendable) in itself.
It is frequently objected that including factors such as race and sex in the input 
data of AI tools violates the requirement to treat persons “as individuals”.43 How­
ever, while this is a common objection (also to other forms of discrimination such as 
“statistical discrimination”), it is often unclear. Facts such as race and sex are after 
all facts about individual persons. Since such facts are not morally significant in 
themselves, they should not be considered by agents who know all the facts that are 
morally significant in themselves. However, when agents do not know all these facts, 
and further inquiries involve a (morally significant) cost, there is a (derivative) reason 
to consider facts that are evidence for the (unknown) facts that are morally significant 
in themselves. Therefore, when facts such as race or sex are such evidence, there is 
a reason to consider them. And the above objection does not clarify why doing so is 
assumed to be objectionable (in itself).44
The objection that considering factors such as race and sex does not treat persons 
“as individuals” is often associated with the claim that people should be treated in a 
way that is sensitive to their choices (or as “autonomous” agents). Here too a literal 
interpretation of the objection is unhelpful. For instance, considering a person’s race 
or sex as evidence of the probability that they would behave in a certain way is com­
patible with the observation that individuals of the same race or sex may behave dif­
ferently from one another (provided the relevant probability is not 100%). The claim 
that people should be treated in a way that is sensitive to their choices is plausible 
only subject to important qualifications, and, once it incorporates these qualifications, 
it does not entail that it is objectionable in itself to consider factors such as race or 
sex (when they are evidence of facts that are morally significant in themselves).45 The

factors such as race or sex is not in itself even a pro tanto reason against acting

5  Conclusion
The extensive and expanding reliance on AI systems generates increasing attention 
to the ways in which such systems should (and should not) be constructed and used. 
Some of the relevant literature suggests standards that are (more or less) unique to 
this context, most notably the statistical “fairness” measures that are used as a basis 
for evaluating the outcomes of AI systems. However, I have argued that these mea­
sures do not identify what is important in itself. Moreover, the fact that an action 
violates these measures is not always even a reliable indication that something else 
is objectionable. In these respects, the focus on these measures may be distracting.
The answer to the question of when the use of AI systems is objectionable is 
unfortunately more complex. Yet one way of moving forward, in the right direction, 
is by relating the concerns regarding actions that rely on the output of AI systems to 
more general concerns, including concerns about fairness and discrimination but also 
other moral concerns – rather than isolating the inquiry regarding the moral status of 
AI systems from similar inquiries in other contexts and from more general normative 
inquiries. This would not yield a simple answer to the question of when actions that 
rely on AI systems are objectionable, but there is no reason to expect otherwise given 
that such an answer is not forthcoming also in other contexts.
