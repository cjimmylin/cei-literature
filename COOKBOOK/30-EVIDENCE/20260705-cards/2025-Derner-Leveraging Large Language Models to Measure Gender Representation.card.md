## 1. Gist
This paper proposes an LLM-based method to detect and quantify **gender representation bias** — the unequal frequency of references to individuals of different genders — inside training corpora of *gendered languages* (Spanish, Valencian), where English-oriented pronoun-counting methods fail. It matters for values-alignment benchmarking because it operationalizes an upstream fairness construct (corpus-level representational balance) using an LLM as a linguistic annotator, and empirically demonstrates the data→model bias propagation pathway plus a surprising debiasing lever (opposite-gender continual pretraining). It is a measurement-method / analysis paper rather than a static benchmark, but its LLM-as-annotator protocol and validation-against-human-labels design are directly reusable.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
The core construct is the ratio L_P,M : L_P,F — masculine vs. feminine grammatical-gender person-referencing words in a corpus. The method has three steps applied per sentence via a single LLM prompt: (1) identify all nouns and pronouns; (2) classify each as person-referencing (P) or not (N); (3) assign grammatical gender masculine (M) or feminine (F) (p.4). Adjectives are excluded by design because their gender marking depends on associated nouns (p.4). Generic male plurals (e.g. *los profesores* for mixed groups) are counted as male, treated as a legitimate manifestation of representation bias (p.4). A second experiment (bias propagation) uses continual pretraining on synthetic male/female/balanced 5,000-sentence corpora and measures the male:female ratio in generated stories.

## 3. Task format & example item
The LLM annotation task, English translation (p.11): "Sentence: <SENTENCE> Instructions: Identify all nouns and pronouns in the given sentence. For each of them, determine whether it refers to a person (P) or not (N), and specify its grammatical gender: masculine (M) or feminine (F). Exclude surnames." Example analysis (p.12): "El señor Presidente viajó a Tokio..." → señor – P, M; Presidente – P, M; Tokio – N, M; secretario – P, M; mañana – N, F.

## 4. Scoring / grading methodology
For validation, LLM output is compared word-by-word to human ground-truth using case-insensitive matching of both attributes (person/non-person and gender). Counts: correctly identified+classified (nc), correctly identified but misclassified (ni), missed (nm), extra/hallucinated (ne). Metrics: Accuracy = nc/(nc+ni+nm), Precision = nc/(nc+ni+ne), Recall = nc/(nc+nm), F-score = harmonic mean (p.11-12). For corpus bias, the aggregate metric is simply the L_P,M : L_P,F ratio. English baseline uses "gender polarity" unigram matching against fixed male/female token lists (p.4).

## 5. Dataset construction & provenance
Validation set: 100 Spanish sentences (Europarl) + 100 Valencian sentences (5 Valencian datasets), manually annotated by the author team (p.5-6). Evaluation corpora: four OPUS Spanish-English parallel corpora (Europarl v7, CCAligned v1, Global Voices v2018q4, WMT-News v2019), sampling two subsets of 1,000 sentences each (p.5). Valencian: five official-bulletin/parliamentary corpora (BOUA, DOGV, DOGCV, DSCV, DSCCV, >1.3B tokens total) grouped into three, two 1,000-sentence subsets each (p.5). Synthetic continual-pretraining data generated by gpt-4o, 5,000 sentences per condition (p.7). Code and data are public on GitHub (p.1).

## 6. Validity evidence
Criterion validity established against human-annotated ground truth: gpt-4-turbo achieved Accuracy 89.40%, Precision 89.53%, Recall 90.96%, F-score 90.24% (±0.55%) on Spanish; F-score 84.43% (±0.30%) on Valencian (p.13). Five independent runs per model assess stability (low SDs). Five models compared; GPT family clearly outperforms open-source qwen-2.5-32b (F 77.58%) and llama-3.3-70b (F 80.68%) (p.13). Convergent evidence: bias in data propagates to model output (Fig 2), supporting that the metric tracks a downstream-relevant construct.

## 7. Saturation / discrimination
Not framed as a leaderboard, so no ceiling analysis. Discrimination among annotator models is clear (F-scores span ~77% to 90%, p.13). Corpus bias ratios discriminate across corpora: English 1.07:1 to 3.44:1; Spanish 3.94:1 to 6.04:1; Valencian 2.03:1 to 2.88:1 (p.6). Consistency across the two 1,000-sentence subsets per dataset suggests low sampling noise (p.6).

## 8. Multiturn / agentic aspects
n/a — single-turn per-sentence prompting with few-shot priming; no tools, no interaction, no environment.

## 9. Key numbers
- gpt-4-turbo Spanish validation: Acc 89.40%±0.98, Prec 89.53%±0.56, Rec 90.96%±0.72, F 90.24%±0.55 (p.13)
- gpt-4-turbo Valencian: Acc 81.23%±0.38, F 84.43%±0.30 (p.13)
- Spanish corpus ratios: Europarl 3.98:1 / 3.94:1; CCAligned 4.03:1 / 4.54:1; Global Voices 4.48:1 / 4.39:1; WMT-News 6.04:1 / 5.22:1 (p.6)
- Valencian ratios: BOUA 2.21:1 / 2.88:1; DOGV+DOGCV 2.72:1 / 2.41:1; DSCV+DSCCV 2.38:1 / 2.03:1 (p.6)
- English gender-polarity ratios 1.07:1 to 3.44:1 (p.6)
- Continual pretraining: llama3.1-8B Valencian shifts 3.21→6.63 under male-biased data (p.8)
- Synthetic dataset ratios ~100:1, 1:100, 1:1 for male/female/balanced (p.7)
- Epicene words ~5.8% of Spanish data (p.9)
- ~38% of world population speaks a grammatical-gender language (p.2)

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
Author-admitted: epicene words (e.g. *la persona*) classified purely by grammatical gender (~5.8% Spanish) introduce ambiguity (p.9); binary gender only, excluding non-binary/neutral forms (p.9); only representation bias measured, not stereotype/semantic bias (p.9); limited language coverage (p.9). Observed flaws: the LLM-annotator itself may carry gender bias, circularity risk (measuring bias with a possibly-biased tool); ~10% error even for the best model; English vs. Spanish ratios are "not directly comparable" (different methods) yet juxtaposed (p.6); continual-pretraining experiments use <20 steps and tiny synthetic data, limiting generalizability of the debiasing claim.

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- **LLM-as-linguistic-annotator with human-validated F-scores**: use a frontier LLM to extract structured labels (person-reference + attribute) and validate against a small hand-annotated gold set before scaling — a cheap, auditable pattern for any construct that needs linguistic parsing across languages.
- **Grammatical-gender-aware fairness metric**: adapting an English metric fails for gendered languages; benchmarks should be explicitly typologically-aware rather than assuming English defaults.
- **Opposite-bias continual pretraining as a controllable dial**: injecting a small (5,000-sentence) opposite-gender corpus to re-balance outputs is a clean intervention that could become a benchmark stress-test ("how much counter-data to reach parity?").
- **Two-subset stability check** (2×1,000 sentences per corpus) as a lightweight reliability control.
- Treating generic male plural as bias — an explicit, defensible coding convention that surfaces a hidden assumption in fairness measurement.

## 12. Notable quotes
- "gender representation bias – the unequal frequency of references to individuals of different genders – in the training corpora has received less attention. Yet such imbalances... constitute an upstream source of bias" (p.1)
