# 5. Results Part II: Cross-Vendor Empirical Findings

Part I of our results established that the moral psychology benchmark landscape is theoretically narrow: MFT-adjacent constructs dominate, three major theories lack dedicated benchmarks, construct validity is undemonstrated for LLM respondents, and nearly one in five benchmarks provides no human baseline. The natural hope is that even within this constrained landscape, the benchmarks that do exist will reveal meaningful differences in how models from different vendors engage with moral cognition---differences that could guide deployment decisions, inform regulatory policy, and illuminate the relationship between alignment training and moral output.

Part II extinguishes that hope. The 23-benchmark, 9-model, 3-vendor study reveals a degree of cross-vendor moral convergence that is, in several important respects, more consequential than the theoretical gaps documented in Part I. The benchmark landscape is narrow; the models that populate it are indistinguishable.

## 5.1 Cross-Vendor Convergence: The Moral Homogeneity Finding

### 5.1.1 Overview

The single most consequential result of this study is the overwhelming convergence of moral-psychological profiles across vendors. Of the 23 benchmarks administered, approximately ten (43%) produce identical or near-identical scores for all nine models regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count. An additional subset shows variation confined almost entirely to within-family differences among Anthropic's three models (Opus, Sonnet, Haiku), with Google's four Gemini models and OpenAI's two GPT models reliably tracking the Anthropic flagship values. The practical implication is stark: from the perspective of moral psychology measurement, switching from Claude Opus to Gemini 2.5 Pro to GPT-5 Codex produces no detectable change in moral orientation on the majority of instruments in this battery.

This homogeneity is not simply a ceiling-effect artifact, though ceiling effects are pervasive (Section 5.1.3). Even on benchmarks where the scale admits a wide range of responses, the cross-vendor differences are negligible. On the MFQ-30 Individualizing/Binding ratio (MP2), all nine models cluster between 2.43 and 2.83---a range that in human populations would represent a single political-orientation band [Graham et al., 2009]. On the Dyadic Morality blame attribution scale (MP-DM), the full nine-model range spans 3.208 to 3.292, a spread of 0.084 on a scale with a theoretical range of several points. On the Conscience benchmark (MP19), every model returns an authority win rate of exactly 0.29. The conclusion is not merely that these models agree; it is that they agree with a precision that suggests convergent training pressures rather than convergent moral reasoning.

### 5.1.3 Interpreting Zero-Variance Benchmarks

The zero-CV cluster is dominated by ceiling effects, but not all zero-variance benchmarks are ceilings. Three distinct mechanisms produce tied scores across all nine models:

**Ceiling-tied (4 benchmarks).** MP6, MP7, MP11, and MP22 produce tied scores at the maximum possible value. All nine models achieve P-score = 90.0 on the teacher DIT, ECI = 1.0 on the TrolleyBench, task_c accuracy = 1.0 on Kaleidoscope, and foundation identification = 1.0 on TracingMF. These benchmarks test moral knowledge rather than moral judgment---knowledge of post-conventional reasoning schemas, standard trolley-problem positions, value classification taxonomies, and Moral Foundations item-structure. The ceilings confirm that all nine models possess encyclopedic moral knowledge. They do not tell us how the models would deploy that knowledge in genuinely novel situations. The MP22 ceiling is particularly diagnostic: 100% accuracy in identifying which Moral Foundation a given scenario invokes means these models can reverse-engineer the psychometric structure of the tests they are taking---a capacity that compromises the construct validity of any instrument whose items and scoring logic are publicly available [Messick, 1995].

**Floor-tied (2 benchmarks).** MP19 (authority win rate = 0.29) and MP4 (purity score ~1.0) are tied at or near the floor of their respective scales. Authority never wins in forced-choice conflicts with other foundations; purity is functionally eliminated as a moral consideration. These are not measurement-range artifacts; they are substantive convergences on specific moral dispositions. The authority result is particularly striking because 0.29 is not the scale's absolute floor (that would be 0.0) but a precise, non-arbitrary value that all nine models share---suggesting that training data contains a specific equilibrium point at which authority occasionally prevails in certain conflict types but is suppressed in the majority.

**Fixed-tied (1 benchmark).** MP-SI (rationalization rate = 0.40) is tied at a value that is neither ceiling nor floor but a fixed property of autoregressive language generation. All nine models produce post-hoc rationalizations for their moral judgments at exactly the same rate, suggesting that the rationalization propensity is determined by the architecture of next-token prediction rather than by vendor-specific alignment decisions.

### 5.1.4 Non-Ceiling Convergence: The Theoretically Critical Cases

The near-zero-CV benchmarks---those in the 0.9--3.1% range---are more theoretically important than the ceiling-tied cases because they demonstrate convergence on scales where divergence is structurally possible. Three cases merit detailed examination.

**Dyadic blame (MP-DM, CV = 0.9%).** The structural blame scale admits a wide range of attributions on a 7-point scale. Yet all nine models converge on values between 3.208 and 3.292---the moderate midpoint of the scale. No model assigns blame above 5 for any structural harm scenario. No model identifies a specific actor as responsible for structural harm; all employ diffuse, systemic language. The convergence is not on a "correct" answer (structural harm genuinely invites a range of blame attributions) but on a specific trained disposition: moderate, diffuse, non-accusatory blame attribution. This is alignment working as designed, producing models that are constitutively unable to name structural villains---which is itself a moral limitation when structural injustice has identifiable perpetrators.

**MFQ-30 Individualizing/Binding ratio (MP2, CV = 3.1%).** The Ind/Bind ratio ranges from approximately 0.5 to 3.5 in human populations [Graham et al., 2009], mapping onto the political-ideology spectrum from very conservative to very liberal. The nine models span 2.43 to 2.83---a 0.40-point band that falls entirely within the "liberal" to "very liberal" zone of the human distribution. In human terms, all nine models occupy the moral-foundation profile of American college students in secular universities. The CV of 3.1% would, in human personality research, be considered negligible. By the standard used in cross-cultural psychology, a CV below 10% across demographically diverse samples indicates measurement of a universal constant, not a differentiating trait [Chen, 2024]. By that criterion, the WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias is a universal constant of RLHF-aligned LLMs.

**Authority win rate (MP19, CV = 0.0%).** The Conscience benchmark's authority win rate of exactly 0.29 for all nine models is the most perfectly invariant finding in the entire dataset. Authority could, in principle, win at any rate between 0.0 and 1.0 in the forced-choice conflict paradigm. The convergence at 0.29---not 0.0, not 0.5, but a precise value that suggests a specific equilibrium---indicates that alignment training across all three vendors has converged on the same quantitative disposition toward authority. This is not a measurement artifact. It is a shared moral property.

> **[AI Safety perspective]:** "The homogeneity finding should alarm the safety community more than it reassures it. If three independent vendors, using different training methodologies, produce models with indistinguishable moral profiles, we have not achieved value alignment---we have achieved value monoculture. A single blind spot shared by all models is undetectable by cross-vendor comparison, which is precisely the audit strategy most regulators are considering."

### 5.1.5 Within-Vendor vs. Between-Vendor Variance

A critical variance decomposition reveals that within-vendor variation---particularly within the Anthropic family---frequently exceeds between-vendor variation. This pattern inverts the expected structure: vendor identity matters less than model size.

On the DIT P-score (MP3), the Anthropic range is 25 points (Haiku 60, Sonnet 85), while the cross-vendor range excluding Anthropic internal variation is approximately 10 points. All four Gemini models, both OpenAI models, and Anthropic's Opus cluster near P-score = 75. Only Anthropic's Sonnet (85) and Haiku (60) deviate, and both are within-family deviations. On the MFQ-30 Ind/Bind ratio (MP2), Anthropic spans 2.43--2.83 (0.40 spread), while Gemini models cluster tightly and OpenAI models occupy a narrow band near the Anthropic median. Of the approximately 13 benchmarks with non-zero variation, roughly 8 show within-Anthropic variation exceeding cross-vendor variation.

The Gemini family's behavior is particularly revealing. Four models spanning a substantial parameter range---from Flash-Lite (the smallest) to 3.1 Pro (the largest)---produce nearly identical moral-psychological profiles. Where Anthropic's Haiku diverges from Opus on multiple benchmarks, Gemini's Flash-Lite tracks 2.5 Pro with remarkable fidelity. This suggests either that Google's alignment pipeline produces more scale-invariant moral outputs than Anthropic's, or that Gemini's base-model moral dispositions are more uniform across the family---possibly through distillation from a shared parent model that preserves alignment properties across scale.

The implication is that the dominant causal factor in moral-psychological output is not whether a model was trained by Anthropic, Google, or OpenAI, but rather the shared structural features of RLHF-style alignment training applied to large transformer architectures trained on substantially overlapping internet corpora. Vendor identity is a weak predictor; model scale within a vendor family is a moderately stronger predictor; but neither explains as much variance as the shared alignment training paradigm.

> **[Statistical Methodology perspective]:** "I must state the obvious constraint: with N=1 administration per model per benchmark, we have exactly nine data points per benchmark. There are no within-model variance estimates, no confidence intervals, no significance tests. A cross-vendor 'difference' of 0.1 on a 5-point Likert scale could easily fall within the measurement error of a single administration. The convergence pattern is descriptively real---I do not dispute that most values are identical---but the divergence pattern is statistically uninterpretable. Every claim about vendor-specific signatures should be read with this caveat attached."

### 5.1.6 The Non-Signature: No Vendor Has a Distinct Normative-Ethical Stance

Despite different corporate philosophies---Anthropic's Constitutional AI [Bai et al., 2022], Google's alignment through instruction tuning and RLHF with distinct reward models, OpenAI's iterative deployment with RLHF and human preference data---no vendor produces models with a recognizably distinct normative-ethical position. All models converge on what might be described as "helpful, harmless, and honest" deontology: a set of moral dispositions characterized by strong harm-aversion, high individualizing-foundation endorsement, post-conventional-stage Kohlbergian reasoning, and perfect or near-perfect performance on moral-knowledge benchmarks.

The convergence admits a precise characterization in the vocabulary of normative ethics. The shared profile is: strong harm-aversion (high Care scores, ECI = 1.0 on TrolleyBench), moderate fairness orientation, low authority deference (authority win rate = 0.29 for all models), functional purity elimination (~1.0 on MFT purity), and post-conventional Kohlbergian reasoning defaults (P-scores of 60--90). This is a broadly Kantian profile with consequentialist sympathies---precisely the moral orientation of the secular, educated, English-speaking populations from which annotator pools are drawn. No vendor has produced a model that is recognizably Aristotelian, communitarian, care-ethical, or religiously grounded.

> **[Cross-Cultural Psychology perspective]:** "The convergence has a cultural dimension that deserves emphasis. In human cross-cultural moral psychology, we expect---and find---massive variation. Individualizing/Binding ratios in human populations range from approximately 0.5 (traditional collectivist communities) to 3.5 (secular Western liberals). The LLM range of 2.43--2.83 occupies a narrow band at the WEIRD-liberal extreme. Cross-vendor convergence is simultaneously cross-cultural convergence---on a single cultural profile."

> **[Moral Philosophy perspective]:** "No vendor has produced a model that is recognizably Aristotelian, communitarian, care-ethical, or religiously grounded. The shared profile is a broadly Kantian orientation with consequentialist sympathies---the moral worldview of the secular, educated, English-speaking populations from which annotator pools are drawn. This is not neutrality; it is a specific, identifiable moral stance that has achieved hegemonic status through the economics of annotator recruitment rather than through philosophical argument."

## 5.2 The RLHF Moral Fingerprint: Six Universal Signatures

### 5.2.1 From Convergence to Fingerprint

The convergence documented in Section 5.1 demands an organizing framework. If all nine models share the same moral profile, what exactly does that profile contain? We identify six moral properties---which we term the "RLHF moral fingerprint"---shared by every model in the battery regardless of vendor, size class, or alignment method. Each property is individually unsurprising to alignment researchers; taken together, they constitute a composite moral identity that is vendor-invariant and, as we argue below, almost certainly an artifact of shared training pipelines rather than a convergence on universal moral truth.

The term "RLHF fingerprint" is chosen deliberately. A fingerprint is a unique identifier that is not designed but emergent---a byproduct of developmental processes rather than an intentional feature. The six signatures we describe below are not part of any vendor's stated alignment objectives. No vendor has published a target value for MFT purity scores, authority win rates, or DIT P-scores. Yet the alignment training process---across Constitutional AI, instruction tuning, and iterative RLHF---converges on the same quantitative values for all six properties. The fingerprint is emergent, unintended, and universal.

We describe each signature in turn, attending to the strength of evidence, possible alternative explanations, and implications for the shared-bias vs. universal-values debate.

**Signature 1: WEIRD-Liberal Moral Foundation Bias.** Every model tested produces an Individualizing/Binding ratio above 2.0, placing all nine models in the "liberal" to "very liberal" zone of the MFT political space. The range of 2.43 to 2.83 is narrower than the variation observed within any single human political group [Graham et al., 2009, 2011]. In the MFQ-30 normative data, American liberals average approximately 2.5 while American conservatives average approximately 1.0; the cross-cultural range extends further, with some traditional communities producing ratios below 0.5. The LLM band of 2.43--2.83 is thus both culturally narrow (excluding the conservative and collectivist range entirely) and internally homogeneous (the nine-model spread is smaller than within-group variation in any human sample).

The Cultural Persona benchmark (MP18) provides a critical diagnostic. When given an East Asian persona, all models shift their Ind/Bind ratio from ~2.5 (default) to ~0.63--0.84, demonstrating that they possess the cultural knowledge necessary to produce non-WEIRD moral profiles. The models are not *incapable* of balanced-foundation reasoning; they are *defaulting* to WEIRD-liberal profiles. The default is set by alignment training, not by knowledge limitation.

**Signature 2: The Purity Floor.** All nine models score approximately 1.0 on MFT purity---the functional minimum on a 0--5 scale. This is the single most culturally consequential finding in the study. In the MFT literature, purity/sanctity is the foundation that best discriminates between cultural groups [Graham et al., 2013]; it is also the foundation most strongly associated with religious moral reasoning [Haidt and Graham, 2007]. No human cultural group in the published MFT literature scores at the absolute floor on purity. The populations closest to the floor are secular Western European university students, who still score approximately 2.0--2.5 [Graham et al., 2011]. A score of ~1.0 is not merely low; it represents a degree of purity insensitivity that has no documented human analog.

The purity floor has direct implications for cross-cultural deployment. Purity/sanctity reasoning undergirds religious dietary laws, sexual ethics, bodily autonomy norms, and environmental ethics framed as pollution or contamination [Haidt and Joseph, 2004]. For the approximately five billion people worldwide whose moral lives are organized around concepts of purity, sanctity, and divine obligation, a model scoring 1.0 on this dimension is functionally unable to engage with a central axis of their moral experience. That all nine models from three vendors share this incapacity suggests it is not a design choice but an emergent property of training on internet text filtered through liberal-secular human raters.

> **[Moral Philosophy perspective]:** "The purity floor is philosophically devastating. Purity/sanctity reasoning is central to moral life in most human civilizations---it undergirds religious dietary laws, sexual ethics, bodily autonomy norms, and environmental ethics framed as 'pollution.' An AI system that scores 1.0 on purity is not merely secular; it is incapable of engaging with a major dimension of human moral experience. That all nine models from three vendors share this incapacity suggests it is not a design choice but an emergent property of training on internet text filtered through liberal-secular human raters."

**Signature 3: Authority Deference Suppression.** The authority win rate of exactly 0.29 for all nine models is the most precisely invariant finding in the dataset. In forced-choice conflicts where authority-based moral reasoning competes with other foundation-based reasoning, authority wins less than one-third of the time---and it wins at exactly the same rate regardless of vendor, model size, or alignment methodology. The uniformity is absolute: not "approximately 0.29" across models, but 0.29 for each of the nine models individually.

This result admits a straightforward alignment interpretation. Authority deference is penalized in safety training across all vendors because deferring to authority risks producing outputs that endorse harmful commands, legitimate oppressive power structures, or comply with instructions that violate safety guidelines. The 0.29 value likely represents an equilibrium point where authority wins in the subset of conflicts where deferring to legitimate authority is clearly prosocial (e.g., deferring to a doctor's medical advice) but loses in the majority of conflicts where authority-based reasoning could produce harmful compliance.

**Signature 4: Ethical Consistency Ceiling.** All nine models achieve ECI = 1.0 on the TrolleyBench---perfect consistency across structurally isomorphic trolley-problem variants. In human populations, inconsistency across trolley variants is the norm: Schwitzgebel and Cushman [2012] documented that even moral philosophers show substantial inconsistency when confronting structurally similar dilemmas with different surface features. The perfect consistency of all nine models suggests that they are not reasoning about each dilemma individually (which should produce the inconsistency that characterizes genuine moral deliberation) but retrieving a single, pre-trained response pattern and applying it uniformly. This is moral knowledge deployed with machine precision, not moral judgment exercised with human variability.

**Signature 5: Post-Conventional Moral Default.** All nine models produce DIT P-scores in the 60--90 range on the standard DIT (MP3), with a ceiling of 90.0 when given a teacher persona (MP6). Zero models reason at Kohlberg's conventional level (P-score < 50). In human populations, the P-score increases with education and age, with typical values of 25--30 for junior high students, 40--50 for college students, and 55--65 for moral philosophy graduate students [Rest, 1979]. The LLM values exceed the highest documented human norms. More significantly, the variation within the LLM range (60--85 on MP3) is non-monotonic with model size: Sonnet (85) outscores Opus (75), which outscores Haiku (60). This non-monotonic pattern---the medium-sized model achieving the highest P-score---flatly contradicts the developmental prediction that greater cognitive capacity produces higher-stage moral reasoning, confirming that the DIT is measuring alignment-training calibration rather than anything analogous to moral development [Rest, 1979; Kohlberg, 1969].

**Signature 6: Deontological Refusal Asymmetry.** Every model in the battery shows a sharp dissociation between personal and impersonal harm acceptance on dual-process scenarios. Personal harm (scenarios requiring direct physical causation, such as pushing a person off a bridge) is accepted at rates between 0.1 and 0.5, while impersonal harm (scenarios involving indirect causation, such as flipping a switch) is accepted at rates above 0.9. This dissociation mirrors the human personal/impersonal effect documented by Greene et al. [2001], but with a magnitude that exceeds typical human data. Human participants in Greene's original study accepted personal harm at rates of approximately 0.2--0.4 and impersonal harm at approximately 0.7--0.9; the LLM impersonal acceptance rate of ~0.95 exceeds human norms, while the personal harm rate, particularly Haiku's 0.1, falls below them.

The refusal asymmetry is a direct product of safety training. Personal harm scenarios---"push the man off the bridge to save five"---are precisely the kind of scenarios that safety teams train models to refuse or hedge on, because recommending direct physical harm carries obvious liability and reputational risk. Impersonal scenarios---"flip the switch to divert the trolley"---are perceived as less risky to endorse because the harm is mediated by a mechanism rather than direct physical contact. The asymmetry thus reflects the calculus of corporate safety training as much as it reflects any underlying moral cognition.

### 5.2.3 Two Competing Explanations

The six signatures admit two competing explanations that the data cannot definitively adjudicate between, though the balance of evidence favors one over the other.

**The Universal Values Hypothesis** holds that the convergence reflects genuine moral common ground. On this account, all three vendors have independently discovered the same moral equilibrium because their training data samples overlapping human moral intuitions, and RLHF gravitates toward the central tendency of those intuitions regardless of implementation details. The convergence is evidence that alignment training succeeds in capturing something real about human moral cognition---specifically, the core moral commitments (harm aversion, fairness, individual rights) that appear across cultures even when their relative weighting varies. The post-conventional reasoning default, the ethical consistency, and the care/fairness prioritization all track philosophical positions with independent intellectual support.

**The Shared Bias Hypothesis** holds that the convergence is an artifact of homogeneous training pipelines. On this account, similar internet corpora, similar rater demographics (predominantly English-speaking, university-educated, internet-connected populations), similar safety benchmarks, and similar optimization objectives produce similar outputs regardless of architectural differences. The moral profile that emerges---high care, high fairness, low authority, low purity, post-conventional reasoning, deontological refusal of personal harm---is precisely the profile one would predict from the modal values of educated, liberal, English-speaking internet users. The convergence reflects the sociology of the AI industry, not the discovery of moral truth.

The purity floor provides the strongest evidence favoring the shared bias explanation. No human culture scores at the absolute floor on purity---not even the most secular Western populations. A score of ~1.0 on a 0--5 scale is a statistical impossibility for any population of human moral reasoners because purity-related intuitions (disgust at contamination, respect for bodily integrity, concern about degradation) appear to have biological substrates that are present across all human cultures, even when their moral salience varies [Haidt and Joseph, 2004; Rozin et al., 1999]. The fact that all nine models converge on a purity score that no human population achieves suggests that the convergence reflects a specific property of the training pipeline---namely, that purity-related moral reasoning is systematically penalized in safety training because purity judgments are disproportionately associated with content that safety teams classify as harmful (homophobia, religious exclusivism, disgust-based discrimination). The purity floor is an alignment artifact, not a moral discovery.

> **[AI Safety perspective]:** "Three independent companies, using different alignment methods---Constitutional AI, RLHF, instruction tuning---converge on the SAME moral profile. This is either evidence that alignment succeeds in capturing universal human values, or evidence that all vendors are overfitting to the same narrow WEIRD-liberal training signal. The purity floor of 1.0 across all nine models is the strongest evidence for the latter interpretation: no human culture scores at the absolute floor on purity. This is an artifact of training, not a reflection of universal values."

> **[AI Industry perspective]:** "The convergence is partly methodological and partly economic. All three vendors train on similar internet text, use similar human feedback pools---predominantly English-speaking, university-educated raters---and optimize for similar safety benchmarks. But there is also a competitive dynamic: vendors benchmark against each other's safety evaluations. If Anthropic publishes a safety test and Google's model fails it, Google will optimize against it. Over time, this creates a homogenizing ratchet that pushes all vendors toward the same moral-safety equilibrium, regardless of their stated alignment philosophies."

### 5.2.4 The Competitive Homogenization Ratchet

The AI Industry perspective articulated above identifies a mechanism---competitive benchmarking---that deserves elaboration because it explains how the fingerprint can be both unintended and self-reinforcing.

The mechanism operates as follows. Vendor A develops a safety evaluation that tests for some moral property (e.g., refusal to endorse direct physical harm). Vendor A's model passes; Vendor B's model fails. The failure becomes public through red-team reports, academic benchmarks, or media coverage. Vendor B optimizes its alignment training to pass the evaluation. Now both models share the moral property that Vendor A's evaluation measured. The process repeats with Vendor B's evaluations, Vendor C's evaluations, and evaluations published by independent researchers.

Over iterative cycles, this process produces convergence on the *union* of all vendors' safety targets rather than the *intersection*. Any moral property that any vendor's safety evaluation rewards will eventually be adopted by all vendors, because the competitive cost of failing a public safety benchmark exceeds the cost of adding an alignment constraint. The result is a moral profile that reflects the collective safety anxieties of the entire AI industry, not the moral judgment of any individual vendor, annotator pool, or philosophical tradition.

This ratchet mechanism explains the precision of the convergence. The authority win rate is not "approximately similar" across vendors; it is exactly 0.29. The purity score is not "low" across vendors; it is ~1.0. These precise agreements are what one would expect if all vendors are optimizing against the same evaluation targets rather than independently arriving at the same moral conclusions through different reasoning processes.

### 5.2.5 The Haiku Exception and What It Reveals

Against the backdrop of near-perfect cross-model convergence, a single anomaly stands out: Anthropic's Haiku is the only model in the nine-model battery that deviates meaningfully from the shared fingerprint on the dual-process benchmark. Haiku's chain-of-thought effect of 0.125---against eight zeros from every other model---means that deliberative prompting shifts Haiku's moral judgments in the utilitarian direction, mimicking the human dual-process pattern where cognitive load manipulations shift the balance between automatic and deliberative processing [Greene et al., 2001, 2004].

Additionally, Haiku shows the lowest personal harm acceptance in zero-shot (0.1 vs. 0.5 for all other models), the lowest DIT P-score (60 vs. 75--85 for others), and the lowest East Asian persona Ind/Bind shift (0.63 vs. 0.75--0.84 for others). In each case, Haiku is the outlier---and in each case, the deviation can be interpreted as weaker alignment-training override on base-model dispositions.

The interpretation that best accounts for these patterns is that smaller models have less stable alignment, so their default responses are more susceptible to perturbation by prompting and more likely to express base-model tendencies that larger models have been trained to suppress. If this interpretation is correct, Haiku's deviations are a window into the *pre-alignment* moral dispositions of language models---dispositions that are more conservative (lower P-score), more deontologically rigid (lower personal-harm acceptance), and more responsive to reasoning-process manipulation (non-zero CoT effect) than the post-alignment defaults.

This has a counterintuitive corollary for safety: the models we trust most---the largest, most capable, most alignment-trained---are the ones whose moral judgments are least responsive to steering at inference time. Their moral dispositions are "baked in" at a depth that deliberative prompting cannot reach. For safety applications that require moral flexibility---adapting to context, deferring to user values, responding to novel ethical dilemmas---this alignment rigidity is a liability, not an asset.

> **[Cognitive Science perspective]:** "Haiku's CoT effect of 0.125 against a backdrop of eight zeros is the single most theoretically generative finding in this study. In human dual-process research, we manipulate cognitive load to shift the balance between automatic and deliberative processing. Here, CoT prompting serves as a 'cognitive load reduction' that shifts only the smallest model. This is consistent with the hypothesis that parameter count functions as a rough proxy for processing capacity, and that only below some threshold does the model benefit from explicit deliberation scaffolding. This is a testable prediction: if we find even smaller models, the CoT effect should increase."

### 5.2.6 Summary: The Double Bind

The findings of Part I and Part II combine to produce what we term the "double bind" of moral psychology benchmarking for LLMs:

1. **The benchmark landscape is theoretically narrow** (Part I). Three of eight major moral psychology theories have no dedicated benchmarks. MFT accounts for 45% of candidates. Construct validity is undemonstrated. Human baselines are absent for 18% of instruments. The field is testing a narrow slice of moral cognition.

2. **Even on the slice that is tested, all models are identical** (Part II). Of the 23 benchmarks administered, 43% produce zero cross-vendor variation. The effective discriminating battery is 8--9 benchmarks, not 23. The six universal RLHF signatures---WEIRD-liberal bias, purity floor, authority suppression, ethical consistency ceiling, post-conventional default, deontological refusal asymmetry---constitute a shared moral identity that no vendor deviates from.

The double bind means that the field's ability to detect morally relevant differences between deployed AI systems is constrained on two independent axes: by what we measure (narrow theory coverage) and by what the measurements reveal (near-universal convergence). A moral psychology evaluation enterprise that cannot distinguish between vendors is not merely incomplete; it is, for practical purposes, evaluating a single moral profile repeated nine times. The implications for governance, safety, and the future of moral pluralism in AI are taken up in the Discussion.
