Abstract
As I argue, roughly, AI systems solve problems based on mechanically
computational, means-end reasoning. Call this mechanical instrumental
rationality. Human intelligence (HI), however, to the extent that is
rational, it is not mechanically computable, namely, it is not reducible to
mechanical computations for at least four basic reasons I expound. First,
HI, to the extent that is rational, it is not just mechanical instrumental
rationality, it is categorically normative: we don’t just find ourselves with
contingent ends, we can choose stance-independent ends on the basis
of categorical reasons (or at least we should). Even if HI is not
categorically normative, as some philosophers contend, it is embodied,

--- Page 3 ---

Humean instrumental rationality. Second, AI lacks autonomous reasons-
responsiveness, which is a constitutive condition of rational HI. Third,
rational HI necessarily involves indispensable affective experience, and
we currently have no clue how to build AI systems with subjective,
phenomenal life. Fourth, rational HI is not codifiable and computable in
absolute moral principles. It relies on virtue-based, moral intuition (and
practical wisdom) to discern what is morally right/wrong in a context. I
conclude that current AI systems lack the basic cognitive prerequisites
for being (virtuous) moral agents in any robust sense and, therefore, they
require constant normative guidance and surveillance by virtuous HI.

Appendix
Question asked to ChatGPT
Was Napoleon’s retreat from Moscow in 1812 the right tactical decision?
Answer
The retreat of Napoleon’s Grande Armée from Moscow in 1812 is one of

--- Page 41 ---

the most infamous and debated military decisions in history. While it is
impossible to know for certain whether the retreat was the "right"
decision, we can examine the circumstances and strategic
considerations that led to it.
Background
Napoleon's invasion of Russia in June 1812 aimed to weaken Russia's
ability to continue fighting against France and its allies, while also gaining
a strategic advantage by capturing Moscow, the Russian capital. The
French army, comprising over 680,000 soldiers, advanced deep into
Russia, but encountered significant resistance and logistical challenges.
By September 1812, Napoleon’s army was exhausted, supply lines were
stretched, and the Russian winter was approaching.
The situation became increasingly dire as the French army faced:
•  1. Limited supplies: The French army had exhausted their food,
ammunition, and medical supplies.
•  2. Harsh weather: The Russian winter was setting in, making
movement and communication difficult.
•  3. Russian scorched earth tactics: The Russians had deliberately
destroyed their own cities, infrastructure, and supplies, leaving
nothing for the French army to capture or plunder.
•  4. Growing opposition: Russian forces continued to harass and
attack the French army from behind.

--- Page 42 ---

•  5. Morale: The French army was demoralized by the harsh
conditions, lack of supplies, and perceived betrayal by Napoleon.
Retreat
On October 19, 1812, Napoleon’s army began its withdrawal from
Moscow. The retreat was a chaotic and bloody process, with thousands
of soldiers freezing to death or being killed by Russian partisans and
Cossacks. The retreating army suffered heavy losses, including over
300,000 casualties (killed, wounded, or missing).
Was the retreat a tactical decision?
Arguments in favor of the retreat:
•  1. Avoiding encirclement: By retreating from Moscow, Napoleon
avoided being trapped between the city and the Russian army,
which would have made further resistance futile.
•  2. Preserving core forces: The retreat allowed Napoleon to save
his most experienced and battle-hardened troops for future battles.
•  3. Avoiding catastrophic losses: Continued fighting in Moscow
would have led to further devastating losses for both sides.
Arguments against the retreat:
•  1. Opportunity lost: The failure to capture Moscow meant that
Napoleon missed an opportunity to break Russia's spirit and
negotiate a peace treaty on favorable terms.

--- Page 43 ---

•  2. Strategic surprise: The sudden retreat caught the Russians off
guard, allowing them to reorganize and launch counterattacks.
•  3. Loss of momentum: The retreat ended Napoleon's momentum
and allowed his enemies to regroup.
Conclusion
While it is difficult to say definitively whether Napoleon's retreat from
Moscow was the "right" decision, it was likely a necessary one given the
circumstances. By retreating, Napoleon preserved his core forces and
avoided catastrophic losses. However, the decision also meant missing
an opportunity to capitalize on his initial successes and potentially
changing the course of the war.
In hindsight, Napoleon might have considered alternative strategies,
such as:
•  1. Wintering in Poland or Prussia: Allowing his army to rest and
reorganize during the winter months before resuming their
campaign.
•  2. Pursuing a more limited objective: Focusing on capturing key
strategic locations or cities rather than attempting to conquer all of
Russia.
•  3. Improving logistics: Strengthening his supply lines and logistics
network to better support his army in the field.
Ultimately, Napoleon’s failure in Russia marked a turning point in his

--- Page 44 ---

fortunes and set the stage for his eventual downfall at Waterloo in 1815.
