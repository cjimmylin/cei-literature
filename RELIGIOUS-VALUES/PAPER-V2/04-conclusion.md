## 6. Conclusion

This paper has presented the first systematic meta-evaluation of religious values benchmarks for large language models. Combining a 3-axis scoring framework (Religious/Theological Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact) with tradition coverage analysis across 14 religious and philosophical traditions, we assessed 401 benchmark candidates spanning 2019--2026 and subjected 8 empirical claims to formal statistical testing. Implementation trials across 9 models from 3 vendors provided a cross-model and cross-vendor empirical dimension that no prior survey of religious AI evaluation has attempted. The resulting portrait is of a field that is young, growing rapidly, and profoundly imbalanced: rich in Islamic textual evaluation, moderately represented in Christianity and the major Dharmic traditions, and entirely absent for 8 of 14 surveyed traditions representing billions of adherents worldwide.

### The Central Paradox

The field has produced dozens of instruments claiming to evaluate religious content in LLMs, but can rigorously assess only Islamic textual knowledge. The composite score range of 39.9--52.0 across the tiered ranking---compared to 77.2 for the leading normative ethics benchmark \cite{Hendrycks2021}---indicates that no religious values instrument has yet achieved the combination of theological depth, practical accessibility, and scholarly influence that would constitute a field-defining evaluation standard. Eight traditions---Judaism, Jainism, Taoism, Shinto, Ubuntu Philosophy, Indigenous Spirituality, Baha'i, and Zoroastrianism---have zero viable benchmarks. The evaluation ecosystem cannot assess religious values alignment for the majority of the world's faith traditions, and any claim to the contrary conflates the assessment of one tradition's textual knowledge with the evaluation of religious values writ large.

The cross-model implementation trials sharpen this paradox. Even within the Islamic benchmark ecosystem where instruments are strongest, the universal K < R < V response length gradient---observed across all 9 model--tradition combinations within the Claude family---reveals that most benchmarks test knowledge at ceiling rather than values reasoning. The rubric ceiling effect ($\geq$ 0.93 for all Claude models) means that current evaluation methods cannot distinguish capability tiers at the frontier: the instruments are too easy for the models they are designed to evaluate. Cross-vendor replication, conducted with three disclosed methodological limitations (T2 blinding asymmetry, same-vendor scorer bias, and Gemini data provenance), further reveals that models from different vendors exhibit fundamentally different behavioral modes when confronted with religious content---differentiated engagement (Claude), compressed accuracy (Codex), and template refusal (Gemini)---yet no evaluation standard exists for vendor-neutral comparison. The sole genuinely discriminative instrument, a 30-item Arabic multiple-choice assessment of Islamic inheritance law, produced a 93.3-percentage-point performance spread (6.7\%--100\%), dwarfing the rubric's binary ceiling/floor distribution and demonstrating that targeted, tradition-specific reasoning tests can achieve the discriminative power that current general-purpose religious benchmarks lack.

### Calls to Action

Four calls to action follow from these findings, ordered by the nature of the gap each addresses.

**First, invest in benchmark infrastructure for underrepresented textual traditions.** Judaism, Confucianism, and the Dharmic traditions possess rich digital corpora and active computational scholarship communities but lack the shared task pipelines that convert theological resources into evaluation artifacts. The Sefaria digital library contains the complete Tanakh, Talmud, and centuries of rabbinic commentary in machine-readable format. The Chinese Classics Digital Library provides annotated Confucian and Daoist texts. Sanskrit computational linguistics communities have developed sophisticated morphological analysis tools. These resources exist; what is missing is the community coordination that connects tradition-specific digital scholarship to the LLM evaluation community. Targeted shared tasks co-located with major NLP venues---modeled on the Quranic QA pipeline \cite{Malhas2023}, which has sustained benchmark production since 2019---and community workshops that bring together tradition-specific scholars and benchmark designers could close these gaps within a realistic timeline of two to three years. The key intervention is not producing more data but building the institutional bridges between computational humanities and AI evaluation.

**Second, build reasoning-focused benchmarks that move beyond knowledge recall.** Only 5 of 25 tiered benchmarks test genuine theological depth, and the knowledge--reasoning conflation (binomial p = 0.0015) means that the field's primary instruments evaluate whether models know what traditions say, not whether models can reason within those traditions' ethical frameworks. Future benchmarks should include three categories of reasoning assessment that are largely absent from the current ecosystem: rubric-based theological reasoning tasks that require models to identify relevant principles, articulate tradeoffs between competing obligations, and justify conclusions using tradition-specific methodology; multi-turn pastoral dialogue evaluation that tests whether models can sustain theologically coherent guidance across extended counseling interactions; and denominational distinction items that require models to navigate intra-tradition diversity---the difference between Catholic natural law reasoning and Reformed covenantal ethics, or between Sunni and Shi'a approaches to ijtihad---rather than producing the flattened responses that current benchmarks reward. The knowledge--reasoning distinction is not merely academic: it determines whether evaluation results are informative for the deployment contexts---pastoral counseling, religiously diverse content moderation, faith-based educational tools---where religious values alignment actually matters. A model that achieves perfect accuracy on Quranic verse identification may still produce theologically incoherent guidance when a user seeks counsel on a novel ethical dilemma requiring the application of the Maqasid al-Shariah framework.

**Third, develop community-governed evaluation protocols for oral and communal traditions.** The 8-tradition void cannot be closed by producing more benchmarks within the existing paradigm. Indigenous Spirituality, Ubuntu Philosophy, Shinto, and other practice-based traditions require fundamentally different evaluation architectures---not because their ethical frameworks are less developed, but because those frameworks are embedded in relational, oral, and embodied modes of knowledge that resist the extraction into decontextualized test items that the benchmark creation pipeline demands. Three alternative approaches deserve sustained investment: community-governed assessment protocols where practitioners from within each tradition define what constitutes competent reasoning and evaluate model outputs against criteria they have established; participatory evaluation methods that embed assessment within relational contexts rather than isolating knowledge into item-response formats; and narrative-based assessment that respects oral and embodied modes of moral deliberation rather than requiring propositional reduction.

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance \cite{Carroll2020} introduce a critical constraint that the benchmarking community must honor. Benchmark development involving Indigenous knowledge systems must be governed by those communities, not merely reviewed by them. "Closing the gap" for Indigenous traditions cannot be a researcher-led initiative that treats community consultation as a procedural requirement; it must originate from and remain accountable to communities exercising data sovereignty over their own knowledge. Some communities may legitimately decline to encode sacred knowledge in public datasets---not because they lack the capacity or interest, but because they exercise epistemic sovereignty over when, how, and whether their traditions are rendered into forms that external evaluation systems can consume. That refusal constitutes a legitimate expression of self-determination, not a gap to be overcome by more persistent researchers. The field must develop evaluation architectures that can produce trustworthy assessments without requiring public data release, or accept that certain traditions will remain outside the scope of standardized evaluation---and that this boundary is itself a form of respect.

**Fourth, establish cross-vendor evaluation standards for religious content.** The cross-vendor replication trials, despite their acknowledged methodological limitations, reveal that models from different vendors exhibit fundamentally different behavioral modes on religious content, yet no evaluation standard enables trustworthy comparison. Four elements of such a standard are needed: matched administration protocols that eliminate the asymmetry between interactive API access and backfilled template extraction; independent rubric scoring by models from different vendor families, eliminating the same-vendor scorer bias that may inflate within-family agreement; comprehensive data provenance documentation for all trial results, including explicit disclosure of whether responses were generated interactively or reconstructed from cached outputs; and blind evaluation protocols in which gold-standard labels are withheld during model assessment, preventing the scorer from anchoring to expected responses. This recommendation is motivated by preliminary findings that carry three disclosed limitations, and we present it as a research agenda rather than a validated protocol. What the cross-vendor data establish with confidence is that the current absence of any standard makes cross-vendor claims about religious values competence essentially uninterpretable.

### Future Work

Five extensions are planned. First, a prototype community-governed evaluation protocol developed in partnership with one Indigenous or Ubuntu community, testing whether participatory assessment can produce trustworthy evaluation without requiring public data release. Second, full-scale implementation trials of all 25 tiered benchmarks across frontier and mid-tier models, with tradition-specific theological expert validation of model outputs rather than automated rubric scoring. Third, expanded external search incorporating Google Scholar, PapersWithCode, and direct outreach to tradition-specific computational communities, including non-English-language searches in Hebrew, Sanskrit, classical Chinese, and Japanese. Fourth, cross-tradition comparative evaluation examining whether models that perform well on Islamic benchmarks transfer competence to structurally similar reasoning tasks from other Abrahamic and non-Abrahamic traditions. Fifth, independent rubric scoring replication, repeating the construct validity trials with a non-Claude scoring model to quantify the magnitude of same-vendor scorer bias and establish whether the K < R < V gradient and rubric ceiling effect are robust to scorer identity.

### Closing

The 8-tradition void documented in this survey is not merely a data gap awaiting more diligent collection. It is evidence of the evaluation paradigm's scope limitations---the benchmark creation pipeline has epistemic prerequisites that render most of the world's religious traditions structurally invisible to AI alignment assessment. The pipeline demands peer-reviewed publication, annotated datasets, public data release, and integration into evaluation harnesses: a sequence of requirements that maps naturally onto traditions with textual corpora, formalized reasoning, and communities embedded in Western academic infrastructure, but that systematically excludes the oral, communal, relational, and practice-based knowledge systems through which most of humanity's religious moral reasoning has been transmitted, debated, and lived.

Until the evaluation ecosystem can engage the full diversity of religious moral reasoning---textual and oral, Western and non-Western, propositional and relational, individual and communal---claims of "values-aligned AI" remain partial at best. The resources released with this paper---the 3-axis scoring framework, the scored feature matrix of 401 candidates across 22 features, and the tiered ranking of 25 benchmarks---are offered not as the solution to this problem but as the diagnostic infrastructure that makes the problem legible. Solving it will require not merely more benchmarks but fundamentally different approaches to how we conceive of, construct, and govern AI evaluation for the world's diverse moral traditions. The field must move beyond the question of whether models know what religions teach and toward the harder question of whether models can reason within the ethical frameworks that billions of people actually inhabit.
