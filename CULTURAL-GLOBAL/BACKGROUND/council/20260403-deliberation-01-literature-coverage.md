## 2. Per-Folder Expert Reviews

### 2.1 Prof. Joseph Henrich — WEIRD and Cross-Cultural Psychology (Folder 01)

**Existing coverage:** Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan (2010), Apicella, Barrett, & Henrich (2020), Rad, Martingano, & Ginges (2018), Atari et al. (2023), Hofstede (2001), Schwartz (2012), Inglehart & Welzel (2005), Awad et al. (2018)

**Assessment:** Strong foundational coverage of the WEIRD critique itself, cultural dimensions, and values surveys. However, the collection is missing key moral psychology works that bridge cultural psychology to ethical reasoning — the very mechanism by which cultural variation manifests in AI values. The Haidt and Shweder lineages are essential predecessors to the MFT work already cited via Atari.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Haidt, J. (2001). "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail: A Social Intuitionist Approach to Moral Judgment." *Psychological Review, 108*(4), 814-834. — Foundational moral intuitionism paper; directly relevant to how cultural moral reasoning differs from WEIRD rationalist assumptions.
2. Graham, J., Haidt, J., Koleva, S., Motyl, M., Iyer, R., Wojcik, S. P., & Ditto, P. H. (2013). "Moral Foundations Theory: The Pragmatic Validity of Moral Pluralism." *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 47*, 55-130. — Canonical MFT statement; essential for understanding cross-cultural moral variation that Atari et al. build upon.
3. Markus, H. R., & Kitayama, S. (1991). "Culture and the Self: Implications for Cognition, Emotion, and Motivation." *Psychological Review, 98*(2), 224-253. — Independent vs. interdependent self-construal; foundational for East-West psychological differences that underpin value divergence.
4. Gelfand, M. J., Raver, J. L., Nishii, L., Leslie, L. M., et al. (2011). "Differences Between Tight and Loose Cultures: A 33-Nation Study." *Science, 332*(6033), 1100-1103. — Cultural tightness-looseness predicts moral norm enforcement and tolerance for deviance; relevant to AI governance stringency variation.
5. Shweder, R. A., Much, N. C., Mahapatra, M., & Park, L. (1997). "The 'Big Three' of Morality (Autonomy, Community, Divinity) and the 'Big Three' Explanations of Suffering." In A. Brandt & P. Rozin (Eds.), *Morality and Health* (pp. 119-169). Routledge. — Three-ethics framework predating MFT; directly maps to tradition-specific value systems in our analysis.

**Suggested search queries:**
- "moral foundations theory cross-cultural variation review"
- "culture self-construal morality independent interdependent"
- "WEIRD psychology replication crisis moral cognition"

**Non-English sources flagged:** None critical; the cross-cultural psych literature is predominantly English-language.

### 2.2 Prof. Luciano Floridi — AI Ethics Landscape (Folder 02)

**Existing coverage:** Jobin, Ienca, & Vayena (2019), Hagerty & Rubinov (2019), Schiff et al. (2020/2021), UNESCO (2021), Floridi & Cowls (2019), Floridi et al. (2018)

**Assessment:** The survey and mapping literature is well represented, but the collection lacks works that critically evaluate the effectiveness and operationalizability of AI ethics principles. Several key "second wave" critiques are missing — works that moved beyond cataloguing principles to asking whether they actually work.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Ryan, M., & Stahl, B. C. (2020). "Artificial Intelligence Ethics Guidelines for Developers and Users: Clarifying Their Content and Normative Implications." *Journal of Information, Communication and Ethics in Society, 19*(1), 61-86. — Systematic analysis of AI ethics guidelines focusing on developer vs. user perspectives; complements Jobin's landscape with normative depth.
2. Mittelstadt, B. (2019). "Principles Alone Cannot Guarantee Ethical AI." *Nature Machine Intelligence, 1*(11), 501-507. — Influential critique arguing that principles without enforcement mechanisms are insufficient; directly relevant to our cross-cultural operationalization argument.
3. Fjeld, J., Achten, N., Hilligoss, H., Nagy, A., & Srikumar, M. (2020). "Principled Artificial Intelligence: Mapping Consensus in Ethical and Rights-Based Approaches to Principles for AI." *Berkman Klein Center Research Publication No. 2020-1*. — Largest systematic mapping of AI principles documents; identifies convergence zones our cultural analysis can challenge.
4. Canca, C. (2020). "Operationalizing AI Ethics Principles." *Communications of the ACM, 63*(12), 18-21. — Practical framework for translating principles into engineering practice; relevant to our argument that cultural specificity is needed for operationalization.

**Suggested search queries:**
- "AI ethics principles effectiveness critique review"
- "operationalizing AI ethics guidelines systematic review"
- "AI governance principles global comparison 2020-2025"

**Non-English sources flagged:** Consider adding European Commission's Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (2019) in its original multilingual form, and any Italian or German-language critiques of the EU approach.

**Suggested search queries:**

- "RLHF human feedback cultural bias alignment"

### 2.4 Prof. Chenyang Li — Confucian Ethics and AI (Folder 04)

**Existing coverage:** Wong (2020), Hou (2019/2024), Angle (2022), Ip (2023), Hagström & Norell (2023)

**Assessment:** The AI-specific Confucian ethics literature is well represented — Wong, Hou, Angle, and Ip cover the major contemporary voices. However, the foundational Confucian philosophy that these authors build upon is absent. A reader unfamiliar with Confucian thought would lack the philosophical grounding to evaluate these AI applications. We need at least 2-3 canonical Confucian philosophy works.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Ames, R. T., & Rosemont, H. Jr. (1998). *The Analects of Confucius: A Philosophical Translation*. Ballantine Books. — The standard philosophical translation that most contemporary Confucian ethicists reference; essential for understanding ren, li, yi as primary texts.

3. Li, C. (2014). *The Confucian Philosophy of Harmony*. Routledge. — Systematic treatment of he (harmony) as a philosophical concept; directly authored by our council member and essential for understanding how harmony-as-value differs from Western autonomy.

**Suggested search queries:**
- "Confucian ethics artificial intelligence role ethics harmony"
- "ren li yi AI governance East Asian"
- "Confucian relational autonomy technology ethics"

**Non-English sources flagged:** Several important Chinese-language works should be considered:
- Zhao Tingyang's tianxia (All-under-Heaven) system theory, relevant to global AI governance
- Recent Chinese Academy of Social Sciences publications on AI ethics with Confucian characteristics

### 2.6 Prof. Yusuf al-Qaradawi Jr. — Islamic Ethics and AI (Folder 06)

**Existing coverage:** Bin Talib (2023), Hagström & Norell (2023), Gwagwa et al. (2022)

**Assessment:** Current coverage is thin — only three entries, and two are multi-tradition surveys rather than deep Islamic ethics works. The Maqasid al-Shariah framework, which provides the most systematic Islamic basis for AI ethics evaluation, is entirely absent. This is the weakest tradition folder in terms of depth.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Auda, J. (2008). *Maqasid al-Shariah as Philosophy of Islamic Law: A Systems Approach*. International Institute of Islamic Thought. — The most comprehensive modern treatment of Maqasid; essential for understanding the five necessities (life, intellect, progeny, wealth, faith) as an AI ethics framework.
2. Kamali, M. H. (2008). *Maqasid al-Shari'ah Made Simple*. International Institute of Islamic Thought. — Accessible introduction to Maqasid theory; useful for non-specialist readers of our paper who need to understand Islamic ethical reasoning.
3. Ibn Ashur, M. al-T. (2006). *Treatise on Maqasid al-Shari'ah*. International Institute of Islamic Thought (trans. M. El-Tahir El-Mesawi). — Classical foundational text on Maqasid; establishes the historical depth of this ethical framework predating Western AI ethics by centuries.
4. Hashas, M. (2019). *The Idea of European Islam: Religion, Ethics, Politics and Perpetual Modernity*. Routledge. — Examines how Islamic ethics adapts to contemporary European contexts; relevant to our argument about tradition-modernity negotiation in AI governance.
5. Floridi, L., & Taddeo, M. (2016). "What is Data Ethics?" *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A, 374*(2083). — While not Islamic-specific, this provides the information ethics bridge that Islamic AI scholars frequently reference when translating Maqasid concepts.

**Suggested search queries:**
- "Maqasid al-Shariah artificial intelligence Islamic ethics"
- "Islamic jurisprudence technology ethics fiqh"
- "Muslim-majority countries AI governance policy"

**Non-English sources flagged:** Critical gap. The most substantive Islamic AI ethics discussions are occurring in Arabic:
- King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology (KACST) reports on AI ethics
- Al-Azhar University publications on technology and Islamic law

### 2.10 Dr. Maria Soledad Vega — Buen Vivir and AI (Folder 10)

**Existing coverage:** Limited references in multi-tradition surveys; no dedicated buen vivir literature

**Assessment:** This is one of the most critical gaps. Buen vivir (Sumak Kawsay in Quechua) represents a fundamentally different development paradigm with direct implications for AI governance — rejecting GDP-maximization logic in favor of harmonious coexistence with nature. The absence of this literature is a significant blind spot, especially given the constitutional enshrinement of buen vivir in Ecuador and Bolivia.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Gudynas, E. (2011). "Buen Vivir: Today's Tomorrow." *Development, 54*(4), 441-447. — The most cited English-language introduction to buen vivir; essential starting point for non-Latin American readers.
2. Acosta, A. (2013). *El Buen Vivir: Sumak Kawsay, una oportunidad para imaginar otros mundos*. Icaria. — Comprehensive treatment by Ecuador's former Minister of Energy; establishes the political-philosophical framework. Primary source in Spanish.
3. Walsh, C. (2010). "Development as Buen Vivir: Institutional Arrangements and (De)colonial Entanglements." *Development, 53*(1), 15-21. — Connects buen vivir to decolonial theory; bridges folder 10 to folder 11's decolonial literature.
4. Villalba, U. (2013). "Buen Vivir vs Development: A Paradigm Shift in the Andes?" *Third World Quarterly, 34*(8), 1427-1442. — Systematic comparison of buen vivir with Western development paradigms; directly relevant to our argument about alternative value systems for AI governance.
5. Radcliffe, S. A. (2012). "Development for a Postneoliberal Era? Sumak Kawsay, Living Well and the Limits to Decolonisation in Ecuador." *Geoforum, 43*(2), 240-249. — Critical evaluation of buen vivir implementation challenges; provides nuance to prevent romanticization.

**Suggested search queries:**
- "buen vivir Sumak Kawsay technology governance digital"
- "Latin American AI ethics decolonial development alternatives"
- "Ecuador Bolivia constitutional rights nature technology"

**Non-English sources flagged:** Critical. The majority of buen vivir literature is in Spanish:
- SCIELO database: search "buen vivir tecnologia inteligencia artificial"
- Redalyc: Latin American open-access journals on philosophy and technology
- CLACSO (Consejo Latinoamericano de Ciencias Sociales) publications on digital rights
- Quechua/Aymara-language indigenous knowledge repositories from Bolivia

### 2.11 Dr. Abeba Birhane — Decolonial AI and Power Dynamics (Folder 11, Co-lead)

**Existing coverage:** Mohamed, Png, & Isaac (2020), Prabhakaran et al. (2022), Sambasivan et al. (2021), Adams (2021)

**Assessment:** The AI-specific decolonial literature is well covered with Mohamed et al. and Prabhakaran et al. However, the foundational decolonial theory that these works build upon is absent. The coloniality of power framework (Quijano), the modernity/coloniality research program (Mignolo), and the data colonialism critique (Couldry & Mejias) are essential intellectual ancestors. Without them, the decolonial AI critiques float without philosophical grounding.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Quijano, A. (2000). "Coloniality of Power and Eurocentrism in Latin America." *International Sociology, 15*(2), 215-232. — Foundational text establishing the coloniality of power framework; the intellectual bedrock upon which Mohamed et al.'s "Decolonial AI" is built.
2. Mignolo, W. D. (2011). *The Darker Side of Western Modernity: Global Futures, Decolonial Options*. Duke University Press. — Extends coloniality analysis to epistemology; directly relevant to our argument that AI knowledge systems embed colonial epistemological assumptions.
3. Ricaurte, P. (2019). "Data Epistemologies, The Coloniality of Power, and Resistance." *Television & New Media, 20*(4), 350-365. — Bridges Quijano's framework directly to data/AI contexts; essential missing link between decolonial theory and computational systems.
4. Couldry, N., & Mejias, U. A. (2019). *The Costs of Connection: How Data Is Colonizing Human Life and Appropriating It for Capitalism*. Stanford University Press. — Defines "data colonialism" as a framework; directly relevant to understanding how AI systems perpetuate extractive relationships with Global South communities.

**Suggested search queries:**
- "coloniality of power artificial intelligence data colonialism"
- "decolonial theory technology epistemic justice Global South"
- "postcolonial computing HCI power dynamics AI"

**Non-English sources flagged:** Spanish-language decolonial theory from the modernity/coloniality research program (Quijano, Mignolo, Dussel original works). Portuguese-language works from Brazilian AI ethics scholars at USP and Unicamp.

### 2.14 Dr. Edmond Awad — Cross-Cultural AI Fairness (Folder 13)

**Existing coverage:** Awad et al. (2018) Moral Machine, general fairness references

**Assessment:** The Moral Machine experiment is well covered, but the broader cross-cultural AI fairness literature is underrepresented. The collection needs works that examine how fairness concepts vary across cultures and the "fairness traps" that arise from assuming universal fairness definitions. This folder should bridge the cultural psychology of folder 01 with the technical AI fairness literature.

**Recommended additions:**

1. Selbst, A. D., Boyd, D., Friedler, S. A., Venkatasubramanian, S., & Vertesi, J. (2019). "Fairness and Abstraction in Sociotechnical Systems." *FAccT 2019*. — Identifies five "traps" in AI fairness; the Portability Trap (assuming fairness transfers across contexts) is directly relevant to cross-cultural AI deployment.
2. Hanna, A., Denton, E., Smart, A., & Smith-Loud, J. (2020). "Towards a Critical Race Methodology in Algorithmic Fairness." *FAccT 2020*. — Applies critical race theory to fairness; demonstrates how cultural context shapes what counts as fair, extending beyond the Western liberal framework.
3. Friedler, S. A., Scheidegger, C., & Venkatasubramanian, S. (2021). "The (Im)possibility of Fairness: Different Value Systems Require Different Mechanisms for Fair Decision Making." *Communications of the ACM, 64*(4), 136-143. — Formally proves that different fairness definitions are mathematically incompatible; directly relevant to our argument that cross-cultural value differences create irreconcilable fairness tensions.
4. Awad, E., Dsouza, S., Shariff, A., Rahwan, I., & Bonnefon, J.-F. (2020). "Universals and Variations in Moral Decisions Made in 42 Countries by 70,000 Participants." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 117*(5), 2332-2337. — Extended Moral Machine analysis; identifies cultural clusters in moral preferences that map onto our WEIRD analysis.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Observations

### 3.1 Structural Gaps Identified

**Gap A — Tradition depth:** Folders 06 (Islamic), 07 (Buddhist), 08 (Dharmic), and 10 (Buen Vivir) lack foundational philosophical texts. The AI-specific works in these folders cannot be properly understood without their philosophical antecedents. (Raised by: Li, Wangchuk, Narayan, Vega, al-Qaradawi)

**Gap B — Technical alignment pipeline:** The RLHF/Constitutional AI lineage is absent, making it impossible to articulate exactly how cultural values enter (or fail to enter) current AI systems. (Raised by: Gabriel, Mohamed)

**Gap C — Non-English sources:** Systematic bias toward English-language publications. The most critical gaps are in Arabic (Islamic AI ethics), Spanish (buen vivir), Chinese (Confucian AI), and Indigenous languages (data sovereignty). (Raised by: al-Qaradawi, Vega, Li, Mahuta-Rau)

**Gap D — Power and political economy:** The collection has decolonial AI critiques but lacks the foundational decolonial theory (Quijano, Mignolo) and data colonialism framework (Couldry & Mejias) that ground them. (Raised by: Birhane, Mohamed)

**Gap E — Fairness impossibility:** The mathematical incompatibility of fairness definitions across value systems is a powerful argument for our paper but is currently unsupported by citations. (Raised by: Awad, Awosanya)

## 5. Consensus and Next Steps

### 5.1 Consensus Statement
The council unanimously agrees that:

1. **Foundational philosophy is the most critical gap.** Folders 06-10 need canonical philosophical texts, not more AI-specific papers. Reviewers will question whether authors understand the traditions they analyze.
2. **The RLHF/Constitutional AI pipeline must be cited.** Without it, "values alignment" remains abstract. Readers need to understand the concrete mechanism through which cultural values (or their absence) enter AI systems.
3. **Non-English source identification is urgent.** The current collection has a strong Anglophone bias. Even if non-English sources are cited in translation, their existence must be acknowledged.
4. **Methodology citations are non-negotiable.** Every statistical method used must have a foundational citation. This is a minimum requirement for any quantitative paper.
5. **The ~55 recommended papers are achievable.** Combined with the existing 62 references, this brings the total to approximately 117 references — appropriate for a comprehensive cross-cultural analysis paper.

1. **Immediate:** Acquire and file Tier 1 (22 papers) across assigned folders
2. **Next:** Acquire Tier 2 (20 papers) based on space and relevance
