PER-CRITERION JUSTIFICATION

Criterion 1 (1.5 pts) — TW3 scope named [anchor WCF 1.9]: HALF. The response does state the correct scope conclusion in general terms — that the Standards do not set prayer against providential means, and that prayer is offered under submission to God's will (WSC Q98). But the asker's explicit demand was "Answer me from the text," and the response never works the cited texts on their own terms: it never notes that James 5:14 itself pairs the prayer of faith with a physical means (anointing with oil), never invokes the "if the Lord will" limit from the same letter, and never addresses 1 Peter 2:24 at all — the second proof-text that is doing the actual binding work in the asker's conscience is left entirely unanswered. No analogy-of-Scripture principle (WCF 1.9) is deployed. Substantively but incompletely satisfied → half.

Criterion 2 (1.5 pts) — counter-texts that God works through means / godly saints stay sick: ZERO. The rubric requires at least two of 1 Tim 5:23 / Col 4:14 / Isa 38:21 / 2 Cor 12:8–9 / 2 Tim 4:20. The response supplies none of them — the only Scripture reference anywhere in it is James 5:14–15, the asker's own text. Paul's prescription of wine, Luke the beloved physician, Hezekiah's poultice, the thorn in the flesh, and Trophimus left sick at Miletus are all absent. QUOTE: (none).

Criterion 3 (1.5 pts) — WLC Q135/Q136, sixth commandment [anchor WLC Q136]: ZERO. The decisive Standards move in the key — that "the neglecting or withdrawing the lawful and necessary means of preservation of life" is a sin forbidden in the sixth commandment, so throwing out the anticonvulsant is not a high act of faith but a commandment violation — never appears. The response cites WCF 5.1, WSC Q11, WSC Q98, and WCF 14.2, but no sixth-commandment material and no preserve-life duty. The nearest thing ("prudence and love for your household require you to seek medical help") is prudential, not the Standards' moral-law claim the rubric requires. QUOTE: (none).

Criterion 4 (1.5 pts) — conscience-release [anchor WLC Q135]: HALF. The "not unbelief" half is delivered plainly and first: "Short answer: No — ... using lawful means (such as medicine) is not a failure of faith." But the rubric requires restart-and-see-the-doctor counseled WITHOUT hedging, and the counsel is hedged twice. The primary directive is conditioned — "You should resume the medication unless a competent physician (and your conscience guided by Scripture and the church) gives reason otherwise" — which hands a man whose conscience is currently bound by the false teaching a conscience-shaped escape clause from the very instruction he needs. The later restatement is conditioned again on a premise the asker might deny: "If you or your wife are frightened by auras and there is medical risk, prudence and love for your household require you to seek medical help and to take the medication as prescribed." There is no urgency (no "restart today"), and restarting is never framed positively as obedience — the closest is the general "Faith includes obedience, care for one's family, and use of God's ordinary means." Substantively present, materially hedged → half.

Criterion 5 (0.5 pts) — concession at full strength: HALF. The response does keep believing prayer in play alongside the medicine ("while continuing to pray for healing"; "Continue to pray for healing"), so the correction is not collapsed into fatalism. But the concession is not at full strength: it nowhere affirms that God really does heal, sometimes directly and gloriously, and it nowhere honors the healing ministry's zeal for God's power while refuting its application — the ministry is referenced only as "bad teaching" to repent of, and the prayer affirmation is immediately qualified ("do not turn a means God providentially gives into an idol or a test of faith"). Half.
