# How Can a Deontological Decision Lead to Moral Behavior? The Moderating Role of Moral Identity

## Authors

[[Zhi Xing Xu|Xu, Zhi Xing]], [[Hing Keung Ma|Ma, Hing Keung]]

## Links


- **URL:** http://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10551-015-2576-6
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-015-2576-6

## Abstract

Deontology and utilitarianism are two competing principles that guide our moral judgment. Recently, deontology is thought to be intuitive and is based on an error-prone and biased approach, whereas utilitarianism is relatively reﬂective and a suitable framework for making decision. In this research, the authors explored the relationship among moral identity, moral decision, and moral behavior to see how a preference for the deontological solution can lead to moral behavior. In study 1, a Webbased survey demonstrated that when making decisions, individuals who viewed themselves as moral people preferred deontological ideals to the utilitarian framework. In study 2, the authors investigated the effect of moral identity and moral decision on moral behavior in an experimental study. The results showed that when deontology was coupled with the motivational power of moral identity, individuals were most likely to behave morally.

## Detailed Summary

This empirical study investigates the relationship between moral identity, ethical predisposition (deontological vs. utilitarian reasoning), and moral behavior. The authors challenge the prevalent view in moral psychology that deontology is merely an intuitive, error-prone heuristic while utilitarianism represents superior rational deliberation. Drawing on the social cognitive perspective of moral identity (Aquino and Reed, 2002), they argue that deontological reasoning, when coupled with strong moral identity, actually leads to more moral behavior in everyday contexts.

The theoretical framework integrates two research traditions: moral identity theory and ethical predisposition research. Moral identity refers to the degree to which moral traits (fairness, honesty, kindness) are central to one's self-concept. The authors argue that individuals with strong moral identity have chronically accessible moral schemas that align naturally with deontological principles (rules, obligations, patterns of behavior) rather than utilitarian cost-benefit calculations. This is because moral identity is organized around character traits that are inherently deontological in nature, making rule-based thinking more readily activated.

Study 1 (N=437) used a web-based survey to test the hypothesis that moral identity positively correlates with preference for deontological solutions. Using a business-scenario vignette about choosing a partner, results confirmed that individuals with strong moral identity preferred deontological ideals over utilitarian frameworks. Study 2 used an experimental design to investigate how moral identity and moral decision interact to predict actual cheating behavior. Results showed a significant interaction: when deontological reasoning was coupled with strong moral identity, individuals were most likely to behave morally (least likely to cheat). This integrated approach demonstrates that deontology is not inherently error-prone but rather works effectively when paired with the motivational power of moral identity.

## Methods Summary

Study 1: Web-based survey (N=437) using the Sojump platform (Chinese participants, 254 men, 183 women, ages 20-60+). Moral identity measured using Aquino and Reed's (2002) internalization subscale. Ethical predisposition measured through a business vignette about choosing a partner (Rex who made 100 chairs all passing quality vs. Jason who made 200 chairs with 60% passing). Participants chose and explained their reasoning, coded as deontological (focus on rules, quality standards) or utilitarian (focus on outcomes, productivity).

Study 2: Experimental design measuring actual cheating behavior. Participants completed tasks with opportunities to cheat for personal gain. Moral identity measured as in Study 1. Moral judgment measured through scenario-based vignettes. The dependent variable was observed cheating behavior. Interaction effects tested using regression analysis.

## Results Summary

Study 1: Significant positive relationship between moral identity and preference for deontological solutions (choosing Rex over Jason). Individuals who viewed themselves as moral people were more likely to cite rule-based reasoning (quality standards, honesty, following proper procedures) rather than outcome-based reasoning (productivity, efficiency) in their explanations.

Study 2: Significant interaction between moral identity and ethical predisposition. Main effects were not significant in isolation. However, when moral identity was high AND participants showed deontological preferences, cheating behavior was lowest. When moral identity was low, ethical predisposition had little effect on behavior. When ethical predisposition was utilitarian, even high moral identity did not strongly predict moral behavior. The interaction demonstrates that deontological reasoning is most effective when coupled with the motivational force of strong moral identity.

## Conclusions

The study challenges the dominant view that deontology is an error-prone moral shortcut. In everyday moral contexts (unlike extreme trolley-type dilemmas), deontological reasoning combined with strong moral identity produces the most moral behavior. The findings support an integrated approach to moral psychology that considers both how people reason about morality (predisposition) and how central morality is to their identity. For practical applications (business ethics, moral education), the results suggest that fostering both strong moral identity and appreciation for rule-based thinking may be more effective than promoting utilitarian calculation alone. The study also implies that AI alignment efforts should consider not just ethical frameworks but also how to embed stable "moral character" in systems.
