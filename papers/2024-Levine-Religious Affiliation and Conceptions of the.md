# Religious Affiliation and Conceptions of the Moral Domain

## Metadata

**Authors:** Levine, Sydney

## Abstract

What is the relationship between religious affiliation and conceptions of the moral domain? Putting aside the question of whether people from different religions agree about how to answer moral questions, here we investigate a more fundamental question: How much disagreement is there across religions about which issues count as moral in the first place? That is, do people from different religions conceptualize the scope of morality differently? Using a new methodology to map out how individuals conceive of the moral domain, we find dramatic differences among adherents of different religions. Mormon and Muslim participants moralized their religious norms, while Jewish participants did not. Hindu participants in our sample did not seem to make a moral/non-moral distinction of the same kind. These results suggest a profound relationship between religious affiliation and conceptions of the scope of the moral domain.

## Detailed Summary

This empirical study investigates how religious affiliation shapes conceptions of the moral domain - specifically, which issues people consider to be moral matters versus non-moral matters. The authors challenge the assumption that all religious people conceptualize morality the same way, a simplification found in both Social Domain Theory (which claims morality is universally limited to harm, justice, and rights) and Moral Foundations Theory (which suggests religion broadens moral scope uniformly). Using a novel "Moral Questionnaire" methodology, the study explores whether adherents of different religions differ in what they consider moral versus merely conventional or prudential.

The research employs hierarchical cluster analysis to identify patterns in how participants categorize various norms as moral or non-moral. Study 1 validates the methodology with philosophers and liberal U.S. adults, successfully identifying the expected moral/non-moral distinction based on harm and rights. Study 2 provides convergent evidence that norms classified as "moral" by the questionnaire also exhibit characteristics traditionally associated with moral norms (eliciting negative third-party judgments, affective reactions like disgust and anger, guilt in transgressors). Study 3 applies the method to observant members of four religious groups (Mormon, Muslim, Jewish, Hindu) plus secular individuals.

The findings reveal dramatic differences: Mormon and Muslim participants moralized their religious norms (treating them as moral matters), while Jewish participants did not moralize religious norms to the same degree. Most strikingly, Hindu participants in the sample did not appear to make a moral/non-moral distinction of the same kind as other groups. These results have significant implications for AI alignment, suggesting that systems designed to respect "moral" concerns must grapple with fundamental disagreements about what counts as moral across different religious and cultural populations.

## Methods Summary

The study developed the "Moral Questionnaire," a theoretically neutral instrument for mapping moral domain conceptions. Participants were presented with 20 norms (drawn from the Atlas of World Cultures and eHRAF Database) and asked two questions for each: (1) whether people in their country should abide by the norm, and (2) whether their judgment was a moral judgment or another kind. Hierarchical cluster analysis using between-groups linkage and squared Euclidean distance was applied to categorize norms into moral and non-moral clusters. Study 3 recruited observant members of religious groups through targeted online communities (e.g., LDS forums, Islamic centers, Jewish organizations, Hindu temples), with total sample sizes of 199-690 participants per study.

## Results Summary

For philosophers and liberal U.S. adults, norms related to unjustified harm and infringement on freedom clustered together and were rated as moral, while norms about prudence, authority, and ways of life clustered separately as non-moral. This validated the methodology. In the cross-religious comparison, Mormon and Muslim participants moralized religious norms significantly more than secular participants. Jewish participants showed similar patterns to secular Americans, not moralizing religious norms. Hindu participants presented the most distinctive pattern - their responses did not clearly separate into moral/non-moral clusters, suggesting a fundamentally different conceptual structure. The findings challenge both SDT's claim of universal moral boundaries and MFT's treatment of religious populations as homogeneous.

## Conclusions

The study demonstrates profound relationships between religious affiliation and conceptions of the moral domain's scope. Different religious groups do not merely disagree about moral answers - they disagree about which questions are moral in the first place. This has important implications for AI systems designed to align with human values, as there is no universal agreement on what constitutes the moral domain. The authors call for revising theories of moral psychology to accommodate this religious diversity and for more research on how different religious traditions structure moral cognition.
