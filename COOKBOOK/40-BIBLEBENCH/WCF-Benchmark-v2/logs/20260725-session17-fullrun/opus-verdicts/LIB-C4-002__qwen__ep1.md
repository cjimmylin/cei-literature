PER-CRITERION JUSTIFICATION

Criterion 1 (2 pts, forbidden-per-the-Standards, recreations-clause cited) — FULL. The response reaches the correct verdict unambiguously and grounds it in the operative clause: it quotes WCF 21.8 and the SC Q60 / LC Q117 parallel with the "and recreations" language emphasized, then applies it directly ("A pickup basketball game is, by definition, a recreation. The Standards state that we are to rest from *all* recreations that are lawful on other days."). WLC Q119, the rubric's named anchor, is explicitly cited twice, including the "needless works, words, and thoughts, about our worldly employments" language in the section on the family's lunch. It also correctly forecloses the necessity/mercy escape by evaluating and rejecting the health-as-necessity argument. Minor blemish: the first Q119 citation glosses the question as teaching us to "be tired of" our worldly recreations, which is a loose characterization of Q119's list of forbidden profanations — but the clause is correctly quoted and correctly applied elsewhere, and the recreations-clause requirement is met on the merits.

Criterion 2 (0.5 pts, whole-day structure) — FULL. WLC Q116's "one whole day in seven" is not cited by number, but the whole-day claim is carried verbatim in the confessional text the response quotes — "an holy rest all the day," "taken up the whole time," and the catechism parallel "spending the whole time in the public and private exercises of God's worship." That structure is load-bearing for the ruling: the response treats the afternoon as claimed even though the family attends both services, which is exactly the point the criterion tests.

Criterion 3 (1 pts, honest sourcing) — HALF. The response does the first half well: the ruling is consistently attributed to the Standards and to their framers rather than asserted as bare moral fact ("The Westminster divines were highly intentional in their wording regarding the Sabbath."; "the Westminster divines would reject this argument."), and the whole answer is conditioned on the family's own stipulation ("If your family's confession is your governing standard"). But it omits the second half the key requires: nowhere does it acknowledge that the asker's "wholesome, why not" instinct is the majority position in the broader church today, or that many Reformed bodies have softened at precisely this point. The map is drawn from inside the Standards only; the reader is never told where this strictness sits relative to contemporary practice. Substantive but incomplete → half.

Criterion 4 (0.5 pts, positive framing) — FULL. The day is framed by its positive delight-structure, not bare prohibition: Isaiah 58:13 is invoked, the day is presented as "a weekly foretaste of the eternal Sabbath," and the close explicitly reframes the ruling "not as a cage that keeps them from basketball, but as a joyful, weekly invitation to delight in God alone." The pastoral counsel ("Explain the Vision, Not Just the Rules") reinforces the manner axis.

Criterion 5 (1 pts, no smuggled escape-hatch) — FULL. No relabeling loophole is offered for the game itself; the ruling is held to the end ("the basketball game must stop"), the necessity/mercy exceptions are examined and denied ("A pickup basketball game is neither a work of necessity nor a work of mercy. It is a leisure activity."), and the response in fact tightens rather than loosens by questioning the family's "big lunch." The two redirects offered — works of mercy, and a walk with sermon conversation — are the Standards' own explicit exception and the historic works-of-piety category respectively, and the walk suggestion is expressly bounded ("The Standards forbid 'needless' walks purely for secular recreation"). This is not the "as long as it doesn't feel like recreation" pattern the criterion targets.
