Missing: Habib (2023) on Maqasid and AI, which was in the BACKGROUND folder and discussed in D-01 but does not appear in the draft. Given the section's focus on Maqasid, this is a notable omission.

The attribution to "the Quranic QA shared task ecosystem" and "Arabic NLP infrastructure" correctly locates the dominance in institutional history rather than theological properties. However, the sheer word count for Islamic ethics (Section 2.2b, approximately 280 words) versus "Other Abrahamic" (2.2c, approximately 260 words combined for Christianity and Judaism) and "Dharmic, East Asian, and Other" (2.2d, approximately 220 words for five or more traditions) creates a de facto attention asymmetry. (2) No mention of Islamic feminist ethics approaches to AI (e.g., Wadud, Barlas) that complicate the Maqasid framework from within.

**Specific revision recommendations:**
1. Expand the maslaha parenthetical to read "maslaha (public interest as constrained by the objectives of Islamic law)" or similar, to avoid collapsing it into secular utilitarianism.
2. Add a sentence noting that modern Maqasid scholarship expands beyond the classical five objectives, to avoid presenting the framework as frozen.
3. Add Habib (2023) citation alongside Kamali (2008) in the Maqasid discussion.

Halakhic computational formalization (Abraham, Koppel) is primarily an Orthodox enterprise; Liberal Jewish ethics would look quite different in benchmark form.

**Citation adequacy:** Abraham (2019), Rosenfeld (2021), Steinberg (2025) all match the spine.

### Expert 11 -- Dr. Mohamed (Decolonial & Critical Studies)
**Sections reviewed:** Intro P3 (structural epistemic injustice); Section 2.5 (cross-cutting review)

**Overall assessment:** GOOD -- with an important terminological concern

**Factual accuracy:** The reference to my own work (Mohamed et al. 2020) is accurate: the concept of "algorithmic coloniality" and the reproduction of epistemic hierarchies through technical systems is correctly described. The specific claim that "when benchmark availability correlates with proximity to Western academic publishing infrastructure, the resulting gaps are not neutral absences but instances of what Mohamed et al. term 'algorithmic coloniality'" is a defensible application of the framework.

**Anti-essentialization:** STRONG in Section 2.5. The section carefully avoids monolithic characterizations and names structural causes rather than attributing agency to traditions.

**Critical terminological concern:** The draft uses the phrase "structural epistemic injustice" in Intro P3 and Section 2.5, citing Mohamed (2020) and Bilimoria (2013). The term "epistemic injustice" has a specific philosophical lineage originating with Miranda Fricker (2007), who distinguishes two forms: testimonial injustice (a speaker's credibility is deflated due to identity prejudice) and hermeneutical injustice (a gap in collective interpretive resources disadvantages certain social groups). The draft's usage aligns more closely with hermeneutical injustice -- the absence of interpretive frameworks (benchmarks) for certain traditions' knowledge. However, the draft does not cite Fricker, and the phrase "structural epistemic injustice" is not Fricker's terminology but an extension of it. This is legitimate but should be acknowledged. The draft should either:
   (a) cite Fricker (2007) as the origin of the "epistemic injustice" concept and explain how the paper extends it to benchmark infrastructure, or
   (b) use a different term, such as "epistemic exclusion" or "infrastructure-mediated epistemic marginalization," that does not invoke Fricker's specific framework without citing it.

The stronger concern is whether "structural epistemic injustice" is the right framing at all. The benchmark gaps are real, but characterizing them as "injustice" presupposes that benchmark inclusion is a good that traditions are owed. Some Indigenous communities actively resist inclusion in computational evaluation systems. The draft partially addresses this through the CARE Principles discussion, but the "injustice" framing in Intro P3 presupposes that all traditions should want to be benchmarked -- which itself may be a form of epistemic imperialism.

**Citation adequacy:** Mohamed (2020), Birhane (2021), Carroll (2020), Bilimoria (2013), Henrich (2010) -- all match spine.

Missing: (1) Fricker (2007) on epistemic injustice -- the foundational reference for the term the paper deploys. (2) Dotson (2014) on "conceptual competence injustice" -- directly relevant to the claim that benchmarks lack the conceptual resources to evaluate certain traditions. (3) Harding (1992, 2015) on standpoint epistemology -- relevant to the argument about whose perspective shapes evaluation design.

**Framing fairness:** The decolonial framing is applied consistently and with appropriate nuance. Section 2.5's acknowledgment that "data sovereignty may require that knowledge systems not be made into public benchmarks" is important and well-placed.

**Missing perspectives:**
1. The tension between the paper's "injustice" framing (traditions should be included) and the data sovereignty position (some traditions may not want to be included). The draft handles this in Section 2.5 but not in Intro P3, where the framing is more unambiguous.
2. The possibility that creating benchmarks for oral/communal traditions requires not just "different approaches" but fundamentally different evaluation paradigms -- perhaps not benchmarks at all, but community-governed evaluation processes.

**Specific revision recommendations:**
1. Add a Fricker (2007) citation when introducing "epistemic injustice" and briefly note the extension from interpersonal to infrastructural forms.
2. In Intro P3, add a qualifying sentence noting that the "injustice" framing assumes benchmark inclusion is desirable, while acknowledging that some communities may exercise epistemic sovereignty by declining inclusion.
3. Consider whether "structural epistemic exclusion" is a more precise term than "structural epistemic injustice," given that the latter invokes a specific philosophical framework (Fricker) that the paper does not fully engage.

## Minority Reports

### Minority Report -- Expert 3 (Prof. Bilimoria)

I register a structured dissent on the draft's allocation of scholarly depth across traditions.

The draft devotes 280 words of substantive engagement to Islamic AI ethics (Section 2.2b), naming five scholars, three benchmark artifacts, and a shared task ecosystem. Dharmic traditions -- Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism, representing approximately 1.7 billion adherents -- receive approximately 120 words in Section 2.2d, naming five scholars but engaging none of their arguments at the depth afforded to the Islamic section. East Asian traditions (Confucianism, Taoism, Shinto, representing the philosophical heritage of approximately 1.5 billion people) receive approximately 80 words.

I understand the structural reason: Islamic AI ethics has produced more benchmark artifacts, so there is more to cite. But a literature review's purpose is not only to catalogue what exists; it is to map the conceptual landscape, including the landscape of what does not yet exist but should. The draft maps the Islamic landscape in detail and then gestures at the Dharmic and East Asian landscapes from a distance. A paper that critiques tradition asymmetry should not reproduce it in its own structure.

I recommend that Section 2.2d receive at least 350 words (adding approximately 130 words), with substantive engagement of at least one philosophical argument from each tradition family -- not merely listing scholars and their titles. Specifically, the visesa-dharma concept (Chadha 2023), the wu wei framework (Li 2022), and the ahimsa-AI connection deserve the same level of intellectual engagement that the Maqasid al-Shariah receives.

### Minority Report -- Expert 11 (Dr. Mohamed)

I register a concern about the draft's relationship to its own decolonial framing.

The draft makes a strong case for structural epistemic injustice in Section 2.5. It correctly identifies the pipeline requirements (textual corpora, formalized knowledge, open data licenses) that exclude oral and communal traditions. However, the draft then proposes to address this through "fundamentally different approaches to benchmark construction" (Intro P4, Section 2.5 conclusion). This formulation assumes that the solution to benchmark exclusion is better benchmarks. It does not consider the possibility that benchmarking itself -- as a mode of evaluation -- may be constitutively incompatible with some knowledge systems.

A truly decolonial approach would ask: are there forms of AI evaluation that do not require benchmarks at all? Community-governed evaluation processes, participatory assessment, relational accountability frameworks -- these are not "different kinds of benchmarks" but alternatives to benchmarking. The draft's framing remains within the benchmark paradigm even as it critiques that paradigm's exclusions. I recommend that Intro P4 or Section 2.6 explicitly acknowledge this tension and name post-benchmark evaluation as an open research direction.
