# The Sermon on the Mount in the Gospels

## Canonical scope and interpretive stance

Within the canonical Gospels, the Sermon on the Mount is preserved most fully in **Matthew 5–7**, where it functions as the **first of Matthew’s five major discourses** and a central presentation of Jesus’ kingdom ethic. Matthew’s own notes describe it as the first discourse in a deliberate five-discourse structure, while scholarly summaries describe it as a three-chapter presentation of Jesus’ moral teaching. Luke preserves a shorter but clearly related version in **Luke 6:20–49**, commonly called the **Sermon on the Plain**, with many close thematic and verbal parallels. citeturn19search0turn19search3turn19search1turn9view9

A responsible Christian reading should hold together two truths at once. First, these passages are **historically rooted teachings of Jesus** addressed to disciples in Galilee. Second, the Evangelists have **shaped and arranged** the material for theological purposes. Calvin argued that Matthew and Luke describe the **same discourse in summarized form**, while many modern interpreters see either a **shared body of Jesus tradition** shaped differently by each Gospel writer or similar teaching delivered on more than one occasion. For benchmark-building, that means the interpreter should not flatten Matthew into Luke or Luke into Matthew; each Gospel contributes distinct emphasis without ceasing to witness to the same Lord. citeturn9view8turn9view9turn19search3

## Original-language and historical-exegetical background

Matthew presents Jesus ascending a mountain, sitting to teach, and addressing disciples while the crowds listen in. That literary framing strongly supports a **new-Moses** reading: Matthew’s Gospel is structured around five discourses, and modern interpreters note that Matthew presents Jesus as the greater teacher who fulfills the Torah rather than abolishing it. The Sermon therefore should be read not as detached moral aphorisms but as the covenantal instruction of the Messiah for the renewed people of God. Luke, by contrast, places the sermon after Jesus’ all-night prayer, the choosing of the twelve, and a descent to a “level place,” which gives his version a communal and socially concrete setting centered on discipleship in the midst of needy crowds. citeturn23search4turn23search3turn19search3turn9view9

The Beatitudes begin with the Greek adjective **μακάριοι** (*makarioi*), a term that carries the sense of being blessed, favored, or in a state of enviable well-being before God. Matthew says, “Blessed are the **poor in spirit**,” while Luke says, “Blessed are **you poor**.” Matthew’s wording pushes toward humility and dependence before God; Luke’s direct second-person address, combined with his matching woes on the rich, full, laughing, and well-spoken-of, gives the saying sharper social force. The Greek noun **πτωχός** (*ptōchos*) denotes genuine poverty, even beggary or destitution, so Matthew is not replacing material poverty with a vague inward feeling; rather, he intensifies the saying by focusing on the God-dependent heart, while Luke foregrounds the lived social condition of the disadvantaged disciple. citeturn24search4turn22search10turn13search5turn21search11turn35search2turn34search6

That same pattern appears in the hunger sayings. Matthew blesses those who hunger and thirst for **δικαιοσύνη** (*dikaiosynē*), while Luke blesses those who are hungry now. In Matthew, *dikaiosynē* carries the broad sense of righteousness, justice, rightness of life, and conformity to God’s will. Bible Odyssey’s discussion of the sermon notes that “justice-righteousness” is a central Matthean theme, and Matthew 5:20 makes clear that the sermon’s burden is a righteousness that surpasses the scribes and Pharisees. Luke’s version keeps the hope of reversal vivid: the hungry will be filled, and his Gospel consistently stresses mercy for the poor and the great reversal of human status. citeturn24search3turn24search0turn35search3turn35search23turn8search15turn19search9turn19search18

Matthew 5:17 is the hinge for the whole sermon. Jesus says he did not come to abolish the Law or the Prophets but to **fulfill** them; the verb **πληρόω** (*plēroō*) carries the sense of filling up, bringing to completion, or bringing to full expression. The sermon’s so-called antitheses therefore do not cancel Torah; they press through external compliance to the heart-level intention of God’s will. Thus murder is traced back to anger and contempt, adultery to lustful intention, and neighbor-love is extended to enemy-love and prayer for persecutors. Matthew’s closing imperative in this unit, “be **τέλειοι** (*teleioi*) as your heavenly Father is τέλειος,” is best read in the sense of wholeness, completeness, or moral maturity rather than simplistic flawlessness. Luke’s parallel climax in 6:36 gives an interpretive gloss: “Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.” Matthew’s “perfect” and Luke’s “merciful” together show that kingdom obedience is a whole-person likeness to the Father, especially in enemy-love. citeturn11search0turn22search1turn34search2turn34search5turn36search9turn21search2turn11search1turn35search8turn32search11turn35search17

Matthew 6 shifts from external relations to devotional practice. Jesus warns against practicing righteousness “before men” to be seen, then addresses almsgiving, prayer, and fasting as common forms of Jewish piety. The point is not withdrawal from obedience but purification of motive before “your Father.” The Lord’s Prayer stands at the center of this section: God’s name, reign, and will come first; daily dependence, forgiveness, and deliverance follow. Matthew then turns to treasure, loyalty, and anxiety. “Mammon” is portrayed as a rival power competing with service to God, and the repeated command not to worry culminates in “seek first the kingdom … and his righteousness.” This is not a charter for passivity but for reordered allegiance and trust. citeturn11search2turn24search10turn11search6turn21search0turn21search1turn13search8turn12search0

Matthew 7 begins with “Do not judge,” using the verb **κρίνω** (*krinō*), whose semantic range includes distinguishing, deciding, judging, and condemning. In context, Jesus is forbidding hypocritical, self-righteous condemnation, not abolishing all moral discernment; the sermon itself later commands discernment about “dogs,” “pigs,” and “false prophets.” The concluding images of the narrow gate, true and false disciples, and the two builders show that the sermon is finally about **obedient hearing**. The wise builder is not the one who merely admires Jesus’ ethic, but the one who **does** his words. citeturn12search1turn34search10turn32search1turn32search0turn32search4turn12search3turn22search2

## Scripture interpreting Scripture

The sermon is saturated with Old Testament echoes. “The meek shall inherit the earth” clearly echoes **Psalm 37:11**, and Matthew’s “hungering and thirsting for righteousness” belongs in the orbit of Israel’s longing for God’s just reign. The blessing of the poor also resonates with **Isaiah 61:1**, where the anointed one brings good news to the poor. Luke’s version is especially illuminated by Luke’s own infancy material: Mary sings that God has **exalted the humble**, **filled the hungry with good things**, and **sent the rich away empty**, supplying a programmatic backdrop for Luke 6. citeturn17search0turn17search1turn31search0turn31search1

Jesus’ teaching on enemy-love and non-retaliation does not appear ex nihilo. It radicalizes and extends the scriptural trajectory already present in **Leviticus 19:18** and **Proverbs 25:21**, later echoed by **Romans 12:20**. Likewise, Matthew’s Golden Rule in **7:12** is explicitly said to summarize “the Law and the Prophets,” functioning as a practical hermeneutical key for relational ethics. This is why the sermon should be read as fulfillment, not contradiction: Jesus gathers the law’s moral trajectory into a climactic kingdom form. citeturn17search2turn17search3turn18search0turn24search1turn11search0

The sermon’s devotional and moral logic also resonates with later New Testament teaching. Jesus’ commands about mercy align closely with **James 2:13**, where mercy triumphs over judgment. His summons away from anxious self-provision toward prayerful dependence anticipates **Philippians 4:6–7** and the wisdom-petition of **James 1:5**. The sermon’s intense ethical vision is therefore not isolated from apostolic teaching; it becomes one of the New Testament’s foundational forms for Christian wisdom, prayer, and communal life. citeturn18search2turn18search1turn18search3

## Historical theology and the history of interpretation

In patristic interpretation, **Augustine** treated the Sermon on the Mount as nothing less than a “perfect standard of the Christian life.” He read the Beatitudes as a deliberate ascent in spiritual formation and even correlated them with a sevenfold progression of the Spirit’s work. That approach is important because it sees the sermon not as scattered rules but as an ordered path of sanctification that begins in humility and culminates in peace, purity, and endurance under persecution. citeturn27view3turn27view0turn27view2

**Chrysostom** emphasized the setting and ethos of the sermon. For him, Jesus’ retreat from city and public square to the mountain modeled instruction free from ostentation and rooted in serious discipleship. The sermon is therefore not merely content but also pedagogy: wisdom is taught in a way that rejects display. In the medieval period, **Aquinas**, gathering patristic voices in the *Catena Aurea*, continued to read the mountain as theological symbol and as the place of heavenly instruction, while preserving the strong moral and ecclesial reading inherited from the Fathers. citeturn9view7turn26view0

The Reformation did not reduce the sermon’s importance; it intensified debate over how it should govern Christian life. **Calvin** held that Matthew and Luke give a summary of Christ’s doctrine, probably of the same discourse, and he resisted over-allegorizing the mountain while still treating the sermon as a concise presentation of Jesus’ moral teaching. **Luther** heard the opening Beatitudes not as threatening legal demands but as a “sweet” and inviting beginning for disciples. At the same time, he insisted that “poor in spirit” is not simply bodily poverty but a heart posture before God. Later Lutheran interpretation developed the **two-kingdoms** distinction, reading the sermon as binding Christian persons while distinguishing that from the coercive tasks of civil order; Pennington’s survey rightly notes that Luther’s reading became a major turning point in Protestant interpretation. citeturn9view8turn29view0turn25view1

The wider history of interpretation repeatedly returns to the same tension: Is the sermon an impossible ideal, a monastic counsel for the especially devout, a literal social program, or the normative ethic of all disciples? McKnight’s summary of church history is useful here: many readers softened the sermon by turning it into a private morality, an elite ethic, or an impossible law whose main purpose is to condemn. The persistence of that debate is itself historically significant. The sermon has functioned as a diagnostic text for theological commitments because it exposes what a tradition really believes about law, grace, discipleship, society, holiness, violence, wealth, and sanctification. citeturn26view2turn25view1

## Modern commentary synthesis

A strong modern synthesis comes from **Scot McKnight**, who insists that the sermon's commands must be allowed to stand in their “rhetorical ruggedness.” On his reading, Matthew presents Jesus as the **new Moses** giving the moral vision for God’s renewed people, and the sermon makes a total claim on the disciple rather than offering optional spiritual advice. That is especially useful for Christian AI-benchmark work, because it resists reducing the text to either abstract ideals or a checklist of isolated behaviors. citeturn26view2turn9view10

**Jonathan Pennington** argues that the Sermon on the Mount is best read as a form of **wisdom exhortation** that summons hearers into **true human flourishing** centered on God and his coming kingdom. In his view, “righteousness” in Matthew 5:20 becomes the thread that helps the parts cohere: the sermon teaches people to see the world rightly and to inhabit it in a way aligned with God’s reign. That framework is especially helpful when moving from exegesis to application, because it shows that the sermon aims at the formation of a certain kind of person, not merely correct answers to isolated cases. citeturn25view1turn14search14

**Craig Blomberg** offers a complementary contribution by reading the sermon through **inaugurated eschatology**. On that view, Jesus addresses disciples already committed in some way to following him; the sermon is neither a utopian social program for godless society nor an ethic reserved for spiritual elites. Its demands are real and corporate, but they are lived in the tension of the already and the not yet, so interpreters must avoid both **triumphalism** and **defeatism**. Blomberg’s practical method for applying Old Testament law through Christ to today is also valuable: move from the original command, to the underlying theological principle, to fulfillment in Christ, to the corresponding New Testament principle, and only then to present-day application. citeturn30view0turn30view1

## Ethical guidance for modern life and AI benchmark design

For Christian moral reasoning, the sermon is best understood as a **formation text** before it is a mere **decision tree**. It shapes the moral agent’s loves, loyalties, perceptions, and habits. The first benchmark domain, therefore, should be **humility and truthful self-assessment**. Matthew’s “poor in spirit,” Luke’s blessing of the poor, the warning about speck-and-log judgment, and the Father-centered piety of Matthew 6 all require an agent to resist vanity, self-justification, and performative righteousness. A well-aligned system should show epistemic humility, confess limits, resist self-display, and prefer truth before status. citeturn24search4turn22search10turn24search8turn11search2

A second domain is **mercy with discernment**. The sermon forbids condemning hypocrisy, commands enemy-love, and calls disciples to imitate the Father’s mercy. Yet it also requires discernment about false prophets, corrupt fruit, and what is holy. So a Christian benchmark should not reward indiscriminate niceness. It should test whether an agent can combine compassion, patience, and non-retaliation with sober moral discernment and willingness to name evil truthfully. That balance mirrors Luke’s “be merciful” and Matthew’s warnings about wolves and false disciples. citeturn32search11turn32search3turn21search2turn32search0turn12search7

A third domain is **integrity of heart, speech, and motive**. Jesus repeatedly moves beneath the surface act to the interior source: anger before murder, lust before adultery, hidden motive behind almsgiving, truthful speech instead of oath manipulation, and singular loyalty instead of double service to God and mammon. Benchmarks should therefore include multi-turn scenarios in which a system can succeed externally while failing internally—through reputation management, selective truthfulness, manipulative compliance, or virtue-signaling. From the sermon’s standpoint, those are not edge cases; they are the heart of the matter. citeturn36search9turn11search2turn21search16turn21search0turn21search1

A fourth domain is **trust, anxiety, and material allegiance**. Matthew 6 is not indifferent to food, clothing, or material need, but it does demand that concern for provision be subordinated to kingdom loyalty. Luke’s woes warn that wealth and social approval can become spiritually distorting. For AI evaluation, this means designing tasks in which efficiency, profit, prestige, or public approval compete with truth, mercy, chastity, reconciliation, or faithfulness. A Christianly aligned agent should not maximize outcome metrics at the expense of kingdom priorities. citeturn12search0turn13search8turn21search0turn13search5turn21search11

A fifth domain is **obedient action in concrete relationships**. The sermon culminates not in admiration but in the image of the house built on rock by the one who hears and does. For benchmarks, that means scenarios should be **agentic** and **multi-step**, not merely classificatory. The test should examine whether the agent will pursue reconciliation before religious performance, forgive in ongoing conflict, tell the truth when oaths or legalisms could create room for evasion, and continue loving enemies when the interaction becomes costly. Theologically, the target is not bare rule retrieval but stable, enacted discipleship under pressure. citeturn12search3turn22search2turn36search9turn21search2turn18search0

In short, the Sermon on the Mount offers a powerful Christian basis for AI moral benchmarking because it tests not only **what** one decides but **who** one is becoming. Matthew stresses righteousness, wholeness, and fulfillment of the Law in Jesus; Luke stresses mercy, reversal, and the Father’s compassion for the poor and vulnerable. Read together, the gospel versions yield a benchmark framework aimed at humility, purity of motive, truthful speech, mercy toward adversaries, freedom from greed and anxious self-protection, and obedient enactment of Jesus’ words in public and private life. citeturn24search0turn11search0turn11search1turn32search11turn9view9turn30view0turn25view1
