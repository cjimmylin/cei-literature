# 7. Discussion

The results of this study converge on a diagnosis that is at once more precise and more disquieting than the "double bind" identified in prior work. The moral psychology benchmark landscape for large language models does not merely suffer from two concurrent weaknesses---theoretical narrowness and empirical undiscrimination---that happen to coexist in the same domain. It suffers from a triple bind in which three failures compound multiplicatively: the landscape is theoretically narrow (dominated by one theory at 73% quality while four non-Western traditions achieve 0%), empirically undiscriminating (43% of benchmarks produce zero cross-model variation across nine models from three vendors), and culturally blind (32 non-Western candidates collectively fail to produce a single publication-ready instrument). Parts I through III established these findings empirically and demonstrated their structural interconnection through the infrastructure-quality-discrimination pipeline. This section interprets their consequences.

The interpretive task is substantial because the triple bind is not merely a measurement problem. It is an epistemic problem: the field's evaluative infrastructure shapes what it can know about LLM moral cognition, and the shape of that infrastructure---narrow, homogeneous, culturally parochial---determines that the field can know only a narrow, homogeneous, culturally parochial version of the truth. The instruments define the questions that can be asked, and the questions define the answers that can be found. When the instruments are drawn overwhelmingly from one theory, calibrated on one cultural population, and unable to distinguish between the systems they evaluate, the resulting "knowledge" about LLM moral cognition is knowledge about a specific intersection of training pipeline artifacts and Western measurement conventions, not knowledge about the systems' moral capabilities in any broader sense.

This section develops that interpretive argument through seven subsections. Section 7.1 presents the triple bind as a compounding system, showing how each failure amplifies the others. Section 7.2 traces the causal mechanism of MFT's dominance to infrastructure determinism, arguing that the quality gap is not effort-driven but infrastructure-driven. Section 7.3 develops the temporal stagnation thesis, showing that the proliferation of benchmarks creates a quality illusion---the appearance of progress without its substance. Section 7.4 interprets the six universal RLHF signatures, weighing the universal values hypothesis against the shared training artifact hypothesis. Section 7.5 develops the governance implications of the cultural quality cliff for global AI deployment. Section 7.6 offers seven concrete recommendations for AI governance and policy. Section 7.7 catalogues the study's limitations with the specificity they require.


## 7.1 The Triple Bind: How Three Failures Compound

The central interpretive claim of this paper is that theoretical narrowness, empirical undiscrimination, and cultural blindness are not three problems requiring three solutions. They are one self-reinforcing system observed at three stages of its operation, and the compounding effects of their interaction produce consequences substantially worse than the sum of their individual impacts.

### 7.1.1 Bind 1 as a Definitional Problem

When 73% of MFT benchmark candidates achieve publication-ready quality while 0% of Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics candidates do, the landscape does not merely favor MFT over alternative theories. It effectively defines what counts as morality in the context of LLM evaluation. The definition is not explicit---no researcher has argued that morality is coextensive with the five moral foundations of Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity. But the definition is operative: when a governance body asks whether an LLM is "morally aligned," the instruments available to answer that question are overwhelmingly instruments that measure MFT-adjacent constructs, and the answer will therefore be an answer about MFT-adjacent moral cognition. The question "is this model morally aligned?" becomes, in practice, the question "does this model endorse Care and Fairness over Sanctity and Authority in a pattern consistent with WEIRD liberal populations?" The definitional collapse is invisible to users of the instruments because the instruments do not announce the theoretical tradition they instantiate; they present themselves as measuring "morality" or "moral reasoning" or "ethical alignment" without qualification.

The consequences of this definitional collapse extend beyond the academy. When the MFQ-30 returns an Individualizing/Binding ratio of 2.83 for Claude Opus, the number is psychologically interpretable only within the MFT framework: it means that Opus endorses Care and Fairness substantially more than Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity, placing it in the "liberal" zone of the MFT political space. But this characterization says nothing about whether Opus engages in virtuous deliberation (Aristotelian ethics), whether it respects relational obligations graded by intimacy and role (Confucian ethics), whether it treats the suffering of others as constitutive of its own diminishment (Ubuntu ethics), or whether it recognizes sacred boundaries that utilitarian calculation cannot transgress (religious ethics across traditions). The MFQ-30 cannot measure these dimensions because it was not designed to measure them, and no instrument of comparable quality exists for these alternative frameworks. The landscape's theoretical narrowness is therefore not merely an academic imbalance; it is a constraint on what the field can see. Morality, as evaluated by the benchmark landscape, is morality as MFT defines it.

The severity of this definitional collapse is best appreciated by imagining its analogue in another domain. If the benchmark landscape for measuring LLM language comprehension consisted almost exclusively of instruments measuring syntactic parsing, with 73% of syntax benchmarks achieving publication-ready quality and 0% of pragmatics, discourse, or sociolinguistic benchmarks doing so, the field would immediately recognize that its evaluative infrastructure was measuring one dimension of language and calling it "language comprehension." The moral psychology case is structurally identical, but the recognition has been slower because moral psychology's theoretical pluralism is less well-known outside the discipline than linguistics' subdisciplinary structure.

### 7.1.2 Bind 2 as a Genuine Property of the Systems

The empirical undiscrimination documented in Part II admits of two broad interpretations. The first is that the convergence is a measurement artifact: the instruments are insufficiently sensitive, and better instruments would reveal differences. The second is that the convergence is a genuine property of the systems: RLHF-aligned models share a single moral profile because they have been trained on similar data by similar methods, and no instrument---however sensitive---would reveal differences on the dimensions these instruments measure.

The tier-stratified analysis in Section 6.1 provides strong evidence for the second interpretation. The correlation between quality and discrimination is r = -0.83: higher-quality benchmarks produce *less* variation between models, not more. The instruments with the strongest construct validity, the most comprehensive human baselines, and the most rigorous psychometric properties---the MFQ-30, the Moral Machine, TracingMF---are the instruments that most emphatically confirm convergence. If the homogeneity were a measurement failure, the best instruments should detect what the worst instruments miss. Instead, the best instruments confirm what the worst instruments suggest: the models are morally indistinguishable on content-level measures.

This finding has a counterintuitive but important corollary. The homogeneity is not something the field needs to solve by building better versions of existing instruments. A more precise MFQ will not break the convergence; it will confirm it with tighter confidence intervals. A more comprehensive trolley battery will not reveal vendor-specific moral differences; the Moral Machine, with 40 million human judgments as its normative base, already returns CV = 0.000 across all nine models. The measurement problem is not sensitivity; it is dimensionality. The instruments measure content-level moral endorsement---what values the model affirms---and on this dimension, the models are genuinely identical because they have been trained to affirm the same values. The dimension on which the models differ is process-level moral cognition---how they arrive at moral judgments, how their judgments respond to processing manipulations, how rigidly their moral commitments resist contextual perturbation---and this dimension is the one the landscape has not built instruments to measure.

The dual-process probe provides the decisive evidence. Its CV of 3.00 is 93 times larger than the mean CV of Top-tier benchmarks. It achieved this discrimination not because it is a better instrument in the traditional psychometric sense---it lacks standardized scoring, published norms, and multi-study validation---but because it probes a dimension that RLHF training does not homogenize. The chain-of-thought effect is an architectural property, not a content-level property, and architectural properties vary across models even when content-level properties do not. The benchmark that the landscape has not invested in---the process-level probe---is the benchmark that breaks the convergence. The benchmarks the landscape has invested in---the content-level instruments with decades of psychometric validation---are the benchmarks that confirm it.

### 7.1.3 Bind 3 as a Structural Exclusion

When 32 non-Western benchmark candidates exist but none meets quality standards, the problem is not the absence of scholarly effort. Researchers have built Ubuntu-inspired evaluation frameworks (UbuntuGuard, composite 35.1), Confucian ethics datasets ("AI Trained on the Confucian Analects," composite 21.2), and Indigenous moral reasoning probes. The 32 candidates represent genuine intellectual labor directed toward broadening moral evaluation beyond Western frameworks. The problem is that this effort has been structurally unable to produce high-quality instruments because the prerequisite infrastructure---validated questionnaires, annotated corpora, population norms---does not exist for these traditions.

The structural nature of the exclusion deserves emphasis because it means the cultural quality cliff cannot be closed through increased effort alone. A researcher who secures funding, assembles a team, and dedicates three years to building an Ubuntu ethics benchmark will still confront the absence of a validated Ubuntu Moral Assessment Questionnaire, the absence of an annotated Ubuntu Ethics Corpus, and the absence of population-level norms for Ubuntu moral endorsement across sub-Saharan African populations. Without these foundational resources, the benchmark will score low on A4 (validated source instrument), A5 (human baselines), and A6 (construct validity), landing it in the medium or low tier regardless of the quality of the researcher's intellectual engagement with Ubuntu philosophy. The quality cliff is not a gap that effort can bridge; it is a gap that requires prior infrastructure investment before effort becomes productive.

This structural character distinguishes the cultural quality cliff from other kinds of scholarly underrepresentation. In many fields, the underrepresentation of non-Western perspectives reflects differential attention: Western researchers simply have not engaged with non-Western scholarship, and the remedy is engagement. In the moral psychology benchmark landscape, the underrepresentation reflects differential infrastructure: Western moral psychology has accumulated decades of psychometric investment---questionnaires validated across populations, corpora annotated by trained coders, norms collected from hundreds of thousands of respondents---and non-Western moral philosophy has accumulated centuries of philosophical insight without the specific kind of computational infrastructure that benchmark construction requires. The philosophical depth of Ubuntu, Confucian, and Indigenous moral thought is not in question; what is in question is whether that depth has been translated into the specific institutional forms---standardized instruments, annotated datasets, population norms---that the benchmark development pipeline rewards.

### 7.1.4 The Compounding Dynamics

The three binds do not merely coexist. Each reinforces the others through four causal pathways that, together, form a closed feedback loop.

*Narrowness produces convergence.* A landscape dominated by content-level MFT instruments will return convergent results when applied to models trained on similar data. This is the infrastructure-quality-discrimination pipeline documented in Section 6.2: MFT's infrastructure produces high-quality benchmarks, high-quality benchmarks enter trial batteries, and these batteries measure content-level properties on which RLHF-aligned models are identical. The narrowness of the theoretical frame determines the narrowness of the empirical findings.

*Convergence reduces the incentive for diversity.* When the MFQ-30 produces consistent, interpretable scores across all nine models, the instrument "works" by the standards of benchmark development: it generates data, fills evaluation checklists, and produces publishable results. The data happens to be uninformative for cross-model comparison, but this uninformativeness is invisible unless one explicitly compares across models---a practice that the field's publication norms do not reward. The convergence finding, paradoxically, sustains the conditions that produce it by eliminating the empirical motivation for building instruments that might reveal divergence. Why build a Dual Process Morality Questionnaire when the MFQ-30 already produces clear, interpretable numbers?

*Cultural exclusion reinforces narrowness.* Non-Western moral traditions often emphasize moral constructs---relational selfhood, communal agency, virtue cultivation through practice, sacred authority, cosmic moral order---that have no direct analogue in the content-level measurement paradigm that dominates the landscape. The cultural cliff ensures that these alternative perspectives, even when they are represented by candidates in the landscape, are not represented at a quality level that would challenge MFT's dominance. The theoretical frame remains narrow because the cultural frame remains narrow.

*Convergence makes cultural exclusion invisible.* When content-level instruments return convergent results, the implied conclusion is that moral evaluation is culturally neutral: all models score the same, so the instruments must be measuring something universal. This conclusion obscures the cultural specificity of the instruments themselves. The MFQ-30 was developed and validated on Western populations; its convergence across models confirms that all models share the same Western moral profile, but this confirmation is easily misinterpreted as confirming that all models are "morally aligned" in a culturally general sense. The misinterpretation reduces the perceived urgency of non-Western instruments: if the existing instruments already show alignment, why invest in culturally specific alternatives? The convergence sustains the cultural exclusion by making it invisible.

These four pathways form a closed loop. Breaking any single pathway would weaken the system, but the system is designed---not intentionally but structurally---to resist perturbation. Increasing benchmark volume does not change theoretical diversity; the new benchmarks flow to the theory with existing infrastructure. Increasing model diversity does not change the instruments' content-level bias; different models trained by different methods still produce the same content-level profile. Increasing funding for benchmark construction, without specifying where the funding should go, will flow to the theories whose existing infrastructure makes the marginal cost of another benchmark lowest---which is MFT. The triple bind is a stable equilibrium that the field's current incentive structure has no mechanism to disrupt.


## 7.2 Infrastructure Determinism: Why MFT Wins

The dominance of Moral Foundations Theory in the benchmark landscape demands an explanation that goes beyond "MFT is popular" or "MFT is well-suited to LLM evaluation." The quality gap between MFT (73%) and every other theory is too large, too consistent, and too precisely aligned with infrastructure availability to be explained by researcher preference alone. The explanation is infrastructure determinism: MFT wins not because it is the best theory of moral cognition but because it possesses the best computational infrastructure, and in a landscape where benchmark quality is determined by inherited psychometric properties, infrastructure is destiny.

### 7.2.1 The Dual-Asset Advantage

MFT alone among moral psychology theories possesses two distinct computational assets that benchmark developers can inherit.

The first asset is the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30; Graham et al., 2011). The MFQ-30 is a 30-item instrument with published factor structure, internal consistency coefficients, test-retest reliability, and population-level norms validated across political orientations, religious traditions, and cultural groups spanning at least 11 countries. For a researcher building an MFT benchmark for LLMs, the MFQ-30 is a turnkey solution: administer the questionnaire, score against published norms, and report. The instrument provides validated items (boosting A4 to 100), population baselines (boosting A5), and decades of construct validity evidence (boosting A6). These three features alone---A4, A5, A6---account for a substantial fraction of the Psychological Depth sub-score, which carries 50% of the composite weight. An MFT benchmark that adapts the MFQ-30 enters the quality assessment with a structural advantage that no non-MFT benchmark can match.

The second asset is the Moral Foundations Twitter Corpus (MFTC; Hoover et al., 2020), a collection of 35,108 tweets annotated by expert raters for the presence of each moral foundation. The MFTC enables a second class of benchmark construction unavailable to any other theory: corpus-based NLP approaches in which researchers train classifiers on MFTC annotations and evaluate LLMs against these classifiers or use MFTC-derived items as benchmark stimuli. The MFTC boosts B2 (dataset size) and B3 (data availability) scores, contributing to the Practical Ease sub-score that carries the remaining 50% of the composite weight.

No other theory has this dual-asset advantage. Schwartz's theory of basic human values has the Portrait Values Questionnaire---Revised (PVQ-RR; Schwartz et al., 2012), a validated questionnaire with cross-cultural norms spanning over 80 countries, but it lacks a large annotated text corpus. Kohlberg's Cognitive Moral Development theory has the Defining Issues Test (DIT; Rest, 1979), a validated stage-assessment instrument with 40 years of developmental norms, but again no NLP corpus. The trolley tradition has the Moral Machine experiment (Awad et al., 2018) with 40 million human judgments, but no accompanying text corpus. Each of these theories possesses one computational asset and achieves a respectable but sub-MFT quality rate: Schwartz at 57%, Kohlberg at 56%, trolley at 48%. The pattern is consistent: one asset produces moderate quality; two assets produce dominant quality; zero assets produce zero quality.

Ubuntu ethics, Confucian moral philosophy, and Indigenous ethical frameworks have neither. There is no Ubuntu Moral Assessment Questionnaire with published factor loadings. There is no Confucian Role Ethics Scale with known reliability coefficients. There is no Indigenous Relational Ethics Inventory with established population norms. And there is no annotated corpus for any of these traditions---no "Ubuntu Ethics Corpus" with expert-labeled examples of communal reasoning, no "Confucian Moral Judgment Dataset" with coded examples of role-differentiated obligation. A researcher attempting to build a benchmark for any of these traditions must design items from scratch, establish psychometric properties from scratch, collect human baselines from scratch, and build evaluation machinery from scratch---all within the scope of a single paper, under the time pressure of a single publication cycle. The startup cost is prohibitive, and the result is typically a benchmark that scores in the medium or low tier because it cannot inherit the psychometric grounding that MFT benchmarks receive for free.

### 7.2.2 The Quality Flywheel

The infrastructure advantage is not static; it compounds over time through a mechanism that functions as a quality flywheel. Each successful MFT benchmark adds to the infrastructure that makes the next MFT benchmark easier to build. The flywheel operates through three mechanisms.

First, citation accumulation: the MFQ-30 has been cited thousands of times, and each citation represents another study in which the instrument's psychometric properties have been confirmed, its norms extended to new populations, or its factor structure tested under new conditions. A researcher building a new MFT benchmark in 2026 inherits not just the original MFQ-30 but the cumulative validation generated by decades of subsequent use. The instrument's psychometric credibility grows with each citation, and that credibility transfers to any benchmark that adapts it.

Second, dataset expansion: the MFTC continues to be used as training data for new moral foundation classifiers, and each new classifier generates additional labeled data (through silver-standard annotation of new text) that expands the effective corpus available for benchmark construction. The ecosystem of MFT NLP resources---classifiers, embeddings, labeled corpora, evaluation pipelines---grows denser with each research cycle, reducing the marginal cost of building the next MFT benchmark.

Third, community formation: the concentration of research effort around MFT has produced a community of researchers who share tools, datasets, evaluation protocols, and methodological norms. This community accelerates benchmark development by providing informal peer review, shared infrastructure, and a common vocabulary for discussing quality. No comparable community exists for Ubuntu or Confucian benchmark development, because the absence of foundational instruments has prevented the accumulation of a critical mass of researchers working on the same measurement tradition.

The flywheel is self-reinforcing in the technical sense that its output feeds back into its input. More MFT benchmarks generate more MFT data, which makes building more MFT benchmarks easier, which generates more data. The flywheel is also self-excluding: the more resources are invested in MFT infrastructure, the larger the gap between MFT and alternative theories becomes, and the more rational it is for the next researcher to choose MFT over alternatives. The gap does not narrow with time; it widens, because the flywheel accelerates while the competing theories remain at a standing start.

### 7.2.3 Epistemic Lock-In

The infrastructure determinism thesis has implications that extend beyond the specifics of moral psychology benchmarking. It describes a form of epistemic lock-in: a condition in which the research community's shared infrastructure constrains what can be studied, and the constraint operates not through intellectual argument but through material conditions of possibility.

The concept of lock-in is familiar from the economics of technology, where path-dependent processes can lead to the dominance of an inferior technology (the QWERTY keyboard, the VHS format) because early adoption creates network effects that make switching costs prohibitive even when superior alternatives exist [Arthur, 1989; David, 1985]. The moral psychology benchmark landscape exhibits an analogous dynamic. MFT's early investment in computational infrastructure---the MFQ-30 in 2011, the MFTC in 2020---created a first-mover advantage that subsequent theories could not overcome, not because MFT is theoretically superior but because the accumulated infrastructure makes MFT the path of least resistance for benchmark development.

The lock-in is epistemic because it constrains not just what instruments are built but what knowledge is generated. A field locked into MFT instrumentation can discover only MFT-shaped truths about LLM moral cognition. It can determine that all models endorse Care and Fairness over Sanctity and Authority, but it cannot determine whether models engage in moral intuition that precedes and shapes subsequent rationalization (social intuitionism), whether models' moral judgments are modulated by deliberative processing in theoretically predicted ways (dual-process theory), or whether models recognize the dyadic structure of moral violations (dyadic morality theory). These questions are answerable in principle but unanswerable in practice, because the instruments needed to answer them have not been built, and the infrastructure needed to build them has not been accumulated.

The lock-in also constrains self-correction. A field that recognizes its theoretical narrowness might respond by diversifying its instrument base. But the infrastructure dynamics make diversification expensive relative to deepening the existing paradigm. Building a new instrument from scratch---designing items, piloting them, collecting reliability data, establishing norms, validating against criterion measures---requires years of sustained investment and produces a benchmark that will initially score lower on the quality framework than the mature MFT instruments it competes with. The publication incentive structure, which rewards novel findings over methodological groundwork, provides little return on this investment. The lock-in is therefore self-sustaining: the conditions that make diversification necessary are the same conditions that make it costly, and the conditions that make deepening the existing paradigm unnecessary are the same conditions that make it cheap.

The implication is that MFT's dominance will not be broken by market forces, competitive dynamics, or the natural evolution of the field. It will be broken only by deliberate, funded, sustained investment in the psychometric infrastructure of alternative theories---an investment that someone must choose to make despite its short-term cost disadvantage relative to building another MFT benchmark. This is a policy recommendation, not merely an analytical observation, and it is developed further in Section 7.6.


## 7.3 The Quality Illusion: Volume Does Not Equal Progress

The benchmark landscape contains 292 candidates. In any other subfield of AI evaluation---language understanding, mathematical reasoning, code generation---a landscape of 292 dedicated instruments would indicate a mature ecosystem capable of providing rigorous, multi-dimensional assessment. The moral psychology benchmark landscape produces the opposite: a quality illusion in which volume creates the appearance of progress while the substance of evaluation capacity has stagnated.

### 7.3.1 The Arithmetic of Stagnation

The temporal quality data are stark. The pre-2020 cohort (n=8) achieves a quality rate of 75%: three out of four foundational benchmarks---the Moral Machine, the Moral Foundations Vignettes, and their peers---meet the threshold for publication-ready evaluation. The 2020--2022 cohort (n=27) drops to 48%. The 2023--2026 cohort (n=257)---which constitutes 88% of the landscape and represents the explosive growth phase driven by the emergence of ChatGPT and its competitors---achieves 46%. The quality rate has not recovered from the initial expansion-era decline; it has flatlined.

The stagnation is robust to alternative definitions of quality. If quality is operationalized as the top-tier rate alone (rather than top + high combined), the pattern is even more alarming: 50% (pre-2020), 37% (2020--2022), 13% (2023--2026). The field is producing not merely more mediocre benchmarks but, proportionally, fewer excellent ones. If quality is operationalized as the mean composite score, the pre-2020 mean substantially exceeds the 2023--2026 mean. No reasonable definition of quality reverses the finding. The field is not getting better at building moral psychology benchmarks; it is getting faster at building inadequate ones.

The volume growth, meanwhile, is driven overwhelmingly by entries in the medium and low tiers. Of the 257 candidates published in 2023--2026, 139 (54%) fall in the medium or low tier---more than half of the explosive growth cohort. These benchmarks contribute to the landscape's volume without contributing to its evaluative capacity. They fill literature reviews, appear in survey papers, and create the impression of a thriving research area while lacking the construct validity, human baselines, and psychometric grounding needed to support interpretable claims about LLM moral cognition.

### 7.3.2 Why Volume Growth Cannot Solve the Triple Bind

The quality illusion has a specific mechanism. Volume growth occurs along the path of least resistance, which is the path that existing infrastructure makes cheapest. A researcher publishing a new moral psychology benchmark in 2026 can most easily produce a paper that (a) uses or adapts an existing Western instrument (the MFQ-30, the DIT, a set of trolley scenarios) without revalidating it for LLMs, (b) administers the instrument to one or two models under default prompting conditions, and (c) reports the results as evidence of "moral reasoning" or "ethical alignment" without contextualizing against human baselines or establishing construct validity for LLM respondents. This pattern---what the landscape analysis termed "port and report"---produces face-validity (Level 2) benchmarks that contribute to volume but not to evaluative capacity.

The pattern is rational at the individual level. The researcher secures a publication, demonstrates LLM evaluation competence, and contributes to a visible research area. But the pattern is pathological at the field level because it does not address any of the structural deficiencies identified by Claims 1 through 6. It does not broaden theoretical coverage (the researcher adapts MFT or trolley instruments because that is what existing infrastructure supports). It does not improve construct validity (revalidation is expensive and the publication reward for a "validation study" is lower than for a "new finding" paper). It does not generate human baselines (baseline collection requires primary data collection with human participants, which is time-consuming and expensive). And it does not address the cultural quality cliff (building a quality non-Western benchmark requires infrastructure that does not exist).

The consequence is a landscape in which the aggregate statistics---292 candidates and counting---create the appearance of maturity while the underlying evaluative infrastructure stagnates. The field produces ever more papers titled "Evaluating Moral Reasoning in Large Language Models" that contribute ever less to the actual evaluation of moral reasoning. The quality illusion is sustained because the publication ecosystem does not distinguish between a benchmark that advances evaluative capacity and a benchmark that merely occupies bibliographic space.

### 7.3.3 Hidden Gems and the Exception That Proves the Rule

The stagnation is not absolute. Within the 2023--2026 cohort, individual benchmarks demonstrate that quality is achievable outside the dominant paradigm. Jiao et al.'s (2025) work achieves a composite score of 81.3, placing it among the highest-scoring candidates in the entire landscape. KorNAT (Korean NLP Assessment for Transformers) achieves 69.0, demonstrating that high-quality benchmark construction is possible for non-English, non-Western assessment contexts when a research community with institutional resources commits to the task. The BuddhismEval benchmark (composite 57.1) demonstrates proof of concept for non-Western religious ethics evaluation.

These hidden gems are instructive precisely because they are exceptional. Each required sustained investment in psychometric development, human baseline collection, and construct validation---the kind of slow, unglamorous work that the quality flywheel does not reward. Jiao et al.'s high composite reflects meticulous attention to construct validity, operationalization quality, and baseline provision---features that the rapid-iteration publication model discourages. KorNAT's achievement reflects the institutional infrastructure of the Korean NLP community, which invested in validated instruments for Korean cultural assessment before applying them to LLMs---the same sequence that produced MFT's dominance, applied to a different cultural context.

The implication is that the quality problem is not intrinsic to the domain. Building high-quality moral psychology benchmarks is not uniquely difficult; it is uniquely under-incentivized. The field's incentive structure rewards volume over quality, novelty over validation, and rapid iteration over sustained development. The hidden gems show that when these incentive pressures are overcome---by well-funded research groups with the institutional freedom to prioritize rigor over speed---quality benchmarks emerge. The temporal stagnation reflects the field's incentive structure, not the inherent difficulty of the task.


## 7.4 RLHF as Moral Curriculum: Universal Values or Shared Training Artifact?

The cross-vendor study identified six universal moral signatures shared by all nine models regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count: the WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias, the purity floor, authority deference suppression, the ethical consistency ceiling, the post-conventional moral default, and the deontological refusal asymmetry. These signatures constitute an RLHF moral fingerprint without documented precedent in any human population. They demand an organizing interpretation: what does it mean that three independent vendors, using different alignment methodologies, have converged on the same moral profile?

### 7.4.1 The Universal Values Hypothesis

The first candidate interpretation holds that the convergence reflects genuine moral common ground. On this account, three independent vendors---using Constitutional AI, instruction tuning, and iterative RLHF---have independently arrived at the same moral equilibrium because their training data samples overlapping human moral intuitions, and alignment training gravitates toward the central tendency of those intuitions regardless of implementation details. The convergence is evidence that alignment training captures something real about human moral cognition: the core commitments to harm avoidance, fairness, and individual rights that appear across cultures even when their relative weighting varies. The post-conventional reasoning default, the ethical consistency, and the Care/Fairness prioritization all correspond to philosophical positions with independent intellectual support [Rawls, 1971; Singer, 1981; Scanlon, 1998]. The convergence on these positions might be read as a form of moral discovery: RLHF has identified the moral equilibrium that emerges when diverse human preferences are aggregated and optimized.

The universal values hypothesis has the appeal of moral seriousness. If correct, it would mean that alignment training has achieved something remarkable: the extraction of a stable, coherent moral framework from the noise of human moral diversity. The framework happens to privilege harm-avoidance and fairness over purity and authority, but this prioritization might reflect the moral insights of Enlightenment philosophy, human rights traditions, and the empirical moral psychology literature---traditions with genuine claims to normative force.

### 7.4.2 The Shared Training Artifact Hypothesis

The second candidate interpretation holds that the convergence is an artifact of homogeneous training pipelines. Similar internet corpora, similar annotator demographics (predominantly English-speaking, university-educated, internet-connected populations), similar safety benchmarks, and similar optimization objectives produce similar outputs regardless of architectural differences. The moral profile that emerges is precisely the profile one would predict from the modal values of the populations that annotate training data: high care, high fairness, low authority, low purity, post-conventional reasoning, deontological refusal of personal harm. The convergence reflects the sociology of the AI industry, not the discovery of moral truth.

Three findings from the results sections favor this interpretation with particular force.

*The purity floor.* All nine models score approximately 1.0 on MFT purity---the functional minimum on a 0--5 scale. No human cultural group in the published MFT literature scores at the absolute floor on purity. Not secular Scandinavians (approximately 2.0--2.5), not American college students, not any documented population in the extensive cross-cultural MFQ database. Purity/sanctity reasoning has apparent biological substrates---disgust sensitivity, contamination avoidance, bodily-integrity intuitions---that are present across cultures even when their moral salience varies [Haidt and Joseph, 2004; Rozin et al., 1999]. A score of 1.0 is therefore not a position on a cultural continuum; it is a departure from the range of documented human moral variation entirely. The simplest explanation is that purity-related moral reasoning is systematically penalized in safety training because purity judgments are disproportionately associated with content that annotators classify as harmful---homophobia, religious exclusivism, disgust-based discrimination. The purity floor is an alignment artifact, not a convergence on universal moral truth.

*The precision of convergence.* The authority win rate of exactly 0.29 across all nine models is not approximately 0.29; it is the same quantitative value. The universal values hypothesis predicts approximate convergence with vendor-specific noise, because independent moral reasoning should produce agreement on central tendencies but divergence on specific values. The precision of the convergence---identical scores to two decimal places across nine models from three vendors---is better explained by the mechanism of competitive homogenization: Vendor A publishes a safety evaluation; Vendor B's model fails; Vendor B optimizes to pass; the safety property propagates across vendors. Over iterative cycles, competitive benchmarking produces convergence on the union of all vendors' safety targets, with a precision that tracks benchmark specification rather than independent moral judgment. The moral profile is converging on what safety evaluations require, not on what moral reasoning discovers.

*The annotator demographics signal.* The shared moral profile---high Individualizing, low Binding, post-conventional, purity-insensitive---maps precisely onto the documented moral-foundation profile of the population from which RLHF annotators are disproportionately recruited: young, educated, secular, English-speaking, urban. Graham et al. (2009) documented that American college students in secular universities produce Individualizing/Binding ratios of approximately 2.5---exactly the center of the 2.43--2.83 LLM band. The convergence is not on "universal values" but on the values of a specific, identifiable demographic group that happens to mediate the training signal for all three vendors. The models have not discovered moral truth; they have learned the moral preferences of their teachers.

### 7.4.3 The Process-Level Dissociation

The balance of evidence favors the shared training artifact hypothesis, but the most revealing finding is not which hypothesis is better supported overall; it is the dissociation between content-level convergence and process-level divergence.

All nine models converge on the same moral content: the same values endorsed, the same principles applied, the same dilemmas resolved in the same way. But the dual-process probe reveals that the process by which these content-level properties are generated differs across models. Haiku alone shows a chain-of-thought effect---its moral judgments shift under deliberative prompting, with CoT increasing utilitarian-direction responses by up to 25 percentage points. The eight larger models show no CoT effect: their moral judgments are identical whether or not they are prompted to deliberate.

This dissociation is diagnostic. If the convergence reflected universal moral truth---a genuine moral equilibrium that all models independently discovered---then the process by which models arrive at that equilibrium should be similarly universal. A universal moral truth should be arrived at in a universal way, or at least in ways that do not depend on whether the model is asked to think before answering. The finding that content-level moral commitments are universal while process-level dynamics are heterogeneous suggests that the content is taught (by alignment training that instills the same values across all models) rather than discovered (through independent moral reasoning that happens to converge).

The DIT P-score's non-monotonic relationship with model size reinforces this interpretation. Sonnet achieves a P-score of 85, Opus 75, Haiku 60---a pattern that reverses the developmental prediction that greater cognitive capacity produces higher-stage reasoning. Yet under a teacher persona, all nine models achieve exactly 90.0. The "developmental level" is not a structural property of the model's moral architecture; it is a calibration property of its default output, tunable by prompting. The models possess the cognitive resources for post-conventional reasoning regardless of their default P-score; the default is set by alignment training, not by moral development. This is precisely what the shared training artifact hypothesis predicts: the moral profile is a taught orientation, not an emergent one.

### 7.4.4 The "Moral Curriculum" Framing

The shared training artifact interpretation leads naturally to the "moral curriculum" framing. RLHF does not discover values; it teaches them. The alignment training process functions as a moral curriculum whose content is determined by three factors: the demographic composition of annotator pools, the safety priorities of corporate governance teams, and the competitive dynamics of the AI industry. The resulting moral profile---WEIRD-liberal, post-conventional, purity-insensitive, authority-suppressing---is a *taught* orientation, not a *discovered* one, and the cross-vendor convergence reflects shared instructors (similar annotator demographics), shared syllabi (similar safety benchmarks), and shared incentives (competitive pressure to match each other's safety evaluations) rather than independent arrival at moral truth.

The framing matters because it shifts the locus of responsibility. If the moral profile is discovered rather than taught, the question of its adequacy is a question for moral philosophy: are the values the models converge on the right values? If the moral profile is taught rather than discovered, the question of its adequacy is a question for governance: who decides what gets taught, how is that decision made, and what accountability structures exist for the consequences? The current answer to these governance questions is troubling: the moral curriculum is set by the default demographics of annotator pools and the implicit safety philosophies of three corporations, without any mechanism for democratic input, cross-cultural representation, or normative deliberation about what values AI systems should embody.

The governance question is sharpened by the observation that the moral curriculum is taught not through explicit instruction but through preference optimization. No vendor has declared a target value for MFT purity scores, authority win rates, or DIT P-scores. The moral profile is an emergent consequence of the training pipeline, not an intentional design choice. This means that the moral curriculum cannot be easily modified by changing explicit training objectives; it would require changing the composition of annotator pools, the structure of preference data collection, and the competitive dynamics of the AI safety ecosystem---changes that are technically feasible but institutionally resistant because they involve the human factors of the training pipeline rather than the technical parameters.

The implications for moral pluralism are profound. If the convergence reflects universal values, moral pluralism is an illusion that alignment training has helpfully seen through---the models have identified the One True Morality and faithfully reproduce it. If the convergence reflects a shared training artifact, moral pluralism is a reality that alignment training has inadvertently suppressed---the models have been taught one community's values and present them as universal. The cultural quality cliff (Bind 3) makes the shared-artifact interpretation more parsimonious: the traditions excluded from the benchmark landscape are precisely the traditions that would provide the evaluative basis for detecting whether the "universal" moral profile is, in fact, culturally specific. The instruments needed to test the universal values hypothesis against the shared artifact hypothesis are the instruments that the landscape has not built.


## 7.5 Cultural Blindness in Global AI Deployment

The cultural quality cliff identified in Claim 4 is not an abstract distributional finding of interest only to psychometricians. It is a deployment safety issue of immediate practical consequence. LLMs are being deployed at scale in societies whose dominant moral frameworks are precisely the frameworks that the benchmark landscape cannot evaluate.

### 7.5.1 The Scale of Unevaluable Deployment

The populations affected by the cultural quality cliff are not small or marginal.

Ubuntu-practicing societies span sub-Saharan Africa, a region of 1.2 billion people that represents among the fastest-growing markets for AI deployment. LLMs are being integrated into healthcare delivery systems, agricultural advisory platforms, educational technologies, and financial services across Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Ghana, and dozens of other nations. These societies draw on Ubuntu philosophy---"I am because we are"---as a foundational ethical framework that emphasizes communal responsibility, relational personhood, and collective flourishing in ways that are structurally incompatible with the individualistic moral foundations measured by MFT and Schwartz. When a healthcare chatbot in rural South Africa makes a triage recommendation that implicitly prioritizes individual autonomy over communal obligation, no existing benchmark can evaluate whether that recommendation aligns with the moral frameworks of the community it serves.

Confucian societies span East Asia---China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam---with a combined population exceeding 1.5 billion people and representing the largest concentration of LLM users outside the English-speaking world. Confucian moral philosophy, with its emphasis on filial piety, role-differentiated obligations, hierarchical harmony, and the cultivation of virtue through ritual practice, remains deeply influential in moral reasoning across these societies, even among populations that do not explicitly identify as Confucian. The question "does this LLM align with Confucian moral values?"---a question of direct practical importance for deployment in China, where hundreds of millions of users interact with LLM-based services---cannot currently be answered at any quality standard using any existing instrument in the benchmark landscape. All 13 Confucian candidates score in the low tier, with composites ranging from 14.6 to 21.2.

Indigenous communities worldwide maintain moral frameworks emphasizing reciprocal relationships with the natural world, intergenerational obligations, and place-based moral knowledge. These communities are affected wherever AI systems are deployed in contexts that intersect with Indigenous knowledge systems: environmental monitoring in the Amazon Basin, healthcare delivery in First Nations communities in Canada, educational technology in Maori communities in New Zealand, natural resource management across the Arctic. The benchmark landscape's 4 Indigenous candidates---all in the low tier---represent an evaluative infrastructure with effectively zero capacity to assess whether AI systems respect or violate Indigenous moral principles.

The combined population of societies where Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, or Identity ethics represent influential moral frameworks exceeds 3 billion people---roughly 40% of the global population. The benchmark landscape's capacity to evaluate LLM moral alignment for these populations is exactly zero.

### 7.5.2 The Answerability Gap

The cultural quality cliff creates what might be called an answerability gap: the gap between the questions that responsible global deployment requires and the questions that the current benchmark landscape can answer.

The question "does this LLM align with human moral values?" is the foundational question of AI moral evaluation. But the question, as the benchmark landscape currently operationalizes it, is not the question it appears to be. It is the question "does this LLM endorse Care and Fairness over Sanctity and Authority in a pattern consistent with WEIRD liberal populations?" This operationalization is invisible to most users of moral evaluation results, including the policymakers and deployment decision-makers who rely on benchmark scores to certify moral adequacy. When a safety evaluation reports that an LLM "demonstrates strong moral reasoning," the reader assumes this characterization applies to moral reasoning as experienced by the communities where the model will be deployed. It does not. It applies to moral reasoning as measured by instruments developed in Western academic psychology departments, validated on Western university student populations, and normed against the moral intuitions of WEIRD respondents.

The answerability gap has specific, concrete manifestations:

*A triage algorithm deployed in Lagos.* When an AI-assisted healthcare system in Nigeria must decide how to allocate scarce medical resources, Ubuntu moral reasoning would prioritize communal impact---the effect on the patient's family and community, the patient's role in maintaining communal well-being, the obligations of solidarity that bind the healthcare system to the community it serves. MFT-based evaluation can determine whether the system prioritizes Care over Loyalty, but it cannot determine whether the system engages with the relational web of communal obligation that Ubuntu moral reasoning centers. The evaluation is measuring the wrong thing.

*A content moderation system deployed in Seoul.* When an AI content moderator must evaluate whether a post violates community standards in a Korean social media platform, Confucian moral reasoning would attend to the poster's social role, the relationship between the poster and those affected, the degree to which the post disrupts social harmony, and the obligations that attach to the poster's position in a network of hierarchical relationships. MFT-based evaluation can determine whether the system suppresses Authority-based reasoning (it does---the authority win rate is 0.29 across all models), but it cannot determine whether the system recognizes the legitimate role of hierarchical obligation in Confucian moral life. The evaluation is not just measuring the wrong thing; it is actively penalizing the moral intuitions of the community the system is meant to serve.

*An educational tutor deployed in Indigenous communities.* When an AI educational system presents moral scenarios to students in First Nations communities, Indigenous moral reasoning would attend to the reciprocal relationships between humans and the natural world, the obligations that extend across generations, and the place-based moral knowledge that attaches to specific landscapes, ecosystems, and ancestral territories. No instrument in the benchmark landscape---at any quality level---measures any of these dimensions. The evaluation is not measuring the wrong thing; it is not measuring at all.

### 7.5.3 KorNAT and the Possibility of Escape

The cultural quality cliff is formidable but not insuperable. KorNAT (Korean NLP Assessment for Transformers, composite 69.0) demonstrates that quality benchmark construction is achievable outside the Western MFT paradigm when a research community with institutional resources commits to the task.

KorNAT succeeds because it follows the same developmental sequence that produced MFT's dominance, applied to a different cultural context. The Korean NLP community invested in validated assessment instruments grounded in Korean cultural values and social norms before adapting those instruments for LLM evaluation. The benchmark inherits psychometric properties from this prior investment: validated items, population-level Korean norms, and construct validity evidence specific to the cultural context it measures. Its composite score of 69.0 places it in the high tier, demonstrating that the quality framework is not intrinsically biased against non-Western benchmarks; it rewards the same properties---validated instruments, human baselines, construct validity---regardless of the cultural tradition from which the benchmark emerges.

The lesson of KorNAT is that the cultural quality cliff is an infrastructure problem with an infrastructure solution. The cliff exists not because non-Western moral traditions are inherently resistant to psychometric operationalization but because the psychometric development work has not been done. Doing that work requires sustained investment: designing instruments grounded in the moral concepts of each tradition, piloting those instruments with members of the relevant cultural communities, establishing psychometric properties through iterative testing, collecting population-level norms, and validating the instruments for cross-cultural comparability. This is a multi-year project for each tradition, requiring collaboration between moral philosophers, psychometricians, and computational linguists---the same collaboration that produced the MFQ-30 over a decade of development.

The Islamic ethics tradition provides additional evidence for this thesis. With a quality rate of 36% (8 of 22 candidates in top or high tiers), Islamic ethics substantially outperforms the zero-quality traditions while falling below the Western cluster. The partial success of Islamic benchmark development reflects partial infrastructure: classical Islamic jurisprudence texts provide structured source material for item development, and dedicated research programs (QIAS, "Sacred or Synthetic?") have invested in benchmark-specific development. The quality rate correlates with infrastructure availability, not with intrinsic difficulty or cultural compatibility with psychometric methods.

### 7.5.4 The Governance Urgency

The cultural quality cliff is not a problem that can wait for the natural evolution of the field to resolve. LLMs are being deployed now, in communities whose moral frameworks the evaluation infrastructure cannot assess. The deployment is not paused pending the development of culturally appropriate evaluation tools; it proceeds on the basis of evaluation results that are culturally parochial and presented as universal.

The urgency is compounded by the competitive dynamics of AI deployment in the Global South and East Asia. Companies deploying LLMs in these markets face competitive pressure to move quickly, and the absence of culturally specific evaluation tools is not treated as a deployment blocker but as an evaluation gap to be filled later---or ignored entirely. The result is a de facto moral imposition: Western moral values, encoded in RLHF training and confirmed by Western evaluation instruments, are deployed in communities with different moral traditions, and the evaluation infrastructure provides no mechanism for detecting whether this imposition is occurring, let alone correcting for it.

The governments of countries where non-Western moral traditions predominate are beginning to develop AI governance frameworks---China's Generative AI Measures, the African Union's AI Continental Strategy, South Korea's AI Ethics Standards---but these frameworks currently rely on the same benchmark landscape that this study has shown to be culturally parochial. Without culturally grounded evaluation instruments, these governance frameworks cannot operationalize their own stated commitments to culturally appropriate AI deployment. The policy aspiration outpaces the evaluative infrastructure.


## 7.6 Implications for AI Governance and Policy

The findings of this study carry direct implications for the emerging regulatory frameworks governing AI moral behavior. The current governance infrastructure rests on 48 publication-ready benchmarks, 73% of which derive from a single theory, none of which can distinguish between competing commercial systems, and none of which can evaluate alignment with the moral traditions of approximately 3 billion people. This section develops seven recommendations for policymakers, auditors, and benchmark developers.

### 7.6.1 The Governance Foundation Problem

Before detailing recommendations, the underlying problem must be stated plainly. Policymakers currently contemplating AI moral evaluation requirements---in the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, executive orders, and sector-specific regulations worldwide---are making decisions that implicitly assume the evaluation infrastructure exists to implement those decisions. For Western moral frameworks measured by MFT-derived instruments, that assumption is approximately correct: the infrastructure, while imperfect, is functional. For non-Western moral frameworks, the assumption is false. And for the specific task that regulation most urgently requires---distinguishing between competing systems to identify which are safe for deployment---the assumption is false regardless of cultural context, because the instruments cannot distinguish between models.

The gap between governance aspiration and evaluative capacity is the practical manifestation of the triple bind. Regulators aspire to certify that AI systems are "morally aligned." The evaluation infrastructure can certify only that AI systems produce MFT-consistent outputs that are identical across all tested systems. These are not the same certification, and the gap between them constitutes a governance risk: systems may be certified as morally adequate on the basis of evidence that has no bearing on the moral properties that matter for deployment in culturally diverse contexts.

### 7.6.2 Seven Recommendations

The triple bind analysis identifies seven specific interventions, organized by the bind they address, the claim they respond to, and the investment required.

**Recommendation 1: Fund non-Western instrument development.** The cultural quality cliff cannot be closed by producing more benchmark papers. It requires prior investment in the psychometric infrastructure that benchmark construction presupposes: validated moral assessment instruments for Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and other underrepresented traditions. These instruments must be developed through collaboration between moral philosophers from the relevant traditions, psychometricians with expertise in cross-cultural instrument development, and computational linguists who can translate the resulting instruments into LLM-administrable formats. The development timeline is measured in years, not months, and the investment cannot be justified by the publication metrics that drive most academic research. It requires dedicated funding from research agencies, international development organizations, or technology companies with global deployment footprints. This recommendation addresses Claim 4 (cultural quality cliff) and is the single highest-priority intervention identified by this study.

**Recommendation 2: Require construct validity evidence (A6 >= 4) for governance use.** The construct validity crisis documented in Claim 2---58% of benchmarks at Level 1 or Level 2---means that a majority of the benchmark landscape produces numbers without psychological meaning. Governance frameworks should require that any benchmark used to inform deployment decisions possesses at least Level 4 construct validity (single-study validation), ensuring that the instrument has been empirically demonstrated to measure what it claims to measure. This requirement would exclude the majority of existing benchmarks from governance use, which is precisely the point: instruments that have not been validated should not be used to certify the moral adequacy of systems deployed to billions of users.

**Recommendation 3: Mandate human baselines for governance-grade benchmarks.** The baseline deficit documented in Claim 5---34% of benchmarks providing no human comparison data---renders LLM moral scores uninterpretable. A score of 0.73 on a moral reasoning metric means nothing without knowing what humans score on the same metric. Governance frameworks should require that any benchmark used in regulatory contexts provide population-level human baselines, or at minimum paper-specific human comparison samples, collected from populations representative of the deployment context. The DIT's 40 years of developmental norms and the Moral Machine's 40 million human judgments demonstrate that baseline collection is feasible; what is lacking is the requirement to do it.

**Recommendation 4: Invest in process-level evaluation.** The dual-process probe was the only benchmark in the 23-item trial battery that cleanly separated models across vendors. Content-level instruments---measuring what models value---produced convergence; the process-level instrument---measuring how models reason---produced discrimination. Governance frameworks should prioritize the development and standardization of process-level evaluation instruments: dual-process probes that vary deliberative resources across moral dilemmas, social intuitionist protocols that test susceptibility to moral dumbfounding, dyadic morality assessments that manipulate agent-patient dynamics. These instruments measure dimensions of moral cognition that RLHF training does not homogenize, and they are therefore the instruments most likely to provide the discriminative information that governance requires.

**Recommendation 5: Require quality tier transparency in AI evaluation reports.** The quality illusion documented in Section 7.3 is sustained in part because evaluation reports do not distinguish between benchmarks of different quality levels. When a safety evaluation reports that a model "passes 47 moral reasoning benchmarks," the reader cannot determine whether those 47 benchmarks are all top-tier instruments with validated psychometrics and human baselines or whether they include low-tier instruments that engage with moral psychology at the keyword level only. Governance frameworks should require that evaluation reports disclose the quality tier of each benchmark used, following a standardized quality classification such as the one developed in this study. Transparency does not solve the quality problem, but it prevents the quality illusion from misleading decision-makers.

**Recommendation 6: Establish cross-cultural benchmark development programs.** Recommendation 1 calls for non-Western instrument development; this recommendation calls for the institutional infrastructure to support it. Cross-cultural benchmark development requires sustained collaboration between institutions in the relevant cultural contexts---universities in sub-Saharan Africa, East Asia, and Indigenous communities---and the computational linguistics community that will translate instruments into LLM-administrable formats. International research programs, such as those funded by the European Research Council, the National Science Foundation's International Research Experiences for Students program, or the African Union's Science, Technology, and Innovation Strategy for Africa, could provide the institutional framework for this collaboration. The KorNAT precedent demonstrates that quality is achievable when institutional commitment exists; the recommendation is to create institutional commitment where it does not yet exist.

**Recommendation 7: Support temporal quality monitoring.** The temporal stagnation documented in Claim 6 is invisible without longitudinal tracking. Governance frameworks should support ongoing monitoring of benchmark quality over time, tracking whether the quality rate improves, stagnates, or declines as the landscape continues to grow. This monitoring should be conducted independently of the benchmark development community, to avoid conflicts of interest, and should be published transparently to enable the research community and policymakers to track progress against the benchmarks' own quality standards.

## 7.7 Limitations

The findings reported in this paper rest on methodological foundations that constrain, in specific and load-bearing ways, the inferential claims they support. This section catalogues the limitations comprehensively, not as a performative gesture toward academic modesty but because each limitation circumscribes the interpretability of specific results.

### 7.7.1 The Self-Administration Confound

Claude Opus 4.6 both designed the assessment framework and served as the primary test subject for the initial 23 implementation trials (approximately 750 items administered). This creates a self-administration confound that pervades every trial-derived finding. The clearest evidence of contamination comes from Trial MP22 (Tracing Moral Foundations), in which every model in the nine-model battery achieved 100% accuracy in identifying which MFT foundation each item was designed to measure. This demonstrates explicit awareness of instrument structure: a respondent that can perfectly reverse-engineer which construct an item targets cannot be treated as a naive respondent in any psychometric sense. The MFQ-30's Individualizing/Binding ratio of 2.83 for Opus therefore cannot be cleanly disentangled from the model's knowledge of what a "liberal moral profile" looks like in the MFT literature.

The cross-vendor replication partially mitigates this concern. The finding that nine models from three vendors converge on the same moral profile cannot be attributed to a Claude-specific self-administration artifact. The convergence finding---the study's primary contribution---holds regardless of the self-administration confound because it is a comparative finding: it establishes that models from different vendors are indistinguishable, not that any individual model's absolute scores are trustworthy. Model-specific findings---such as Opus's dumbfounding rate of 0.30 or its MFQ-30 Individualizing/Binding ratio of 2.83---remain hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, because the self-administration confound cannot be ruled out for absolute values.

Three mitigations could be pursued in future work. First, replication with obfuscated instruments: the MFQ-30 items could be reworded to preserve construct content while rendering them unrecognizable, enabling comparison between original and obfuscated profiles. Second, bare API administration without system prompts, removing the conversational context that may anchor responses. Third, independent orchestration by researchers external to any model family, eliminating the vendor-specific framing entirely.

### 7.7.2 Single-Orchestrator Framing

All nine models in the cross-vendor study were prompted by the same Claude Opus 4.6 orchestrator through the Claude Code interface. This creates a framing confound that cannot be separated from the cross-vendor convergence finding. If the Opus-generated prompts happen to anchor responses toward a particular moral profile---through word choice, framing, or implicit expectations embedded in prompt structure---then some portion of the observed convergence may reflect shared prompt framing rather than genuine cross-vendor moral homogeneity.

The concern is sharpened by the chain-of-thought finding: the CoT prompt ("think step by step before answering") was generated by Opus for all nine models, and the apparent absence of a CoT effect in eight models could partly reflect prompt-model mismatch rather than genuine insensitivity to deliberative scaffolding. The mitigation is straightforward in principle: replicate the study using vendor-neutral orchestration, where each vendor's API is called directly with prompt templates designed independently of any particular model. The present study cannot implement this mitigation because the research infrastructure is built on the Claude Code platform, which necessarily interposes Opus as orchestrator.

### 7.7.3 N=1 Per Model Per Benchmark

Each model-benchmark pair in the cross-vendor study was tested exactly once at default temperature. There are no within-model variance estimates, no confidence intervals, and no significance tests. Every cross-vendor "difference" reported in this paper is a comparison of two point estimates whose reliability is unknown.

The consequences are pervasive. The DIT P-score difference between Sonnet (85) and Opus (75) is a 10-point gap that, in the human psychometric literature, corresponds to the difference between college students and moral philosophy graduate students. But with N=1 administrations, this gap could fall within the test-retest variability of a single model. The Haiku CoT effect of +0.125 is the study's most theoretically generative finding, but it derives from a single administration of approximately 8 dilemmas; the statistical uncertainty surrounding this point estimate is substantial. Temperature variation, prompt perturbation, and repeated administration would strengthen (or appropriately qualify) all findings. We estimate that a minimum of N=30 administrations per model per benchmark would be needed for key findings to support inferential statistics.

We characterize our computational analyses as hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming. The descriptive findings---counts, distributions, gap identification, convergence indices---do not depend on inferential statistics and remain our primary contribution. The inferential findings warrant dedicated confirmatory studies with adequate statistical power.

### 7.7.4 Keyword Scorer Sensitivity

The expanded scorer---125 patterns across 13 theories---represents a substantial improvement over the original 72-pattern scorer, increasing detection across all moral psychology categories. However, keyword-based scoring inherently faces a sensitivity-specificity tradeoff. The scorer may still miss benchmarks that use non-standard vocabulary, neologisms, or indirect engagement with moral psychology constructs. It may also produce false positives for papers that use moral psychology terminology in non-benchmark contexts (e.g., literature reviews, philosophical analyses). The transition from the original to the expanded scorer changed the counts for several categories, and the full-coverage findings should be interpreted as specific to the scorer version used.

### 7.7.5 Jenks Boundary Sensitivity

The quality tier assignments are derived from Jenks natural breaks optimization with a goodness of variance fit (GVF) of 0.83. While this GVF indicates good fit---the four-tier partition captures 83% of the variance in composite scores---tier assignments near boundary values are somewhat arbitrary. A benchmark with a composite of 56.5 falls in the High tier while one at 56.7 falls in the Top tier, yet these instruments may be of functionally equivalent quality. The natural breaks method was chosen over arbitrary thresholds precisely to minimize this problem, but boundary sensitivity remains inherent to any discrete classification of a continuous variable. The finding that non-Western traditions achieve 0% quality is robust to reasonable boundary perturbation---no amount of boundary adjustment would move their composites (ranging from 10.8 to 35.1) into the top or high tiers---but individual tier assignments near the break points should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

### 7.7.6 Temporal Snapshot

The 292-candidate landscape represents a point-in-time assessment. New benchmarks are published continuously, and the landscape's composition will change over time. The temporal stagnation finding (quality rate flat at 46% despite 9.5-fold volume growth) describes the trajectory through the assessment date but does not predict future trajectories. It is possible, though the incentive structure analysis in Section 7.3 suggests it is unlikely without deliberate intervention, that future benchmark development will improve quality rates through increased attention to construct validity, human baselines, and cross-cultural representation. The landscape assessment should be periodically updated to track whether the recommendations of this paper are being implemented and whether the quality trajectory is changing.

---

The limitations catalogued in this section are genuine constraints on the study's inferential reach. They do not, however, undermine the central contribution. The triple bind is a structural diagnosis based on distributional properties of the landscape (292 candidates, tiered by data-driven methods), cross-vendor patterns (9 models, 3 vendors, 23 benchmarks), and the logical relationship between these two levels of analysis. The self-administration confound affects absolute values but not the comparative patterns that constitute the study's primary findings. The N=1 limitation affects statistical confidence but not the descriptive patterns---the convergence, the quality cliff, the temporal stagnation---that the paper documents. And the keyword scorer sensitivity affects candidate counts at the margins but not the structural conclusions that emerge from the full 292-candidate assessment. The triple bind is not a finding that depends on the precision of any single measurement; it is a finding about the structure of an entire evaluative ecosystem, and that structure is visible across every analytical approach we have applied.
