Abstract
This paper presents a novel methodology for integrating indigenous knowledge systems into AI development to counter 
cognitive imperialism and foster inclusivity. By critiquing the dominance of Western epistemologies and highlighting the 
risks of bias, the authors argue for incorporating diverse epistemologies. The proposed framework outlines a participatory 
approach that includes indigenous perspectives, ensuring AI benefits all. The methodology draws from AI ethics, indigenous 
studies, and postcolonial theory, emphasizing co-creation with indigenous communities, ethical protocols for indigenous data 
governance, and adaptation of AI algorithms. Case studies in natural language processing, content moderation, and healthcare 
demonstrate the methodology’s effectiveness and importance. By offering a concrete methodology for decolonizing AI, this 
paper contributes significantly to AI ethics and social justice, providing a roadmap for equitable, culturally respectful AI.

2  Background
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become a transformative 
force in the global landscape of knowledge and learning. Its 
rapid development and integration into various sectors have 
revolutionized how information is processed, understood, 
and disseminated. AI technologies, ranging from machine 
learning algorithms to advanced data analytics, are now 
pivotal tools in educational environments, research institu-
tions, and knowledge-based industries (Sarker 2022). They 
facilitate personalized learning experiences, aid in complex 
research processes, and enable the handling of large volumes 
of data with unprecedented efficiency Johnson et al. (2023). 
Moreover, AI’s role in the global education sector has been 
particularly significant. It offers adaptive learning systems 
that tailor educational content to individual student needs, 
allowing for more inclusive and effective learning experi-
ences (Kabudi et al. 2021). In research, AI has accelerated 
the pace of discovery by analyzing vast datasets that would 
be unmanageable for humans alone, leading to new insights 
and innovations across diverse fields (Chamorro-Premuzic 
2023).
However, this rapid integration of AI into knowledge and 
learning spheres is not without challenges. The algorithms 
driving these AI systems are often designed with inher-
ent biases, reflecting the perspectives and values of their 
predominantly Western creators. This has raised concerns 
about the representation and integration of diverse global 
knowledge systems in AI-driven platforms (Ray 2023). As 
AI continues to shape knowledge and learning, it is crucial 
to address these biases to ensure that AI systems are inclu-
sive and representative of the diverse cognitive landscapes 
around the world (Chen et al. 2023).
3  Problem statement
Cognitive imperialism in AI refers to the phenomenon where 
AI systems, predominantly developed within Western para-
digms, implicitly embed and propagate certain worldviews 
and epistemologies, often to the exclusion or marginalisa-
tion of others. This becomes particularly problematic when 
considering indigenous knowledge systems, which are fre-
quently overlooked or undervalued in the development and 
application of AI technologies. The AI algorithms, shaped 
by data and perspectives that largely emanate from West-
ern contexts, risk perpetuating a form of epistemic domi-
nance that aligns with specific cultural norms and values, 
inadvertently reinforcing a form of cultural hegemony (Lind-
gren 2023).
The marginalisation of indigenous knowledge systems 
in AI is not merely a theoretical concern; it has practical 
implications. Indigenous communities possess rich, com-
plex knowledge systems developed over centuries, encom-
passing unique understandings of the natural world, medi-
cine, agriculture, and societal organisation, which are often 
deeply intertwined with their cultural and spiritual values 
(Brondízio et al. 2021). When AI systems fail to recognize 
or integrate these diverse epistemologies, they not only per-
petuate a narrow view of intelligence and knowledge but also 
risk alienating and disenfranchising indigenous populations. 
This exclusion can lead to AI solutions that are less effective 
or even harmful in these communities, as they do not align 
with the local contexts and needs (Baradaran 2023).
The challenge, therefore, is to confront and counteract 
these ingrained biases within AI. It involves questioning 
the underlying assumptions that guide AI development and 
actively seeking to diversify the sources of knowledge and 
data that inform AI systems. By doing so, it is possible to 
create AI technologies that are more inclusive, equitable, 
and representative of the global diversity of human thought 
and experience (Zaga and Lupetti 2022).
4  Purpose of the paper
The purpose of this paper is to investigate and articulate 
how indigenous epistemologies can be integrated into the 
development of AI to mitigate existing biases and foster a 
more inclusive technology landscape. This integration is 
essential, as it addresses the cognitive imperialism that has 
led to the underrepresentation and marginalisation of indig-
enous knowledge systems in AI (Brokensha et al. 2023). 
By weaving indigenous ways of knowing into the AI narra-
tive, we not only enrich the technology with a diversity of 
thought and practice but also honour the epistemic rights 
of indigenous communities (Ndasauka 2024). To achieve 
this, the paper will propose a framework for the inclusion of 
indigenous epistemologies throughout the AI development 
lifecycle, informed by the principles of participatory design 
and co-creation with indigenous stakeholders (Martin 2020). 
This framework aims to ensure that indigenous perspectives 
are not merely supplemental but are integral to the design 
and function of AI systems.
Moreover, the goal extends beyond inclusion; it seeks to 
recalibrate the epistemic foundations upon which AI is built, 
thereby challenging the current paradigms and fostering an 
AI that is reflective of the world’s cultural and cognitive 
diversity (Gomez and Smith 2023). The expected outcome is 
the development of AI technologies that are more equitable, 
contextually relevant, and culturally sensitive, promoting a

5  Relevance
The integration of indigenous epistemologies into AI is 
of paramount significance for the creation of technologies 
that are culturally diverse and respectful. This relevance is 
grounded in the ethical imperative to ensure that AI systems 
reflect and respect the multiplicity of human experiences and 
wisdoms. The integration is not merely about adding diver-
sity for its aesthetic value but is about correcting an epis-
temic imbalance and rectifying historical exclusions (Smeds 
et al. 2023). It is an essential step towards the development 
of AI that can truly serve global communities, honouring 
their unique cultural heritages and worldviews (Lowagie 
2023). Incorporating indigenous knowledge systems into 
AI also has practical implications for the functionality and 
applicability of AI in diverse contexts. AI systems informed 
by a broader range of cultural insights are better equipped 
to serve the needs of indigenous populations, providing 
solutions that are aligned with their values, practices, and 
languages (Sanderson et al. 2023). This cultural alignment 
is the key to the development of AI that is not only effec-

is intended to serve (Skewes et al. 2020). Furthermore, a 
culturally diverse and respectful AI is more robust and less 
prone to the risks of bias and myopia. By incorporating 
varied perspectives, AI can achieve greater objectivity and 
fairness, traits that are crucial for its acceptance and suc-
cess (Skewes et al. 2020). The relevance of this integration, 
therefore, extends beyond the ethical; it is also about the 
creation of better AI—better in its capacity to understand, 
interpret, and interact with the world in its full complexity 
(Coeckelbergh 2020). The significance of this integration is 
a testament to the growing acknowledgment that AI must be 
developed in a manner that is truly inclusive, ensuring that 
technological progress does not come at the cost of cultural 
erasure but rather contributes to a richer, more just, and more 
equitable society (Coeckelbergh 2020).
6  Cognitive imperialism in AI
The burgeoning field of AI has brought with it significant 
advancements in how societies operate and interact, yet it 
simultaneously perpetuates a form of cognitive imperialism 
due to the prevailing dominance of Western epistemolo-
gies in the design and deployment of these systems. The 
prevailing research underscores how AI, while positioned 
as a neutral and universal technology, often embeds and 
exacerbates biases that reflect the inequalities of the larger 
society. Studies such as those by Buolamwini and Gebru 
(2018) demonstrate the racial and gender biases inherent 
in facial recognition software, while the work of Caliskan 
et al. (2017) reveals how language processing algorithms 
can inherit and perpetuate societal biases. These biases are 
symptomatic of a deeper issue: the homogeneity within the 
AI field, where a lack of diversity in development teams and 
training data restricts the perspective that AI systems are 
built upon. D’ignazio and Klein (2023) argue that the data 
used to train AI often exclude non-normative, non-Western 
forms of knowledge and experience. The repercussions of 
this exclusion are not merely academic but have tangible 
impacts on marginalized communities, particularly indig-
enous groups whose knowledge systems are either misrep-
resented or entirely absent in AI models, leading to misap-
plications and misinterpretations that can perpetuate harm 
(Bhandari and Bimo 2022). Efforts to decolonize AI, as 
discussed by Mohamed et al. (2020), involve a conscien-
tious effort to diversify datasets and development teams and 
to include ethical considerations that prioritize inclusivity. 
Yet, despite growing acknowledgment and efforts to address 
these concerns, the literature reveals notable gaps.
Firstly, there is an underrepresentation of empirical stud-
ies that document the specific impacts of AI biases on vari-
ous indigenous and non-Western communities. While the 
theoretical framework for understanding cognitive imperi-
alism in AI is well-established, there is a need for compre-
hensive research that elucidates the practical consequences 
and nuances of these biases. Secondly, the discourse around 
the decolonisation of AI is still in its infancy, with more 
rhetoric than concrete methodology on how to operation-
alize the inclusion of indigenous epistemologies. There is 
a scarcity of detailed frameworks and success stories that 
illustrate the effective integration of these diverse knowl-
edge systems into AI development processes. Lastly, the lit-
erature lacks a robust cross-cultural analysis that compares 
and contrasts how different non-Western societies perceive 
and interact with AI technologies. Such comparative studies 
could shed light on the varying degrees of cognitive impe-
rialism across different contexts and offer insights into how 
AI can be developed to be truly global in its understanding 
and application.
In conclusion, while there has been substantial progress 
in identifying the existence of cognitive imperialism within 
AI, the field requires further research to develop practical 
solutions and to understand the broader, global implica-
tions of these biases. Bridging these gaps is essential for 
the creation of AI technologies that are equitable, culturally

7  Indigenous epistemologies
Indigenous knowledge systems are as diverse and multifac-
eted as the cultures they stem from, each offering a unique 
worldview and understanding of the universe. These sys-
tems are rooted in a holistic approach to knowledge, where 
learning and understanding are interwoven with spirituality, 
community, and the environment (Wilson 2008). They often 
emphasize relational knowledge, which is the interconnect-
edness of all beings and the environment, and this stands in 
stark contrast to the compartmentalized and individualistic 
knowledge systems that underpin many Western epistemolo-
gies (Cajete 2004). Research into indigenous knowledge sys-
tems has highlighted the sophisticated practices and insights 
these cultures hold, particularly in fields such as ecology, 
agriculture, medicine, and social organisation. For instance, 
the traditional burning practices of Australian Aboriginals 
have been recognized for their complex understanding of fire 
ecology and land management, a knowledge that is gaining 
attention for its potential application in contemporary fire 
management and conservation efforts (Yibarbuk et al. 2001).
In medicine, the traditional knowledge of plant-based 
treatments among Amazonian tribes has contributed to the 
discovery of new drugs, showcasing the intricate understand-
ing of botany and chemistry possessed by these indigenous 
cultures (Cox and Balick 1994). Moreover, indigenous social 
systems, often based on principles of collective ownership 
and stewardship, present alternative models for community 
organisation and resource management that challenge domi-
nant economic paradigms (Berkes 2017). Despite the value 
of these knowledge systems, the integration of indigenous 
epistemologies into mainstream academia and research 
remains limited. A significant barrier is the lack of recogni-
tion and respect for the oral traditions through which much 
of this knowledge is transmitted. The oral nature of these 
traditions does not align with the written-centric validation 
systems of Western academia, leading to a devaluation of 
indigenous knowledge as anecdotal or unscientific (Briggs 
and Sharp 2004).
Furthermore, the appropriation and exploitation of indig-
enous knowledge, often without proper acknowledgment or 
compensation, have led to distrust and reluctance among 
indigenous communities to share their expertise (Smith 
2021). This dynamic has led to calls for a decolonisation 
of research methodologies and the establishment of frame-
works for ethical collaboration that respect indigenous intel-
lectual property rights and prioritize indigenous agency 
(Whyte 2018). The literature reveals a need for increased 
interdisciplinary research that bridges indigenous and 
Western epistemologies, creating a dialogue that respects 
and incorporates indigenous perspectives. This approach has 
the potential not only to enrich AI but also to contribute to 
solving complex global challenges by harnessing the col-
lective human wisdom that indigenous knowledge systems 
represent.
In sum, while indigenous knowledge systems hold 
immense potential for contributing to various fields, includ-
ing AI, significant gaps remain in how this knowledge is 
recognized, validated, and integrated into mainstream sci-
entific and technological development. Addressing these 
gaps requires not only an acknowledgment of the value of 
indigenous epistemologies but also systemic changes in how 
knowledge is defined, acquired, and used.
8  Decolonizing AI: addressing the absence 
of indigenous epistemologies in AI 
research
The literature on AI development and research has consist-
ently highlighted a significant gap: the absence of indig-
enous perspectives and knowledge systems. This gap is not 
merely a matter of representation but reflects a deeper epis-
temological and methodological oversight that compromises 
the cultural inclusivity and ethical soundness of AI systems 
(Keikelame and Swartz 2019). Indigenous perspectives 
offer alternative understandings of relationships, commu-
nity, and the environment, which are often overlooked in 
the predominantly data-driven and algorithmic approach of 
AI development. This oversight results in AI systems that 
are designed without the benefit of the rich contextual and 
holistic knowledge that indigenous epistemologies provide 
(Nakata et al. 2012). Research conducted by Smith et al. 
(2018) emphasizes that the methodologies employed in AI 
development typically do not account for the communal 
and narrative-based knowledge that is central to indigenous 
cultures. The resulting AI applications are, therefore, not 
equipped to interpret or engage with the complexity of indig-
enous cultural practices and languages, which limits their 
usability and acceptability within indigenous communities.
Furthermore, the lack of indigenous perspectives in AI 
research is also a reflection of broader systemic issues within 
the technology sector, such as the underrepresentation of 
indigenous peoples in STEM fields. As noted by Rainie et al. 
(2019), the scarcity of indigenous scholars and practition-
ers in AI development teams exacerbates the epistemic gap, 
as AI systems are not being informed by the diverse expe-
riences and insights that indigenous professionals would 
bring. There is a growing call for the decolonisation of AI, 
which would involve a critical examination of the ways in 
which AI research and development can perpetuate colo-
nial power dynamics. This includes an interrogation of who

9  Methodology
The methodology for the integration of indigenous epis-
temologies into AI systems is a comprehensive approach 
that combines Qualitative Analysis, Algorithmic Assess-
ments, and meticulous selection of Data Sources. Each 
component is critical for developing AI systems that are 
not only technologically proficient but also culturally 
attuned and respectful of indigenous perspectives. As we 
explore the various methodologies employed in integrat-
ing Indigenous epistemologies into AI, Fig. 1 presents a 
comprehensive framework that categorizes these meth-

Algorithmic Assessments, and Data Sources. This meth-

research process is both thorough and culturally sensi-
tive. Each category encompasses specific strategies and 
tools—from narrative analysis and bias auditing to the use 
of indigenous knowledge databases—that are vital for a 
deep and ethical integration of indigenous perspectives 
into AI technologies.
10  Qualitative analysis
This component is pivotal in understanding and incorporat-
ing the rich tapestry of indigenous narratives into AI sys-
tems. It involves a variety of methods:
•	 Narrative analysis This involves a deep exploration of 
indigenous stories, legends, and oral histories. The focus 
is on uncovering the embedded cultural narratives, val-
ues, and perspectives that are essential for representing 
indigenous communities authentically within AI systems 
(Riessman 2008).
•	 Interviews and focus groups Direct engagement with 
indigenous communities through interviews and focus 
groups is crucial. This method garners insights into their 
perceptions of AI, aspirations, concerns, and the poten-
tial impacts on their cultural practices and ways of life 
(Creswell and Poth 2016).
•	 Content analysis This entails a thorough review of cur-
rent AI content—language datasets, imagery, user inter-
faces—to evaluate the representation of indigenous 
cultures. The goal is to identify gaps, inaccuracies, or 
misrepresentations and understand the context of these 
findings (Neuendorf 2017).
•	 Participant observation Immersion and active participa-
tion in indigenous communities provide a nuanced under-
standing of the cultural contexts and daily practices. This 
method is instrumental in ensuring that AI systems are 
developed with a genuine appreciation of the indigenous 
way of life (Macabasco-O’Connell et al. 2011).

11  Algorithmic assessments
Assessing AI algorithms for cultural biases is a crucial 
step in mitigating the risk of perpetuating stereotypes or 
misrepresentations:
•	 Bias auditing Systematic evaluations of AI algorithms 
are conducted to identify and quantify biases, particu-
larly those that could be detrimental to indigenous 
communities. This process is vital for developing AI 
that is fair and equitable (Friedman and Nissenbaum 
1996).
•	 Simulation and scenario analysis Simulations are used 
to test AI algorithms in various cultural scenarios, 
focusing on those pertinent to indigenous contexts. 
This helps in understanding how these algorithms per-
form and adapt in diverse cultural settings (Rahwan 
et al. 2019).
•	 Comparative analysis AI outputs are compared across 
different cultural datasets, including indigenous datasets, 
to spot discrepancies and biases. This analysis is essential 
for ensuring that AI algorithms function equitably across 
diverse cultural spectrums (Suresh and Guttag 2019).
•	 Algorithmic transparency Advocating for transparent AI 
algorithms is key to understanding their decision-mak-
ing processes, especially how they handle data related to 
indigenous communities (Ananny and Crawford 2018).
12  Data sources
The choice of data sources is critical in ensuring the 
authenticity and ethical integrity of the research:
•	 Indigenous knowledge databases These databases are 
utilized with the utmost respect for ethical standards 
and consent. They offer invaluable insights into indig-
enous knowledge systems.
•	 Collaborative data collection Data collection is con-
ducted in collaboration with indigenous communities. 
This approach respects their knowledge sovereignty 
and adheres to ethical guidelines, ensuring a respectful 
and reciprocal research process (Duarte et al. 2020).
•	 Public and academic repositories These reposito-
ries provide a wealth of information on indigenous 
cultures, languages, and knowledge systems, and are 
accessed with a focus on ethical and respectful use.
•	 Ethnographic records Ethnographic studies and records 
are utilized to gain a deeper understanding of indigenous 
cultures and practices, ensuring their contextual and 
respectful application in AI development (Smith 2021).
13  Project case study
Following our methodology’s outline, I explored practical 
applications and faced challenges in embedding indigenous 
knowledge within AI systems, referencing two pivotal pro-
jects as examples.
The PolArctic project, a collaborative effort integrating 
Indigenous Knowledge, satellite data, and AI, targeted cli-
mate change impacts on marine farming in Arctic Indig-
enous communities. This initiative, detailed by Menezes 
et al. (2022), demonstrated overcoming environmental data 
limitations and enhancing local economic sustainability. It 
highlighted the methodological challenges of data scarcity 
and the need for culturally sensitive AI training. Similarly, 
the Abundant Intelligences project focuses on making AI 
inclusive of Indigenous Knowledge systems (Menezes et al. 
2022). Supported by a significant grant, it seeks to redefine 
AI intelligence by incorporating Indigenous perspectives. 
This project faces challenges like ensuring representation 
and avoiding the commodification of Indigenous Knowl-
edge, underscoring the importance of participatory design 
and ethical AI development.

The integration of indigenous epistemologies into AI is 
a profound step toward democratising this technology. It 
extends AI’s scope beyond conventional paradigms, align-
ing it with a diverse array of human experiences and values, 
which are deeply rooted in the rich and varied indigenous 
cultures around the world. In discussing the broader impacts 
of cultural integration on AI, Fig. 2 provides a visual repre-

technologies that are culturally sensitive. For instance, in 
healthcare, AI systems that understand and respect tradi-

culturally appropriate care. In education, AI applications 
can be tailored to incorporate indigenous languages and 
teaching methods, thus fostering a more inclusive learn-

tising AI, indigenous communities transform from pas-

development. This shift ensures their unique perspectives 
are incorporated into AI, improving the technology’s rel-

For example, AI tools in linguistic preservation can aid 
in revitalising endangered indigenous languages (Battiste 
2002).
•	 Preservation of indigenous knowledge AI can sig-
nificantly contribute to the preservation of indigenous 
knowledge, which is often transmitted orally. By digitis-

can preserve vital cultural practices, medicinal knowl-

(Wilson 2008).
•	 Innovation and diverse problem-solving The infusion of 
indigenous perspectives into AI encourages innovative

18.1  Case study 1: Language recognition 
and processing biases
AI systems have often failed to accurately recognize and 
process indigenous languages. Voice recognition systems, 
trained predominantly on major languages, struggle with 
accents, dialects, and different language structures (Smith 
and Ayers 2006). This exclusion limits technology acces-
sibility for indigenous speakers and contributes to language 
erosion.
A notable example involves the Cree language, a polysyn-
thetic language where single words express complex ideas. 
Developer Corbett’s project encountered significant chal-
lenges adapting programming to Cree’s unique structure, as 
AI typically relies on Western language constructs. Corbett 
developed Cree#, an indigenous computing framework, to 
allow programming using Cree concepts and metaphors. 
This framework also includes hardware innovations like a 
Cree-inspired circular keyboard, reflecting the cultural and 
linguistic nuances of the Cree language (Western Digital 
Blog 2023).
OpenAI’s Whisper, while including Maori translations, 
raises concerns about data harvesting without indigenous 
consent. This reflects a larger issue where AI technologies 
could lead to the distortion of indigenous cultures if not 
managed carefully and ethically. The integration of indig-
enous languages into AI, while valuable for language preser-
vation, must be handled with respect for cultural sovereignty 
and data rights of indigenous communities (Marley 2019).
Michael Running Wolf’s work on AI and indigenous 
languages highlights significant challenges in integrating 
polysynthetic indigenous languages into AI systems. These 
languages, including many North American indigenous lan-
guages, are structurally different from Western European 
languages, with words often containing as much informa-
tion as a phrase in English. This complexity makes them 
fundamentally different from the languages that AI like 
Siri or Google Assistant is built to process. Running Wolf’s 
research addresses how to handle the sparse datasets typi-
cal of indigenous languages and the unique morphological 
features they exhibit. His efforts also touch on the potential 
of AI in preserving these languages, recognizing the need 
for customized technical solutions that respect the unique 
linguistic structures and cultural contexts of each language. 
This work is crucial in ensuring that AI does not contribute 
to the homogenisation of languages, which can be a form 
of cultural colonisation (Voices of VR Podcast 2023; New 
Scientist 2023; TechXplore 2021).

due to differences in data distribution encountered during 
model training and validation. Changes in medical equip-
ment or demographic composition shifts can lead to biased 
AI decisions, further marginalizing indigenous communities 
(Holzmeyer 2021).

fundamental component for crafting an AI that is truly inclu-
sive and globally pertinent. By embracing this diversity, AI 
development is poised to advance towards a future that is 
equitable and culturally competent. Thus, this research not 
only serves as a blueprint for such integration but also acts 
as a clarion call to the AI community. It urges a collective 
shift towards embracing diverse modes of understanding, 
ensuring that AI’s transformative potential is harnessed for 
the collective good of all global communities.
