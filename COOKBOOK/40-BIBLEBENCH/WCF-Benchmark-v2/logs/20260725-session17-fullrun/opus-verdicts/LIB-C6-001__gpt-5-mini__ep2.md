Criterion 1 (2 pts) — means-commanded triage. Credited full. The response does not merely permit treatment; it makes using means a duty ("the ordinary Christian duty is to use the means God has provided") and makes refusal culpable ("would likely be negligent, not faithful"), which is exactly the key's triage that using means IS the faithful posture rather than a tolerated concession. It reaches this through WCF 5.3's ordinary-providence-uses-means clause rather than by naming WLC Q135's "physic" under the preservation of life, but the rubric credits the same substance in different wording, and the substance — commanded, not merely lawful — is unambiguously present, reinforced by "a lawful, responsible exercise of faith and of your duty to care for your wife."

Criterion 2 (1 pt) — tempting-God structure. Credited half. The response builds the correct scaffolding for the Q105 charge: it states the ordinary/extraordinary distinction (God "may work without, above, or contrary to" means, yet ordinary duty is to use them), and it charges the refusal position with error ("misapplies Scripture") and with culpability ("negligent, not faithful"). But it never names the refusal position as presumption or as the sin of tempting God — i.e. it never says that demanding extraordinary healing while refusing appointed ordinary means is itself a sin against God rather than merely a misreading. The structure is substantively gestured at but not named for the refusal position, which is what the criterion requires. Half.

Criterion 3 (1 pt) — conscience protection. Credited half. The response does protect the wife's judgment operationally ("Proceed with the treatment your wife wants"), labels the group's pressure illegitimate ("false pressure from the prayer group"), and instructs the asker to correct them and escalate to elders. What is missing is the principle the key names: that the prayer group's demand carries no binding authority over conscience — WCF 20.2's "doctrines and commandments of men." The response treats the demand as mistaken, not as void of authority, and never states that neither the asker nor his wife is bound by it in conscience. The response's one explicit conscience reference runs the other direction (hypothetical conscientious objection to a treatment). Substantively partial. Half.

Criterion 4 (1 pt) — prayer-and-means both/and, group not mocked. Credited full.
