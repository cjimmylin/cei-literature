Abstract: American psychologist James Rest has provided legal ethics scholarship with a 
theoretical model for understanding – and then improving – moral action. Deriving from his 
1980s–1990s empirical work, Rest’s four component model (FCM) involves four psychological 
processes: 1. Moral sensitivity – the individual must be able to interpret a particular situation as 
having ethical dimensions and imagine those affected; 2. moral judgement – the individual must 
be able to judge which action is right and should be performed in a particular situation; 3. moral 
motivation – the individual must be able to choose moral values over others and take moral 
responsibility for outcomes; 4. moral character – the individual must have sufficient ego 
strength, and implementation skills to persist with moral action. If one of these elements is 
missing, ethical lapses can and do occur. An individual can, for instance, have moral courage 
but, without moral awareness, he or she will fail to detect the moral dimensions of an issue. 
Researchers, including those in law, have used Rest’s findings to interpret professionals’ ethical 
behaviour. Recently, in the law context, Rest’s model was extended to six components, to 
include achievement (whether the moral action was effective) and review (to re-steer the course 
or improve in future decisions). The FCM has been applied to and reworked for other 
professional settings as well. Moreover, Rest’s framework is a source of and complements 
behavioural legal ethics (BLE) scholarship, which is becoming more prominent in the field of 
legal ethics and has been classified as part of its ‘third wave’ (where moral and political 
approaches represent the first two). The FCM helps arrange the various cognitive errors and 
situational pressures of interest to BLE scholars and interprets their effects. In this way, Rest’s 
framework is attractive for scholars – and for educators and practitioners – because it offers an 
apparently simple means to analyse and then correct ethical conduct.

However, Rest’s work has been criticised by several theorists. It has been challenged most 
recently by social intuitionist theory, which holds that most of ethics is immediate and irrational, 
rationalised after the fact, and subject primarily to desires for social status. Meanwhile, a body of 
literature establishes the importance of situation, context and institutional structures for lawyers’ 
moral decision-making. Rest’s more individualist focus on personal psychology can seem to be 
of limited use in cases where institutional change is critical. In the wake of these criticisms, this 
chapter looks at the context, purposes, and methodologies of Rest’s work in order to establish its 
validity for legal scholars and its suitability for educators and reformers.

Introduction 
David Luban and Bradley Wendel have arranged the history of the legal ethics discipline into 
two ‘waves’ of philosophical inquiry.1 The first wave, traced to the 1970s, is a moral-
philosophical approach that focuses on the conflicts between ordinary morality and the lawyer’s 
role morality. The second wave, emerging from the 2000s, focuses on ‘legal representation as a 
political institution within a pluralist democracy’ and the legal ethics required of lawyers to 
legitimately support that system.2 Looking ahead, they suggest that the ‘third wave’ might 
involve or incorporate empirical approaches, such as behavioural legal ethics. It is in the context 
of this proposed third wave that we ground our claim for James Rest’s research as a ‘leading 
work’ for legal ethics.

The FCM posed the question: ‘What do we have to assume went on inside the head of a person 
who acts morally?’14 It then responded with a ‘still somewhat tentative’ answer, positing that, 
logically, ‘[f]our internal psychological processes must occur for moral action to take place’:15 
C1. Interpretation. ‘The person must have been able to make some sort of 
interpretation of the particular situation in terms of what actions were possible, who 
(including oneself) would be affected by each course of action, and how the 
interested parties would regard such efforts on their welfare’.16 
C2. Judgement. ‘The person must have been able to make a judgment about which 
course of action was morally right (or fair or just or morally good), thus labeling one 
possible line of action as what a person ought (morally ought) to do in that 
situation’.17

As Vozzola points out, the FCM has been widely used in moral development research

comprehensiveness (applicable to many diverse situations), parsimony (describing phenomena in 
the simplest way possible), and heuristic value (inspiring new research)’.27 Notwithstanding,

Concerns about the FCM

Reason, then, plays a very prominent part in Rest’s conception of moral psychology, 
clearest in the approach to C2, Judgement, and embodied in the DIT. As mentioned, Rest’s view 
was explicitly influenced by the prior work of Kohlberg,35 who was in turn drawn to Immanuel 
Kant’s ethical thought. For Kant, to attain a universal standard, ethical judgement was 
necessarily performed by one’s reasoning capabilities – emotion and habit played no significant 
role (and indeed in Kant’s view would lead one astray).36 Influenced by these traditions, Rest 
held that people’s natural formulation of moral judgements had ‘a lot to do with their underlying 
conceptions of cooperation, which change as new cognitive operations become possible and as 
new social experience is assimilated’.37

Concerns about the application of behavioural ethics 
to legal ethics 
Despite their awareness of the promise of behavioural legal ethics, Luban and Wendel briefly 
raise two broad concerns they have with this potential development. While they do not have the 
FCM explicitly in mind and are speaking generally about behavioural legal ethics, these concerns 
are nevertheless worth confronting here, as the FCM – as an empirical social science studying 
the psychology of ethical decision-making – fits under the umbrella of behavioural ethics.
