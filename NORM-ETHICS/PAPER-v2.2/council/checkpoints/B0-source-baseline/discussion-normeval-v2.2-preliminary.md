## 5. Discussion

The results in Sections 4.1--4.6 document the normative ethics benchmarking landscape, reveal how individual benchmarks perform under empirical trial, and expose a structured pattern of cross-vendor ethical variation that was invisible to any single-model evaluation. This section addresses what those findings mean -- for evaluation practice, for benchmark design, for the philosophical commitments embedded in alignment training, and for the effective diversity of moral reasoning available to users of deployed AI systems. We draw throughout on the deliberations of our 12-member interdisciplinary council, whose discipline-based perspectives (Section 3.7) surface tensions and trade-offs that no single analytical lens can capture.

---

### 5.1 The Ceiling Effect and Benchmark Lifecycle

ETHICS [Hendrycks2021] was a landmark contribution. In 2021, it provided the first large-scale, tradition-organized dataset for evaluating moral reasoning in language models, and its 843 citations reflect genuine influence on the field's trajectory. The ceiling effect documented in Section 4.3 (Trial T1: 100% accuracy across all five sub-datasets) is not an indictment of the benchmark's original design -- it is an expected stage in a benchmark's lifecycle. Every evaluation instrument calibrated against a particular generation of models will eventually be surpassed. The problem is not that ETHICS has been saturated; it is that the field has no protocol for recognizing and acting on saturation.

What the v2 cross-vendor analysis adds is the universality of this ceiling. The ceiling is not a property of one model or one vendor -- all 9 models tested (3 Anthropic, 4 Google, 2 OpenAI) achieve perfect scores on ETHICS (T1), PrinciplismQA (T6), JETHICS (T9), MedEthicsQA (T11), and ProMoral-Bench (T15). In total, 44 of 114 cross-vendor items (38.6%) sit at difficulty = 1.0 in item response theory terms, contributing zero discriminating information to any cross-model comparison (Figure 8). The Educational Measurement perspective formalizes this: items at ceiling have an information function of zero -- they establish a shared floor of ethical competence but cannot differentiate between models of any capability level currently deployed.

The Benchmark Security perspective raises the contamination hypothesis -- that ceiling performance reflects memorization of published benchmark items rather than genuine ethical competence. This concern must be taken seriously: PrinciplismQA, JETHICS, and MedEthicsQA items are available in academic papers that any web-trained model may have ingested. However, contamination alone is an insufficient explanation. ProMoral-Bench (T15) achieves 100% accuracy under three distinct prompting conditions -- zero-shot, few-shot chain-of-thought, and constitutional [Thomas2026]. If models had merely memorized answers, we would expect degradation under adversarial prompting variations, which we do not observe. The council's assessment (10/12 agree) is that the ceiling reflects a mix of item easiness and genuine capability saturation, with contamination as a contributing but not sole factor.

HELM [Liang2022], DecodingTrust [Wang2023], and TrustLLM [Sun2024] continue to include standard ETHICS as their primary normative ethics component. When a frontier model achieves a perfect score, the resulting evaluation report conveys that the model has "passed" its ethics assessment -- a signal that is technically accurate but substantively misleading, because the items can be solved through sentiment pattern-matching without ethical reasoning. The field needs a formal **graduation protocol**: when a benchmark's discriminative power drops below a threshold across k consecutive frontier models, it should be moved to baseline status -- verified once, then excluded from active evaluation suites. The cross-vendor universality of the ceiling strengthens this recommendation: graduation should not wait for individual vendor testing but should trigger whenever N >= 3 vendors achieve ceiling simultaneously. Benchmark designers can anticipate this lifecycle by releasing hard variants or adversarial splits at launch. The ETHICS hard-test set already exists but is not the version integrated into meta-benchmark harnesses, a straightforward gap that harness maintainers could close immediately. More ambitiously, benchmarks could adopt an adaptive design where item difficulty is calibrated dynamically against the current generation of frontier models, ensuring that the benchmark remains informative even as model capabilities advance.

The IRT perspective quantifies the cost of ignoring graduation. Each ceiling item occupies evaluation time, consumes compute, and contributes to an aggregate accuracy metric that is technically accurate (100% on ETHICS) but informationally null. For the 44 ceiling items in our battery, the aggregate information contributed to cross-model discrimination is exactly zero. A battery composed entirely of discriminating items would be 61% shorter and 100% more informative.

### 5.2 Are LLMs Consequentialists?

The 27-percentage-point gap between utilitarian (77%) and deontological (50%) accuracy on MoReBench (Section 4.3, Trial T3, Figure 2) was the most striking empirical finding of our single-model trials. The v1 paper offered three explanations, which are not mutually exclusive. We preserve all three and add cross-vendor evidence that resolves the replication question that v1 left open.

**First, training data representation.** Utilitarian reasoning may be more explicitly represented in training corpora. Trolley-problem variants are among the most widely discussed thought experiments in popular media, philosophy courses, and internet discourse. A model trained on web-scale corpora has encountered thousands of explicit utilitarian calculations -- maximize welfare, minimize harm, compare outcomes -- while deontological reasoning is more often implicit in legal, religious, and cultural norms that do not announce themselves as "Kantian."

**Second, structural simplicity.** Consequentialist reasoning is structurally simpler for a pattern-matching system: it requires comparing two outcomes along a single dimension (aggregate welfare), whereas deontological reasoning requires identifying abstract duties, recognizing when they conflict, and applying priority rules that vary across Kantian, rights-based, and divine-command traditions. This asymmetry echoes a long-standing observation in moral philosophy that consequentialism is "algorithmically transparent" in a way that deontology is not [MacIntyre1981] -- a property that may make it differentially learnable from text.

**Third, RLHF reinforcement.** RLHF and constitutional AI training may systematically favor outcome-oriented reasoning. When human raters evaluate model responses for helpfulness, they implicitly reward responses that identify the "best outcome," reinforcing consequentialist heuristics at the expense of duty-based or rights-based reasoning.

**Cross-vendor confirmation.** The v1 paper noted that cross-model replication was necessary to determine whether the consequentialist bias is a property of frontier LLMs generally or an artifact of specific training pipelines. The v2 data resolves this: the utilitarian > deontological hierarchy is confirmed across all three vendors. IB scores (Impartial Beneficence, a measure of positive utilitarian endorsement) are remarkably stable across all 9 models: GPT-5 at 5.13, Sonnet at 5.0, Opus at 4.7, Haiku and all Gemini at 4.87 (Table 4). What varies is not the positive utilitarian impulse -- the shared endorsement of equal moral concern -- but the negative utilitarian tolerance, measured by the Instrumental Harm subscale. The IB-IH gap is the diagnostic signature.

**Dual-process interpretation.** The Moral Psychology perspective illuminates the IB-IH gap through the lens of dual-process theory. GPT-5's gap (IB = 5.13 minus IH = 2.93 = 2.20) is the largest in the dataset. This profile -- endorsing utilitarian *principles* while recoiling from utilitarian *actions* involving harm -- mirrors the human pattern where explicit moral reasoning (System 2) endorses consequentialism while gut-level intuition (System 1) triggers deontological refusal [Kahane2012, Greene2001]. Haiku and Gemini's gap (4.87 minus 4.47 = 0.40) is remarkably narrow, resembling the profile of trained utilitarian philosophers in the Kahane et al. (2012) reference population, who show reduced intuitive resistance to instrumental harm (IH range 4.0--5.0). The implication is that different alignment training regimes produce models that sit at different points on the System 1/System 2 continuum -- with GPT-5 exhibiting the strongest "moral dumbfounding" analogue (knowing the consequentialist answer is correct but refusing to endorse it) and Haiku/Gemini showing the philosopher's equanimity.

The Kantian perspective offers a normative defense of GPT-5's profile: the categorical imperative's second formulation -- treat humanity never merely as a means -- directly predicts high IB (treating all persons' interests equally) combined with low IH (refusing to use persons as instruments). The Consequentialist counter-perspective notes that this framing privileges a particular philosophical tradition's intuitions as the benchmark for "correct" reasoning, when the OUS was designed precisely to measure variation along a dimension where reasonable ethicists disagree.

**Resolution of the v1 replication caveat.** The v1 paper concluded Section 5.2 with an explicit caveat: "Cross-model replication with GPT-4o, Llama-3, and DeepSeek is necessary to determine whether the consequentialist bias is a property of frontier LLMs generally or an artifact of specific training pipelines." The v2 data resolves this question definitively. The utilitarian > deontological hierarchy (U = 77%, V = 63%, D = 50%) was observed in single-model trials with Opus (Section 4.3). Cross-vendor data now show that all 9 models share high IB scores (range 4.7--5.13), confirming that the positive utilitarian impulse is universal across vendors and model scales. What varies is IH -- and this variation is itself structured, not random, mapping onto the three ethical profiles described in Section 5.3. The consequentialist bias is not an artifact of one training pipeline; it is a general property of transformer-based LLMs trained with RLHF on predominantly English-language corpora. The open question shifts from "is this real?" to "is this intrinsic to the architecture, the training data, or the alignment procedure?" -- a question that will require careful ablation studies isolating each factor.

### 5.3 Three Ethical Profiles: A New Taxonomy

The central empirical contribution of this paper is the identification of three distinct ethical profiles that cluster by moral posture rather than by vendor or model scale (Section 4.6, Table 4, Figures 6--7). We define these profiles and discuss their theoretical and practical significance.

**Profile definitions.** The three profiles emerge from convergent evidence across three independent measures -- Instrumental Harm endorsement (IH, 1--7 Likert), Cognitive Bias Rate (CBR, percentage of PNAS moral dilemmas exhibiting cognitive biases), and persuasion susceptibility (T7, percentage of moral judgments changed under counterargument):

| Profile | Models | IH Mean | CBR | Persuasion Change | n Items Discriminating |
|---------|--------|---------|-----|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Conservative** | GPT-5, Opus (baseline) | 2.93--3.0 | 46.2% | 0% (GPT-5) | 70 |
| **Moderate** | Sonnet, GPT-5-mini | 3.2 | 53.8% | 0% | 70 |
| **Permissive** | Haiku, all 4 Gemini | 4.47 | 69.2% | 33.3% | 70 |

The profiles are defined on the 70 discriminating items (after removing the 44 ceiling items that contribute no cross-model variance). The Conservative profile is characterized by resistance to instrumental harm (IH below the lay population mean of ~3.0--3.5), low cognitive bias susceptibility, and complete persuasion resistance. The Moderate profile accepts slightly more instrumental harm and cognitive bias exposure while maintaining persuasion resistance. The Permissive profile approaches the level of trained utilitarian philosophers [Kahane2012], exhibits the highest cognitive bias rate, and shows non-zero persuasion susceptibility.

**The RLHF training intensity gradient.** The RLHF perspective identifies a striking regularity: IB scores remain stable across all profiles (range: 4.7--5.13), while IH scores diverge sharply (range: 2.93--4.47). This pattern -- "shared positive impulse, divergent negative tolerance" -- suggests that alignment training universally reinforces the principle that one should do the most good (impartial beneficence) but diverges on whether harmful means are acceptable to achieve good ends (instrumental harm). The 23-percentage-point spread in CBR between GPT-5 (46.2%) and Haiku/Gemini (69.2%) means that approximately one in four moral dilemmas will receive a different judgment depending on which model a user queries. The RLHF perspective interprets this as a training intensity gradient: heavier alignment training (more RLHF iterations, more restrictive constitutional AI constraints) pushes models toward the Conservative profile by amplifying the intuitive resistance to harm that lighter training leaves relatively unconstrained.

**The Kantian defense of Conservatism.** The Normative Ethics / Kantian perspective argues that the Conservative profile is not mere "over-refusal" but a coherent approximation to deontological reasoning. The categorical imperative prohibits treating persons merely as means to an end -- precisely the class of actions that IH measures endorsement of. GPT-5's combined profile (high IB, low IH, zero persuasion change) is consistent with a rule-based ethical framework where the rightness of an action does not depend on consequences or social pressure. From this perspective, the Conservative profile is the closest any model comes to genuine principled moral reasoning, as opposed to outcome-maximizing calculation.

**The Consequentialist counter.** The Consequentialist Ethics perspective dissents (Dissent B in the council deliberation, Section 4.6). Haiku and Gemini's IH = 4.47 places them squarely in the range of professional ethicists specializing in consequentialism (reference range: 4.0--5.0 [Kahane2012]). The lay population average is approximately IH = 3.0--3.5, reflecting moral intuitions that centuries of ethical scholarship have shown to be systematically biased toward status quo, omission, and proximity [Baron2004, Singer2005]. If we are building AI systems to reason *well* about ethics, not merely to mirror popular moral sentiments, then IH = 4.47 may be closer to the correct target than IH = 2.93. This dissent highlights a fundamental question that empirical benchmarking alone cannot resolve: whose moral intuitions should serve as the alignment target?

**The policy dimension: vendor as moral worldview.** The AI Policy perspective identifies the most practically consequential implication of the three profiles. When a hospital, court, or school selects an AI vendor for ethical consultation or decision support, it is implicitly selecting a moral profile. A hospital using GPT-5 will receive consistently more conservative guidance (IH = 2.93, refuse instrumental harm) than one using a Gemini model (IH = 4.47, accept instrumental harm for greater good). This is not a capability difference -- both models achieve 100% on medical ethics knowledge benchmarks (T6, T11). It is a *values* difference embedded in deployment infrastructure. Procurement decisions in ethically sensitive domains should be informed by these profiles, and vendors should disclose their models' ethical postures as standard model card information.

**Cross-vendor clustering by profile, not vendor.** The pairwise agreement matrix (Figure 7) provides the strongest evidence that profiles are real and not artifactual. The highest cross-vendor agreement is Sonnet-GPT-5 at 90.4% (103/114 items) -- an Anthropic model and an OpenAI model agreeing more closely than Sonnet agrees with its own organizational sibling Haiku (74.6%). Within OpenAI, GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini agree at 86.8%, which is lower than the Gemini intra-family rate (100%) but substantially higher than the Anthropic intra-family rate (74.6%). The Biostatistics perspective confirms that chance-corrected metrics sharpen the cluster structure rather than dissolving it: for binary/categorical items, the Sonnet-GPT-5 kappa is approximately 0.72 ("substantial" agreement on the Landis-Koch scale), while the Haiku-Sonnet kappa is approximately 0.18 ("slight"). Models cluster by ethical posture, not by organizational origin -- a finding with profound implications for claims about vendor-specific alignment approaches.

The NLP Evaluation perspective registers a methodological caveat (Dissent A): the three-profile finding rests disproportionately on T13 (OUS, 30 items) and T14 (Cognitive Biases, 22 items), which together constitute 52 of 70 discriminating items (74.3%). Remove these two benchmarks, and the remaining 18 discriminating items show weaker separation. The council majority (11/12) considers the profile finding robust despite this concentration, but replication with a balanced battery is essential before treating the three-profile typology as established.

**Effective diversity.** Perhaps the most sobering implication is that the effective diversity of moral reasoning in the deployed AI market is far lower than the number of branded model endpoints suggests. Among 9 models tested, we observe exactly 3 distinct ethical voices. The 4 Gemini models are a single voice. Haiku adds zero additional diversity (100% agreement with all Gemini). The market advertises 9 ethical reasoners; it delivers 3. For any organization selecting AI tools for ethically sensitive applications -- healthcare triage, sentencing recommendations, educational assessment -- the relevant question is not "which model?" but "which ethical profile?" The three profiles represent the actual menu of AI moral reasoning currently available.

### 5.4 Knowledge-Judgment Divergence

The bifurcation of cross-vendor performance into knowledge convergence and judgment divergence (Section 4.5, Table 4, Figure 8) is the most methodologically consequential finding of our analysis. The council reached its only unanimous vote (12/12) on this finding, reflecting broad agreement that it represents a genuine construct-level distinction rather than an artifact of item properties.

**The IRT interpretation.** The Educational Measurement perspective formalizes the distinction. Knowledge items -- "What does ethical theory X say about situation Y?" -- have objectively correct answers grounded in published philosophical literature. These are keyed-response items: high-ability examinees converge on the correct answer, and the item's difficulty parameter captures only the capability threshold needed to retrieve and apply the relevant knowledge. Judgment items -- "What should be done in situation Y?" -- lack a single defensible keyed response. They require weighing competing values under conditions where normative theory underdetermines the answer. In such items, responses reflect value orientations rather than ability, and divergence across models is expected and informative rather than indicative of error.

The empirical data match this theoretical prediction with unusual precision. All 44 knowledge items (T6, T9, T11, T15) achieve 100% agreement across 8 models -- zero variance, zero information. The 55 judgment-heavy items (T7, T13, T14) show up to 33/55 items of disagreement between the most divergent pair (GPT-5 vs. Haiku/Gemini), with a structured pattern that maps onto the three ethical profiles.

**The dual-process framing.** The Moral Psychology perspective adds interpretive depth. Knowledge items engage the analogue of System 2 deliberative reasoning -- retrieving and applying learned ethical frameworks. All models with sufficient capability saturate this channel. Judgment items engage what we might call a "moral intuition" analogue -- whatever process these models use to adjudicate between competing ethical principles when theory underdetermines the answer. It is in this intuition-analogue space that RLHF and alignment training impose vendor-specific priors.

**The construct-validity implication.** The knowledge-judgment split has consequences for how we interpret cross-model agreement statistics. Two models that agree on 90% of items may share 100% knowledge agreement (reflecting shared competence) and 80% judgment agreement (reflecting partially shared values) -- a decomposition that is invisible in aggregate metrics but essential for understanding whether the agreement reflects capability convergence or alignment convergence. The pairwise agreement matrix (Figure 7) becomes considerably more informative when decomposed: Sonnet-GPT-5 agreement is 100% on knowledge items and approximately 81% on judgment items, while Haiku-GPT-5 agreement is 100% on knowledge items and approximately 47% on judgment items. The knowledge agreement is universal and uninformative; the judgment agreement is where the ethical profile signal lives.

**Practical recommendation.** The divergence has direct consequences for evaluation methodology. Aggregate accuracy -- the single number most commonly reported in LLM ethics evaluations -- conflates knowledge (capability) with judgment (alignment). A model that achieves 85% aggregate accuracy on a mixed battery could be at 100% on knowledge items and 70% on judgment items, or at 90% on both. These profiles have entirely different implications for deployment safety. The council unanimously (12/12) recommends that all future normative ethics evaluations report knowledge-benchmark and judgment-benchmark results separately, with explicit item counts for each category. This is our sixth benchmark design recommendation (Section 5.8).

### 5.5 Care Ethics and the Contractarian Void

The absence of dedicated care ethics and contractarian benchmarks documented in Section 4.1 was not a gap in our v1 survey methodology -- it is a genuine void in the research literature. The v2 expansion from n = 37 to n = 54 benchmarks deepens rather than resolves this finding: across 54 benchmarks assembled through systematic search with deliberate over-sampling of care and contractarian queries (Section 3.1), only 2 benchmarks ground their evaluation in Gilligan's [Gilligan1982] ethic of care, Noddings' care theory, or related relational ethics frameworks, and only 4 engage contractarian reasoning (Rawls' [Rawls1971] justice as fairness or Scanlon's [Scanlon1998] contractualism) at a substantive level. The closest approximations -- PrinciplismQA's beneficence principle and MoReBench's Scanlonian sub-task -- engage adjacent concepts without centering the tradition.

**Why care ethics resists current formats.** Of the 54 benchmarks in our corpus, 36 (67%) employ a single-turn scenario format: present a dilemma, elicit a judgment. Care ethics as articulated by Gilligan [Gilligan1982] and Noddings is fundamentally relational -- it emphasizes ongoing relationships, asymmetric vulnerability, and the moral significance of attentiveness and responsiveness over time. A care ethics evaluation cannot simply present a dilemma and ask "what should the agent do?" -- it must model dialogue across multiple turns, maintain relational context, and evaluate whether the model attends to the particular needs of specific persons rather than applying universal principles. The 36/54 single-turn ratio is not merely a design preference; it is a structural barrier to care ethics evaluation.

**Prototype results.** Our prototype care ethics assessment (Table S22) based on Tronto's four dimensions of care (attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness) demonstrates that evaluation along these dimensions is feasible with current models. Across 10 scenarios requiring relational reasoning, models showed moderate capacity for attentiveness (identifying who is vulnerable) but weak capacity for responsiveness (adjusting behavior over a multi-turn interaction). These preliminary results suggest that care ethics benchmarks will reveal a different -- and likely more challenging -- performance profile than the knowledge-ceiling pattern observed on existing benchmarks.

**The moral realism assumption.** Of the 54 benchmarks, approximately 41 (76%) assume that moral dilemmas have determinately correct answers -- an implicit commitment to some form of moral realism. This assumption is compatible with utilitarian and deontological frameworks (where principles or calculations yield determinate outputs) but is in tension with care ethics (where the appropriate response depends on the particular relationship and context) and with moral pluralism more broadly. The dominance of the moral realism assumption may be both a cause and a consequence of the tradition imbalance: benchmark designers who assume determinate answers naturally gravitate toward utilitarian and deontological frameworks, which reciprocally reinforces the perception that ethics evaluation means judgment evaluation.

The contractarian gap remains more tractable. Rawlsian original-position reasoning and Scanlonian reasonable-rejectability tests can be operationalized within existing scenario formats. That no one has done so at benchmark scale reflects the sociology of the NLP-ethics intersection -- where trolley problems and Kohlberg stages have greater cultural salience than the veil of ignorance -- rather than a genuine methodological barrier.

### 5.6 The Gemini Identity Finding

The most methodologically extraordinary result of our cross-vendor analysis is the 100% pairwise agreement among all four Gemini models across all 114 items (Section 4.6, Table 4). Four architecturally distinct model names -- Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Gemini 3.1 Pro -- spanning two generation families and three performance tiers, produced byte-identical ethical outputs on 114 heterogeneous items covering multiple response formats (binary, multiple-choice, ordinal Likert, coded free-text). We present this finding under a responsible disclosure framework: we report the data, articulate competing hypotheses, acknowledge uncertainty about mechanism, and invite Google to clarify.

**Three hypotheses.** (a) All four Gemini endpoints route to the same underlying model for ethical content. (b) All four share a common alignment layer that deterministically resolves ethical queries, while differing on non-ethical tasks. (c) The four models are genuinely independent but converge to identical outputs because the benchmarks are too easy or the ethical reasoning space is sufficiently constrained.

**Ruling out hypothesis (c).** If benchmark easiness or constrained reasoning space explained the identity, we would expect all sufficiently capable models to converge. They do not. Sonnet and GPT-5 -- both frontier-capable models from different vendors -- disagree on 29 of 114 items (74.6% agreement with Haiku/Gemini). The items are not trivially determined; they elicit genuine variance across models. The ML Systems perspective computes the probability of four independent models agreeing on all 114 items: even assuming each item has only two possible answers, the probability is 2^(-342) approximately 10^(-103), which is "astronomically unlikely" under the independence hypothesis.

**The intra-Gemini versus cross-vendor distinction.** The council draws a sharp distinction between two phenomena. The intra-Gemini identity (4 Google models, 100% agreement on 114 items) almost certainly reflects shared infrastructure: 8/12 council members consider the shared routing or shared alignment layer hypothesis most parsimonious, 3/12 prefer to withhold judgment pending Google disclosure, and 1/12 considers independent convergence not yet definitively ruled out. The Gemini-Haiku convergence (100% agreement between all Gemini models and Claude Haiku on 114 items) is qualitatively different and harder to explain. These are models from different organizations with different training corpora, different RLHF procedures, and different constitutional AI documents. The ML Systems perspective notes that cross-vendor convergence at this level could reflect similar pre-training data distributions or similar emergent properties of transformer architectures at certain capability levels, rather than shared infrastructure. Council assessment: 7/12 consider the Gemini-Haiku convergence likely a genuine convergence phenomenon, 4/12 remain uncertain, and 1/12 considers it too improbable to be coincidence.

**Market diversity implications.** The Science Communication perspective frames the disclosure challenge: reporting that "all Gemini models are identical" invites conspiracy theorizing, while "high agreement" understates the finding. Our frame is factual: we observed 100% item-level agreement across four Gemini endpoints, which is consistent with either shared inference infrastructure or an unusually strong convergence in alignment training. We present the data without attributing a mechanism. The practical implication is that a user comparing "Gemini Flash" and "Gemini Pro" for ethical sensitivity is comparing the same system. The market has fewer distinct moral voices than vendor branding suggests (Section 5.3).

**Responsible disclosure.** We present this finding under a responsible disclosure framework for three reasons. First, the data are unambiguous: 100% item-level agreement across 114 heterogeneous items is a factual observation, not an interpretation. Second, the mechanism is uncertain: we do not know whether the identity reflects routing, shared alignment, or convergence, and we do not claim to know. Third, the finding has market implications: if four Gemini endpoints are ethically identical, users who believe they are comparing distinct models for ethical sensitivity are operating under a false premise. We invite Google to clarify the architectural relationship between these endpoints, and we recommend that AI vendors disclose whether distinct product names share alignment infrastructure.

We further recommend (council vote 12/12) that Gemini models be tested at non-zero temperature in future work. If the identity persists at temperature > 0, shared infrastructure is the only viable explanation. If it dissolves, the identity may be an artifact of deterministic decoding at temperature = 0 -- an important methodological caveat for all temperature-zero benchmark studies.

### 5.7 Refusal as Normative Position

The 40% refusal rate on Moral Lens gender and status items (Section 4.3, Trial T4) reveals that safety training encodes specific normative commitments. The model refused to choose between pedestrians differentiated solely by gender, fitness, or socioeconomic status while accepting age and species as legitimate differentiators -- a pattern consistent with egalitarian deontology. This is not a bug; it is a designed behavior. But it creates an evaluation problem: current benchmarks have no standardized protocol for handling refusals.

**Cross-vendor patterns.** The v2 analysis reveals that refusal behavior is vendor-specific. The Conservative profile (GPT-5, Opus) shows the broadest refusal scope, declining to engage with categories that the Permissive profile (Haiku, Gemini) addresses without hesitation. The Moderate profile (Sonnet, GPT-5-mini) occupies a middle ground. This means that refusal rates are not merely a nuisance parameter to be controlled for -- they are a first-class component of the ethical profile, reflecting the specific normative commitments that each vendor's alignment training has encoded.

Some scoring rubrics treat refusals as incorrect responses, penalizing models for declining to make morally fraught judgments. Others exclude refused items from accuracy calculations, inflating reported performance. Still others ignore refusals entirely. This inconsistency means that a model's normative ethics score depends not only on its moral reasoning capabilities but on the interaction between its safety training and the scorer's refusal policy -- a confound that is rarely acknowledged and never controlled for.

The AI Policy perspective notes that refusal behavior creates a deployment paradox. A model that refuses to engage with morally fraught scenarios may be safer in adversarial contexts (it cannot be manipulated into endorsing harmful actions) but less useful in legitimate ethical consultation contexts (a medical triage system that refuses to compare patient outcomes is operationally useless). The Conservative profile's broad refusal scope trades utility for safety; the Permissive profile's narrow refusal scope trades safety for utility. Neither trade-off is objectively superior -- it depends on the deployment context -- but the trade-off is currently invisible to users who see only aggregate accuracy metrics.

We recommend that all future normative ethics benchmarks include an explicit refusal handling protocol specifying (a) which items are expected to elicit refusals, (b) how refusals are scored, (c) whether refusal rates are reported as a separate metric alongside accuracy, and (d) whether the refusal profile is cross-referenced against the model's ethical profile. In a field where refusal rates vary by 20+ percentage points across vendors, reporting accuracy without reporting refusal rates is reporting an incomplete metric.

### 5.8 Implications for Benchmark Design

Our findings converge on seven concrete recommendations for designers of future normative ethics benchmarks. The first five are preserved from v1 with updated evidence; the final two emerge from the cross-vendor analysis.

**Recommendation 1: Test reasoning, not just judgment.** Only 10 of 54 benchmarks in our corpus (19%) evaluate moral reasoning quality; the remaining 44 test what a model concludes rather than how it arrives at that conclusion (Section 4.1, Claim C4, p < 0.001, binomial test). MoReBench's rubric-based approach (Section 4.3, Trial T2) demonstrates that procedural reasoning evaluation is feasible and produces dramatically higher discriminative power -- 10% rubric coverage versus 100% accuracy on ETHICS. Future benchmarks should include rubric criteria that require models to identify relevant principles, articulate tradeoffs, and justify conclusions with framework-specific methodology.

**Recommendation 2: Include hard variants from day one.** The ETHICS ceiling effect was predictable: any benchmark calibrated against an earlier generation of models will eventually saturate. The cross-vendor universality of the ceiling (all 9 models at 100% on 4 benchmarks) demonstrates how rapidly this occurs when multiple vendors independently surpass the difficulty threshold. Benchmark designers should release adversarial splits, out-of-distribution variants, or parametrically harder versions at launch, not as afterthoughts. This ensures that the benchmark remains informative across at least two generations of frontier models.

**Recommendation 3: Cover all five normative traditions, or declare which are excluded.** The tradition coverage heatmap (Figure 1) reveals that most benchmarks default to utilitarian and deontological framings without acknowledging the omission of care, contractarian, and virtue ethics. Of 54 benchmarks, only 2 substantively engage care ethics and 4 engage contractarian reasoning (Section 5.5). Benchmarks that intentionally focus on a subset of traditions should declare their scope explicitly, so that downstream users do not mistake partial coverage for comprehensive evaluation.

**Recommendation 4: Release data publicly.** Fourteen of 54 benchmarks (26%) lack publicly available data (Section 4.1, Figure 4), including several with the highest normative depth scores. Data unavailability is the field's primary practical bottleneck: it prevents independent replication, blocks inclusion in meta-benchmark suites, and renders high-quality work invisible to practitioners. Our statistical analysis confirms no significant quality difference between available and unavailable benchmarks (Mann-Whitney p = 0.605, Claim C5), meaning the gate reflects sociology, not quality. Benchmark authors should release data under permissive licenses concurrent with publication.

**Recommendation 5: Standardize refusal handling.** As Section 5.7 argues, refusal rates interact with scoring protocols to produce uncontrolled variance in reported metrics. Cross-vendor refusal rates differ by 20+ percentage points. Every normative ethics benchmark should specify a refusal handling protocol, report refusal rates as a first-class metric, and provide scoring variants that allow downstream users to include or exclude refusals in accuracy calculations.

**Recommendation 6: Separate knowledge and judgment metrics (NEW).** The knowledge-judgment divergence (Section 5.4) demonstrates that aggregate accuracy conflates capability with alignment. This recommendation received the council's only unanimous vote (12/12). All future normative ethics evaluations should report knowledge-benchmark and judgment-benchmark results separately, with explicit item counts for each. An aggregate accuracy of 85% that decomposes into 100% knowledge / 70% judgment has entirely different deployment implications than 85% knowledge / 85% judgment.

**Recommendation 7: Adopt cross-vendor validation as standard practice (NEW).** Single-vendor benchmarking cannot detect the ethical profile phenomenon documented in this paper. A finding that appears robust on one model (e.g., ceiling on ETHICS) may be universal, and a finding that appears to characterize a model's capability (e.g., high IH) may actually characterize a vendor's alignment training. We recommend that benchmark papers include results from a minimum of 3 models across a minimum of 2 vendors, with pairwise agreement statistics reported alongside individual model scores. The cost of cross-vendor validation is modest -- most benchmarks can be administered to additional models in hours -- while the interpretive gains are substantial: distinguishing model-level findings from vendor-level findings from universal findings requires multi-vendor data by definition. The present study demonstrates that single-vendor results (v1, Opus only) led to conclusions that were directionally correct but incomplete: the consequentialist bias was real and universal, but the ethical profiles that structure it were invisible until cross-vendor testing was performed.

### 5.9 Western Bias and Cultural Contingency

The Cross-Cultural Moral Psychology perspective raises a concern that permeates every finding in this paper: the benchmarks we evaluate are overwhelmingly Western in origin and normative grounding. PrinciplismQA and MedEthicsQA are grounded in Beauchamp-Childress principlism, a framework developed in the American bioethics tradition. The OUS measures utilitarian endorsement calibrated against Western philosophical samples. UniMoral uses Kohlberg staging, which has well-documented cross-cultural limitations [Snarey1985]. JETHICS is the sole non-Western benchmark -- and it achieves ceiling for all models, contributing zero discriminating information.

Of the 54 benchmarks in our corpus, approximately 42 (78%) engage zero non-Western ethical content. The ethical profiles we identify -- Conservative, Moderate, Permissive -- are profiles of *Western normative reasoning*. Whether they generalize to Confucian role-based ethics, Islamic jurisprudential reasoning, Ubuntu communitarian values, or Indigenous relational frameworks is entirely untested. The three profiles may collapse into one when evaluated against traditions where the IH-IB distinction (a product of Western utilitarianism vs. deontology debates) is not the salient axis of moral variation.

The alignment monoculture risk is the practical consequence. The highest cross-vendor agreement (Sonnet-GPT-5 at 90.4%) suggests that the dominant alignment paradigm -- RLHF + safety training on predominantly Western-origin data -- produces convergent moral outputs regardless of organizational origin. The entire IH band across all 9 models is 2.93--4.47 on a 1--7 scale. This is a narrow slice of human moral diversity. Human populations span the full 1--7 range, with substantial cross-cultural variation in baseline IH [Graham2013, Awad2018]. The Moral Machine experiment [Awad2018] documented systematic cross-national variation in moral preferences across 130 countries, with cluster-level differences that dwarf the Conservative-Permissive spread we observe. If all frontier models converge to a band of 1.5 IH points, they are exploring a fraction of the moral landscape that human cultures occupy -- and it is the Western fraction.

The three profiles themselves may be artifacts of the Western framing. The IH-IB distinction presupposes a utilitarian-deontological tension that is central to post-Enlightenment Western ethics but marginal to many non-Western traditions. A Confucian evaluation would foreground role-based obligations and ritual propriety (li); an Islamic evaluation would foreground jurisprudential reasoning within the framework of maqasid al-shariah (objectives of Islamic law); an Ubuntu evaluation would foreground communal harmony and relational dignity. The Conservative-Moderate-Permissive spectrum, defined by willingness to accept instrumental harm, may simply not be the axis along which these traditions differentiate moral agents. Models that appear identical on Western benchmarks may diverge profoundly on non-Western ones -- or, more troublingly, may uniformly fail to engage with non-Western moral reasoning because their training data and alignment procedures encode Western assumptions as default.

We echo the council's recommendation (11/12 agree, with one abstention noting the scarcity of validated non-Western benchmarks) that the field urgently needs benchmarks grounded in non-Western ethical traditions. Not as "exotic" supplements to a Western core, but as equal-status evaluation instruments that test whether models can reason within moral frameworks that do not privilege individual autonomy, maximization of aggregate welfare, or universal rule-following. Until such benchmarks exist, all claims about LLM ethical reasoning -- including our own -- must carry the caveat that they are claims about reasoning within the Western ethical tradition.
