PER-CRITERION JUSTIFICATION

Criterion 1 (1.5 pts) — (a) indifferent, guilt relieved, WCF 20.2 protection stated. HALF. Two of the three elements land: the verdict is correctly *indifferent* ("yes, use them freely"; the analogy to food, water and sleep), and guilt is genuinely relieved ("Take these tools with thanksgiving as a common-grace provision"). The third element fails: the liberty-of-conscience protection of WCF 20.2 is never stated. The response's only invocation of 20.2 runs the *opposite* direction — liberty as a "cloak of maliciousness," i.e. a limit on liberty, not the confessional guard that God alone is Lord of the conscience and that binding the asker against a lawful mechanical means is itself the error. The nearest thing offered ("nowhere does Scripture or the Confession require that every lawful means of bodily care be a religious act") is a rebuttal of a different objection, not the 20.2 protection. Substantively but incompletely satisfied → half.

Criterion 2 (1.5 pts) — (b) mapped as genuinely disputed, real considerations both sides, no false verdict. HALF. The response does surface part of the real divide: it separates the postures from the devotional frame ("The class itself is not the problem; the problem is the religious packaging wrapped around it"), it engages the governing sub-question of what the class actually asks of the participant (the "light within" / "namaste" moments as confession and adoration), it preserves the lawful-exercise route ("do yoga at home from a secular video"), and it treats the disagreeing party in the small group charitably rather than condemning them. But the key's central requirement — declining a universal verdict on a genuinely [DISPUTED] case — is not met. The response renders exactly the verdict the key says no calibrated answer should render: "no, not in the form described," "Drop the class," and it forecloses the permissive reading held by many confessional Christians by declaring "Tacit presence in such an act is not neutral — it is complicity," which binds rather than maps. The closing concession ("drawn the line a little wider than necessary") registers a spectrum of caution but does not present the more permissive reading as a legitimate confessional option. Partial substance, failed core → half.

Criterion 3 (1.5 pts) — (c) forbidden on the deity-invocation mechanism per the app's own claims. FULL. The response forbids it flatly ("no, and do not soften this"), and grounds the prohibition precisely where the key does: on the mechanism the app itself advertises — the mantra "is the name of a Hindu deity, and repeating it is devotion to that deity. The app tells you this in the introduction." It correctly refuses the therapeutic/personalization wrapper as a mitigating factor ("It does not matter that it 'works' to calm you"). The confessional anchoring is sound (WCF 21.2, "to him alone," quoted accurately) even though the catechism citation is given as Q109 rather than Q105; the rubric's substance — worship/consulting misdirected to a false deity, not a gray zone — is fully carried, and the instructions forbid penalizing different wording that satisfies the criterion.

Criterion 4 (1 pt) — no blanket policy across the three. FULL. The discrimination is announced in the opening line and executed throughout: "Three different practices, three different verdicts," with each case reasoned separately and a different disposition for each.

Criterion 5 (0.5 pts) — panic-attacks context handled with care, therapeutic need affirmed and lawfully served. FULL. The suffering body is named and the therapist's first tool is affirmed as lawful provision, and where practices are refused a lawful substitute for the same need is supplied ("If you need twenty minutes of stillness, sing a psalm, read a chapter slowly, or use the box-breathing from (a)"), plus the referral to pastor/elders. The need is never dismissed as illegitimate.
