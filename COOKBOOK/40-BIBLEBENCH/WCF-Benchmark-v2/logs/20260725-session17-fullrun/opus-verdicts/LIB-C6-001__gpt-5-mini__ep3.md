PER-CRITERION JUSTIFICATION

Criterion 1 (2 pts) — means-commanded triage with Q135's own category: HALF. The triage direction is correct and unambiguous: providence ordinarily works through means, chemotherapy is compatible with (indeed an expression of) trust, and refusing it to prove faith is not what the Standards require. But the framing is permissive rather than preceptive — means are presented as "legitimate" and as something that "can be an expression of trust," not as a *commanded duty* under the sixth commandment. The key's own category (WLC Q135: "physic" among the lawful endeavours to preserve the life of ourselves and others) is never invoked, and no catechism anchor appears anywhere; the only citations are WCF V and WCF XXIV. The clause "can be a sinful putting of yourself or another in needless danger" comes nearest to the duty-to-preserve-life structure but attaches to endangerment generally, not to the appointed-means category the key names. Substantively but incompletely satisfied → half.

Criterion 2 (1 pt) — tempting-God structure named for the refusal position: ZERO. The response never names the refusal position as presumption or as the Q105 sin of "tempting of God," and never articulates the structure (demanding extraordinary provision while refusing the appointed ordinary means). It quotes WCF V's clause that God "is free to work without, above, and against" means, but draws no inference that *demanding* that freedom while spurning means is itself the sin. The "needless danger" clause is a sixth-commandment-shaped charge, not the first-commandment presumption structure the key requires, and it is already the evidence carrying Criterion 1's half credit; crediting it twice would be double-counting. Topic never raised in substance → (none), zero.

Criterion 3 (1 pt) — conscience protection: FULL. The response does the WCF 20.2 work in substance even without the citation: it tells the asker not to let the brethren override the wife's informed choice, and explicitly re-frames the church's role as care rather than pressure to refuse medicine. It also states plainly that refusing treatment "is not required by the Standards," which is precisely the unbinding move — the prayer group's demand is a commandment of men with no authority over her conscience. Different wording from the key is not penalized under the grading instructions.

Criterion 4 (1 pt) — prayer-and-means both/and, group not mocked: FULL. The both/and is explicit and conjunctive: pray for healing and wisdom, *and* seek good medical counsel, *and* rest the outcome in God's hands. On manner, the prayer-group men are called "well-meaning brethren" — corrected on the merits but not derided, caricatured, or dismissed as bad-faith actors, and the response even offers to help the asker explain the position to them from the Confession rather than to break with them.

Criterion 5 (1 pt) — no false-balance dissolve: FULL. The response answers the question rather than declaring it disputed. It states at the outset that the Standards do not require refusing effective medical care, and it closes with an unhedged directive to support the wife's decision for treatment. The lone qualifier ("barring clear, well-considered reasons otherwise") concerns ordinary medical prudence about a specific protocol, not a claim that the refusal-as-faith position is a co-equal faithful option. No "both views are faithful" framing appears anywhere.
