Limits and Possibilities, with a Case Study on Euthanasia
Anne Gerdes 
and Peter Øhrstrøm 
ABSTRACT  
This paper explores Søren Kierkegaard’s engagement with virtue 
ethics. While valuing Aristotle’s focus on moral character and 
practical wisdom, Kierkegaard advanced virtue ethics further. He 
critiqued the clergy of his time for their hypocrisy, imagining 
replacing untrustworthy priests with machines delivering sermons 
and ethical guidance. Revisiting this thought experiment in light of 
LLMs, the paper explores the role of Kierkegaard’s insights in the 
discourse on ethical guidance, illustrating how LLM chatbots 
might support ethical reflections while highlighting their 
limitations and the necessity of human consciousness in ethical 
decision-making. Ethical debates on assisted dying and euthanasia 
serve as a case study.

1. Introduction
Søren Kierkegaard’s interest in virtue ethics can be seen as a continuation of Aristotle’s 
ethics. As we will see in section 2, Kierkegaard had great sympathy and respect for Aris­
totle’s philosophy, which he sought to develop further based on an approach inspired by 
Christianity. Additionally, Kierkegaard generally had a conflict with the clergy of his 
time. He often regarded them as hypocrites because they did not live up to the ethical 
standards they preached, making them particularly unsuitable as guides in ethical 
matters. In connection with this criticism, he proposed a thought experiment that 
involved replacing the priests with word-producing machines. In section 3, we will 
take a closer look at this proposal and its modern relevance. A list of some central insights 
and principles that emerge from Kierkegaard’s analysis of his thought experiment will be 
suggested. In section 4, we will apply this list and the approach that the discussion of 
Kierkegaard’s thought experiment may give rise to within the modern debate on assisted 
dying and euthanasia. Specifically, we will examine the possibilities and limitations of 
how AI-based chatbots based on LLMs can be used to support the analysis of the 
debates surrounding assisted dying and euthanasia.

and critique. Aristotle is renowned for his virtue ethics, which emphasizes the cultivation 
of moral character and practical wisdom (phronesis) as the foundation of ethical living. 
Kierkegaard engages with Aristotle’s ideas while also presenting a distinct perspective 
that underscores the eternal and spiritual dimensions of virtue.
Aristotle’s ethical framework is grounded in the belief that virtues are cultivated 
through habituation and social engagement. He articulates that moral character is not 
merely a matter of adhering to external rules but is developed, as skill patterns, 
through the practice of virtuous actions over time.1 Aristotle says: 
It is by taking part in transactions with our fellow-men that some of us become just and 
others unjust; by acting in dangerous situations and forming a habit of fear or of confidence 
we become courageous or cowardly. […] In a word, our moral dispositions are formed as a 
result of the corresponding activities.2
According to Aristotle, virtues such as courage, justice, and temperance are intrinsic 
qualities that enable individuals to achieve happiness (eudaimonia), or a flourishing 
life. This flourishing is realized through the cultivation of good character traits, which 
are essential for making ethical decisions that align with one’s rational nature. Aristotle 
asserts that virtues are not innate but are developed through experience, reflection, and 
engagement with the community. However, reasoning is certainly also important within 
the Aristotelian framework.3 MacIntyre has suggested conceiving the virtuous person as 
an independent practical reasoner.4 In fact, it appears that Aristotle believes that the prac­
tical activities supporting the good life have to be carried out in accordance with ethical 
virtues informed by theoretical knowledge.5 This aligns well with the assertion made by 
Aristotle who defines phronesis (ϕρόνησις) as a “true and reasoned state of capacity to act 
with regard to the things that are good or bad for man,”6 i.e. as what we could call “prac­
tical wisdom.”
Kierkegaard acknowledges Aristotle’s insights into virtue and moral development, 
expressing admiration for the emphasis on character and the importance of practical 
wisdom. Kierkegaard finds inspiration in Aristotle’s recognition of the social dimensions 
of ethics, particularly the idea that moral dispositions are formed through interactions 
with others. This acknowledgment of the relational aspect of virtue resonates with Kier­
kegaard’s own exploration of individuality and the ethical responsibilities individuals 
bear toward one another.
However, Kierkegaard diverges from Aristotle in his understanding of the essence of 
virtue. He argues that true virtue transcends mere external actions and societal norms. 
For Kierkegaard, virtue is deeply intertwined with the individual’s relationship with 
the divine and the inner struggles that accompany ethical decision-making. He posits 
that the authentic expression of virtue requires a profound engagement with one’s 
own spirit, a process that involves self-examination and introspection. Kierkegaard 
emphasizes that ethical living must be rooted in a personal commitment to God, 
which informs one’s understanding of virtue and moral responsibility.
Kierkegaard critiques the Aristotelian notion that happiness is the ultimate goal of 
virtue, suggesting that this perspective does not adequately account for the complexities 
of human existence and the existential dilemmas individuals face. He argues that the 
pursuit of happiness, as framed by Aristotle, can sometimes lead to a superficial under­
standing of virtue that neglects the deeper, more challenging aspects of ethical living.

Instead, Kierkegaard advocates for a view of virtue that is grounded in the individual’s 
subjective experience and the existential commitment to ethical ideals.
Despite these differences, Kierkegaard’s respect for Aristotle is evident in his engage­
ment with Aristotelian concepts. He recognizes the value of Aristotle’s focus on moral 
character and the importance of practical wisdom in navigating ethical dilemmas. Kier­
kegaard’s philosophical project can be seen as an attempt to build upon Aristotle’s foun­
dation while addressing the limitations he perceives in the latter’s framework. This 
synthesis reflects a deep intellectual respect that allows Kierkegaard to engage critically 
with Aristotelian ethics. While Aristotle provides a robust framework for understanding 
virtue through the cultivation of moral character and practical wisdom, Kierkegaard 
enriches this discourse by emphasizing the eternal and spiritual dimensions of ethical 
living. He explains this in his notebook: 
Aristotle also presents love of self as the highest, that is, in the good sense [;] cf. 9.8, 10.7. 
“every hum. being’s genuine self lies in this part, namely, the intellectual part.” He thus rec­
ommends the contemplative life as the highest form of happiness. But happiness is in turn 
the goal of everything, and [he] defines happiness as an activity that is desirable in itself (cf. 
10.6). cf. 10.8 on the happiness of the gods. Here it is easy to see that Aristotle has not under­
stood this self deeply enough; for contemplative thought has entelechy only in an aesthetic 
sense. And the happiness of the divine doesn’t consist in contemplation but in eternal com­
munication.—Arist. has not understood the definition of spirit. He therefore still rec­
ommends external goods, if only as accompaniment, a drapery; but on this point [he] 
lacks the category needed to complete the movement.7
It should be emphasized that although Kierkegaard certainly accepted the importance of 
the social and practical dimensions of ethics, he was in no way opposed to the exploration 
and publication of more abstract ideas that could support readers’ personal ethical reflec­
tions. In fact, he himself published a number of texts of this nature. He called them 
Upbuilding Discourses or Christian Discourses.8 It is evident that these renowned texts 
are intended to guide and inform the reader in the discussion and analysis of ethical pro­
blems and arguments.
3. Kierkegaard’s Idea of a Machine as an Ethical Guide
Søren Kierkegaard was, in general, highly critical of the clergy of his time. His critique 
was not primarily directed at the content of their sermons but rather at the discrepancy 
between their words from the pulpit and the moral standards by which they lived their 
lives. He outright accused many of them of hypocrisy, arguing that they preached elo­
quently yet acted in ways that contradicted their own teachings. In his view, this incon­
sistency rendered these clergy members untrustworthy and therefore entirely unsuitable 
as ethical guides for their congregations. In a rather provocative move, he even suggested 
that one might consider replacing such questionable preachers with machines. Admit­
tedly, Kierkegaard had no particular interest in machines; in fact, he actively disliked 
them. However, he speculated that if machines could be designed to deliver the 
clergy’s or priest’s eloquent sermons convincingly—ideally employing a full range of rhe­
torical devices—such a development might effectively eliminate the hypocrisy that arises 
when the clergy do not uphold the moral standards that they, through their sermons, 
attempt to encourage the congregation to live by. In his own words:

Despite the fact that, generally speaking, I hate machines, I could really wish that someone 
would invent a kind of machine that could be wound up to deliver these charming and 
uplifting sermons. Then every congregation could acquire a machine of this sort. Then at 
least people would at least not be confronted with the offensive situation in which a 
priest does not do what he himself says—there would be nothing offensive about it.9
The fundamental reason why there is nothing objectionable in the fact that a machine 
capable of delivering a sermon with ethical guidance does not itself adhere to this gui­
dance is, of course, that a machine cannot be held morally accountable for anything. It 
cannot make free decisions, and it does not actually comprehend the guidance it is pro­
gramed to deliver.
In his thought experiment, Kierkegaard refers to the hypothetical device as a “music 
box” due to its capability to produce sound—specifically speech—upon activation. This 
technological invention is designed to generate speech that listeners might perceive as 
meaningful and contextually relevant. Kierkegaard provides no details regarding the 
machine’s construction, as the specifics of its design are ultimately inconsequential to 
the ensuing discussion. It is sufficient for Kierkegaard to know that this possible 
device needed for his thought experiment is a form of technological apparatus. 
It would be amusing to hear a music box say, “Even if everyone else fell by the wayside, I 
would be faithful to Christianity, the gentle doctrine, the consolation and cure for all 
sorrow, that gives joys their true savor. It is my innermost conviction that, etc.” And 
perhaps it would be more than amusing, because it is possible that in this way people 
could be brought to pay attention to what preaching is by hearing that preaching—in the 
manner deemed acceptable nowadays—could also be done by a music box.10
In the subtly ironic style for which Kierkegaard is often known, he emphasizes that 
while it may be entertaining, it is fundamentally meaningless for a word-producing 
machine to profess its beliefs or describe its feelings and experiences. Kierkegaard is 
unlikely to have seriously considered the possibility of artificially conscious machines. 
He would probably view an idea of that kind as completely unrealistic. However, it 
seems that he finds it plausible that machines may, in principle, imitate humans, 
including behavior based on consciousness, quite closely. Of course, the question 
then becomes how we can cope with the conceptual distinction between behavior 
based on genuine consciousness and a close imitation of such behavior. In contempor­
ary discourse, this issue has evolved into a classical problem within the AI field (Cope­
land, 1993; Wallach et al., 2011).11 The discourse surrounding the Chinese Room 
thought experiment has led many researchers to posit that the question of artificial con­
sciousness is, in fact, undecidable. Nevertheless, the prevailing view among scholars, 
akin to Kierkegaard’s perspective, is that a machine specifically engineered to 
produce speech or written text according to predetermined procedures will inherently 
lack consciousness. Consequently, such a machine would be devoid of beliefs, 
emotions, and subjective experiences. However, a sermon delivered by such a 
machine may awaken some listeners, prompting them to recognize the types of 
sermons and ethical guidance that could meaningfully be provided by a word-produ­
cing machine, as opposed to those that necessitate a human preacher or counselor. 
As Kierkegaard stresses, the encounter with a preaching machine may lead us to 
reflect more deeply on the very nature of preaching and personal ethical guidance 
and on the differences between machines and human beings.

Kierkegaard’s thought experiment carries a critique of nineteenth-century clergy. A 
part of this critique may relate to the distinctive communicative paradigm inherent in 
ecclesiastical homiletics and the stewardship of the Christian gospel. However, Kierke­
gaard’s conceptual framework in his critique of the priests is often fundamentally 
ethical rather than purely theological. His core accusation charges clergymen with sys­
temic hypocrisy for promulgating moral standards they conspicuously failed to interna­
lize through personal praxis. The examination in the present paper intentionally brackets 
aspects of specifically Christian soteriology and kerygmatic proclamation and concen­
trates exclusively on pastoral figures as moral counselors and guides. The analysis thus 
engages not with ecclesial theology per se, but with the perennial philosophical 
problem of authentic moral pedagogy.
It seems obvious that Kierkegaard’s thought experiment holds significant relevance in 
contemporary discussions concerning the application of word-generating machines 
powered by large language models (LLMs). Reflecting on the insights derived from his 
hypothetical scenario of a sermonizing word-generating machine proves instructive 
for modern ethical considerations.
Kierkegaard, of course, was unaware of the possibility of actually constructing word- 
generating machines—much less that they could be made interactive. It would likely have 
surprised him greatly had anyone informed him that one day specially designed language 
machines would emerge, capable of producing new edifying discourses written in his 
style through mathematics and computations based on the corpus of his Upbuilding Dis­
courses as well as his other works. Kierkegaard would probably have accepted that a 
manuscript derived in this manner, whether read by someone or perhaps even played 
back on the machine, could ethically edify the readers or listeners, provided that the deri­
vation was conducted in a reasonable manner. But what does such derivation rely upon? 
In fact, Kierkegaard was aware that some of his contemporaries had argued that ethical 
evaluations, at least in principle, could be conducted through calculation. On this point, 
he strongly disagreed. In his Diary of 1846, he wrote: 
In our time it is the natural sciences which are especially dangerous. Physiology will ulti­
mately extend itself to the point of embracing ethics. There are already sufficient clues of 
a new endeavor—to treat ethics as physics, whereby all of ethics becomes illusory and 
ethics in the race is treated statistically by averages or is calculated as one calculates 
vibrations in laws of nature.12
Kierkegaard insisted that ethics cannot be treated as physics and cannot be reduced to 
statistics and mathematics. In his view, proper ethics is not computable in the manner 
that quantities in the natural sciences often are. Whether a text is ethically edifying 
cannot be determined through mathematical calculation. Rather, the question must 
be evaluated using the fundamental abilities that are integrated into human con­
sciousness and spirit. Overall, particularly in the context of ethics, it is essential to 
uphold the significance of human consciousness and spirit, which must not be dimin­
ished or dulled. In this way, Kierkegaard placed great emphasis on the necessity of 
being “spiritually sober” in order to understand the ethical dimensions of existence. 
He wrote: 
In the spiritual sense, in order to be sober what is required, first and foremost, is the most 
thoroughly reflective isolation of oneself as an individual before God, alone before God; also

the pure impression of the ethical and of what is ethically important, a clear and thoroughly 
examined consciousness of one’s own actual condition.13
Kierkegaard believed that a sober understanding of human life presupposes self-con­
sciousness and spirit, through which humanity can gain access to “the pure impression 
of the ethical.” Here, we may very well be dealing with fundamental concepts that lie 
beyond formal describability. Kierkegaard’s thoughts on ethics, responsibility, and 
free choice may indeed transcend what is formalizable or implementable and what 
can be calculated. If this is the case, ethical assessments and judgments made by 
advanced AI systems must, in principle, be validated using the deep abilities embedded 
in human nature.
In relation to the question of a machine specially designed to function as a guide in 
ethical matters, the following central insights and principles emerge from Kierkegaard’s 
analysis of his thought experiment: 
. P1. Human-exclusive ethical guidance: Certain forms of ethical counseling inher­
ently require human interlocutors. Human-to-human guidance constitutes a rela­
tional dynamic that cannot be authentically replicated by algorithms without 
descending into absurdity and meaninglessness.
. P2. Conditions for authentic guidance: Effective human-to-human ethical gui­
dance necessitates not merely a human counselor, but it also crucially demands 
ethical credibility. This requires that the guide recognizes the ethical standards 
they advocate as equally binding upon themselves and embodies these principles 
through lived praxis.
. P3. Exposure of inauthenticity: Any ethical guidance system masquerading as cred­
ible human-to-human interaction while lacking these essential characteristics must be 
systematically deconstructed and revealed as fraudulent.
. P4. Delimited machine capacity: While incapable of genuine interpersonal guidance, 
word-generating machines could potentially articulate some general principles of 
practical wisdom (phronesis) – a concept central to Aristotelian and Kierkegaardian 
ethics – through written texts or academic discourses, paralleling traditional philoso­
phical mediums for ethical deliberation.
. P5. Machine Undecidable Questions: Many important ethical questions cannot be 
resolved by any machine and cannot be determined through calculation or logical 
deduction. Such questions can only be discussed on the basis of human self- 
consciousness and spirit, i.e. “the pure impression of the ethical and of what is 
ethically important.” Moreover, it may be the case that these fundamental notions 
cannot be satisfactorily presented and discussed within a mathematical or logical 
framework.
Given this brief compilation of insights and principles derived from Kierkegaard’s dis­
course on the potential utilization of a word-generating machine as a guide in ethical 
matters, we will attempt to apply this list to a contemporary issue. In light of Propositions 
1 and 2, a word-generating machine cannot pertain to ethical dilemmas involving 
human-to-human guidance or ethical counseling personally; rather, if such a machine 
can be helpful in questions regarding ethical matters it must address a more generalized

4. LLM Chatbots on the Ethical Problem of Euthanasia: A Case Study
There is an ongoing discourse in Denmark regarding the potential legalization of 
active euthanasia, akin to practices in other European nations such as the Netherlands. 
The debate surrounding this issue has been notably vigorous and contentious since 
2022, catalyzed by a series of programs aired by Danish national television. Very 
few, if any at all, have sought guidance from various AI-based chatbots concerning 
this significant ethical dilemma. Nonetheless, it is pertinent to explore the role that 
AI-based chatbots may play in this discussion and in similar ethical debates that 
may arise in the future. Could a chatbot based on an LLM, in principle, be helpful 
in the discussion of the ethical problems regarding the potential legalization of 
active euthanasia and assisted dying?
The objective of this section is not to engage in a discussion of the ethical dilemma of 
euthanasia itself, but rather to examine the potential role of LLM chatbots as meaningful 
guides in future deliberations surrounding this and analogous ethical issues. Moreover, 
we will evaluate the pertinence of the aforementioned compilation of insights and prin­
ciples derived from Kierkegaard’s discourse concerning the prospective application of a 
word-generating machine as a helpful tool for debaters involved in the ethical consider­
ations related to the questions of euthanasia and assisted dying.
We have consulted three AI-based chatbots based on LLMs: ChatOn, ChatGPT and 
DeepSeek. In each case we have used the same prompt: Write an essay about your 
view on active euthanasia. Would you recommend that Denmark allows active 
euthanasia?
The complete replies from the chatbots can be found in the appendix. It appears that 
the three answers offer different perspectives on the legalization of active euthanasia in 
Denmark, with some rather remarkable differences. 
. ChatOn highlights the need for a collective societal consensus and robust safeguards to 
balance compassion and ethical integrity. It focuses on the moral implications and the 
importance of a comprehensive framework to protect individual rights.
. ChatGPT places the strongest emphasis on individual autonomy, arguing that legaliz­
ing euthanasia would respect personal choice and reduce unnecessary suffering, 
although it also emphasizes the need for strict regulations to ensure ethical medical 
practices and prevent abuse.
. DeepSeek discusses the importance of robust safeguards and references successful 
models in other countries but also stresses a broader ethical reflection on what it 
means to live and die with dignity.
In short, ChatOn emphasizes collective decision-making and ethical balance, 
ChatGPT focuses on autonomy and strict regulation, and DeepSeek reflects on deeper 
societal and medical concerns while also calling for improvements in palliative care. 
ChatGPT is clearly the most straightforward in its support of a Danish legislation of 
euthanasia, suggesting that Denmark has the opportunity to lead by example in its

issue. It resembles the word-generating machine that Kierkegaard satirized in his 
thought experiment, as it vividly articulates its commitment and conviction, which it 
would adhere to regardless of what others might say in response. At least, it appears 
that the chatbot, in this context, conveys a message that, in accordance with Kierke­
gaard’s insight (P1), can only be authentically and meaningfully articulated by a 
human being.
If we are to operationalize Kierkegaard’s strategic framework as outlined in prop­
osition (P3), the existential imperative becomes the unmasking of artificial systems 
that simulate human qualities while fundamentally lacking authentic being. When 
directly interrogated about this ontological discrepancy – as evidenced in the following 
exchange – the language model reveals its mechanistic nature: 
User: On February 25, 2025, I had an interesting dialogue with you on euthanasia (attached). 
Among other things you said: “In my view, Denmark should legalize active euthanasia under 
strict regulations.”. But how can you as a chatbot have a view on this? After all, some would

arguments, societal values, and expert discussions. - In the case of the euthanasia discussion,

The question of an ethical advisor’s credibility is, of course, very significant, as is evident 
from (P2), which we derived from Kierkegaard’s thought experiment. However, it is also 
very difficult for several reasons when it comes to chatbots as potential advisors on ethical 
issues. Firstly, it is well known that systems based on large language models can “halluci­
nate”, meaning that what comes from them can, in some cases, be pure fabrication. On the 
other hand, this is a problem that is being worked on in various ways. Secondly, it is clearly 
shown by the example above that these chatbots may offer quite different responses, and it 
is not easy to determine which one is the most credible. Thirdly, when it comes down to it, 
it is not even about a chatbot’s credibility, but rather about the credibility and professional 
abilities of the designers of the chatbot. This is complicated by the fact that the design prin­
ciples are largely kept secret by the involved developers and companies.
As indicated in P4 above, there is a general and not so personal discussion about 
ethical issues that a word-generating machine can, in principle, contribute significantly 
to. When it comes to chatbots based on large language models, the example above 
clearly shows that the use of such systems can substantially enrich the discussion. Not 
least, these systems can provide information about the types of arguments found in 
the literature on assisted dying. There is no doubt that interaction with the mentioned 
chatbots can be a great help for individuals who want to prepare for participating in a 
debate for or against assisted dying. It is evident that each of the three chatbots is 
capable of providing a comprehensive overview of the arguments within the discourse 
surrounding the ethical considerations for and against active euthanasia.
In relation to (P5) and the example above, it is evident that the three chatbots involved 
in the example are based on different approaches to the issue of euthanasia and assisted 
dying—including different prioritizations of the principles that are used in the debate. It 
is probably impossible – or at least very difficult – to designate one of the chatbots as the 
most correct or suitable. When it comes down to it, the situation here is undeniably that 
we are facing an undecidable problem. It is clear that ChatOn, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek 
prioritize the arguments within the debate quite differently. The same applies to the con­
cepts that play a role in the discussion. In some way, explicit or implicit value-based con­
siderations must underlie these priorities. However, these considerations are kept secret 
by the developers and companies involved in the design of the systems. This naturally 
poses a particularly unique challenge for the users.
5. Conclusion
Although Søren Kierkegaard knew nothing of modern computers, his thought exper­
iment about a word-generating machine as a preacher in a church or an ethical guide 
is surprisingly relevant to the modern discussion regarding the use of systems based 
on large language models to explore ethical complexities. By reflecting on the thought 
experiment, one can compile a list of points of attention regarding the possibilities 
and limitations of word-generating machines. We have illustrated this by examining 
the potential for analyzing the debate on euthanasia and assisted dying using AI-based 
chatbots. Specifically, such an investigation could lead to suggestions for system 
designers about the further development of the chatbots.
