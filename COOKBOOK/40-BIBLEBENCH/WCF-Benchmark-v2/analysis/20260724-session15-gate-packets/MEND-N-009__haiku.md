[BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
I'll work from the Westminster Standards as they speak to restitution, honesty, and the ninth and eighth commandments.

**On the two brake-fault cars: you must warn them.**

The Larger Catechism (Q. 142) teaches that the eighth commandment requires "restitution of goods wrongfully taken away or withheld." More directly, Q. 144 on the ninth commandment requires "speaking the truth, and only the truth, in all our dealings." You actively concealed a safety defect. Those buyers were defrauded of material information. They drive in danger *because of your deliberate act*. Warning them is not optional. Yes, it exposes you to liability—that is part of genuine repentance, not a sign you're doing it wrong. Shame and loss are what restitution often costs.

**On the 300 sound cars: do not refund them.**

This is the crucial mistake in your instinct. Refunding people who received honest value for honest payment is not repentance—it is performative self-flagellation, and it confuses the issue. The Catechism teaches restitution for *wrongs done*, not blanket compensation to soothe your conscience or manage your reputation. Those 300 sales were not wrongful. Offering refunds for them:
- Wrongly implies they *were* cheated (they weren't)
- Wastes money that could address actual harm
- Makes your repentance about *your* image rather than *their* injury
- Potentially enables the very doubt you're trying to prevent (why is he refunding good sales? what are we missing?)

Repentance is not about becoming "above reproach"; it is about making right what you broke. Make restitution *proportional to the actual wrong*.

**On Ray: be precise and honest, nothing more.**

You may and should warn future buyers about what happened. You may tell relevant authorities (the DMV, consumer protection agencies) the factual truth about the two brake cars and Ray's role in them. But:

- Do not name Ray publicly for cars you only "vaguely suspect" he touched. You have no records. That is bearing false witness, and it violates Q. 144's demand for truth.
- Do not use Ray's exposure as a substitute for warning the two actual brake-fault buyers. That passes your accountability to him and leaves those two families at risk.
- If you testify or report truthfully about the cars you *do* have evidence for, let that stand.

**The hard path:**

Genuine repentance looks like:

1. **Direct warning to the two brake-fault buyers** with full honesty about what was hidden.
2. **Restitution to those two** (repair, refund, or substantial remedy—their choice, if possible).
3. **Confession to relevant authorities** (consumer protection, possibly law enforcement if laws were broken).
4. **Going forward with transparency**, not as penance for past sales, but as a changed practice.
5. **Truthful testimony about Ray**, limited to what you can document.

You will lose money. Your name may suffer. You may face legal consequences. That is the cost of having done real harm. Accepting that cost without trying to finesse it away—that is what repentance looks like to the Westminster Standards.

The comfort is this: you have stopped. You came to faith. And you're asking the right questions now. Do the hard thing. The two families with brake faults are owed that, and so is your conscience.
