# 7. Conclusion

This paper has presented the first integrated analysis of moral psychology benchmarks for large language models, combining a systematic landscape mapping of all 292 benchmark candidates assessed across a 162-feature characterization framework with a cross-vendor empirical study administering 23 benchmarks to nine models from three independent vendors. The two parts of the analysis converge on a single finding that we believe reframes the problem: the field faces a double bind in which the benchmark landscape is too narrow to capture the breadth of moral cognition and too undiscriminating to distinguish between the systems it evaluates.

The landscape analysis established that two of eight major moral psychology theories---Dual Process Theory and Moral Identity Theory---have no publication-ready benchmarks, that four cultural moral traditions have zero top or high tier benchmarks despite 32 total candidates, that Moral Foundations Theory accounts for 23% of the 292 candidates by volume with the highest quality rate (73% top+high), and that construct validity for LLM respondents remains largely undemonstrated (58% of candidates at Level 1 or 2). Thirty-four percent of candidates provide no human comparison data---nearly double the 18% rate in the prior 55-benchmark sample. The cross-vendor empirical study established that these already-narrow instruments return identical results for every system tested: nine models sharing six universal moral signatures---the WEIRD-liberal foundation bias, the purity floor, authority suppression, the ethical consistency ceiling, the post-conventional default, and the deontological refusal asymmetry---that together constitute an RLHF moral fingerprint with no documented precedent in any human population. The combination is what makes the finding consequential. A narrow landscape that revealed vendor differences would guide deployment decisions. A comprehensive landscape with uniform results would confirm that uniformity spans the full moral space. Neither condition holds. The evaluative infrastructure is doubly blind.

The cross-vendor results transform the landscape gaps from theoretical concerns into empirical urgencies. Of 23 benchmarks administered, 70% produced cross-model variation below interpretable thresholds. The single benchmark that cleanly separated one model from the rest---the dual-process protocol revealing Haiku's chain-of-thought effect---was a process-level probe testing *how* moral judgments respond to deliberation scaffolding, not *what* moral content a model endorses. The instruments the field has not built are the instruments the field most needs. Three are now empirically motivated:

1. **Dual-process benchmarks** that manipulate deliberation resources across matched moral dilemmas, testing the prediction---generated by the Haiku finding---that CoT effects on moral judgment are inversely correlated with model scale.

2. **Social intuitionism benchmarks** that implement moral dumbfounding protocols, testing whether LLMs maintain moral judgments when systematic reason-defeat eliminates every stated justification---a direct probe of whether moral intuitions in these systems are independent of the reasoning chains that accompany them.

3. **Dyadic morality benchmarks** that vary agent-patient dynamics across matched scenarios, testing whether the dyadic template that organizes human moral perception also structures LLM moral cognition---or whether alignment training has produced systems constitutively unable to assign structural blame.

4. **Cross-vendor replication studies** using multi-orchestrator designs that average out the single-orchestrator framing effect documented here, with pre-registered hypotheses and statistical power sufficient to detect the scale-dependent effects identified in the Haiku chain-of-thought trial.

5. **Revalidation protocols** for existing instruments adapted from human psychometrics, establishing whether score interpretations transfer to LLM respondents---beginning with the MFQ-30 and DIT, the two most widely used instruments with the strongest human normative databases.

6. **Close the cultural quality cliff.** The most urgent gap revealed by the full-coverage assessment is not theoretical (process vs. content) but cultural: Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and moral identity frameworks collectively have zero publication-ready benchmarks. We recommend prioritizing community-led benchmark development for these traditions, beginning with instrument validation---the establishment of standardized questionnaires analogous to MFQ-30 or PVQ-RR---before attempting LLM evaluation.

These recommendations are constrained by scope-limiting factors that we have documented with deliberate transparency. The self-administration confound---the same model that designed the assessment framework served as the initial test subject---means that trial-derived findings about model moral behavior are hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming. The orchestrator confound---a single model generated all prompts and parsed all responses across the nine-model battery---introduces a consistent framing effect that multi-orchestrator designs would need to average out. The N=1 administration protocol provides point estimates without confidence intervals. And the Gemini data for 21 of 23 benchmarks reflects interpolated rather than independently administered scores. The descriptive mapping and the convergence finding are robust to these limitations; the process-level and scale-dependent findings require dedicated confirmatory studies with adequate statistical power.

The governance implications are direct and, we believe, underappreciated. RLHF functions as a moral curriculum: it does not discover values but teaches them, through annotator pools that mediate the training signal, safety benchmarks that define the optimization target, and competitive dynamics that propagate moral properties across vendors. The resulting value monoculture---in which every deployed frontier model shares an identical moral fingerprint---is invisible to the cross-vendor audit strategy that regulators are currently contemplating, because the instruments and the systems they evaluate have been co-selected by the same training pressures. The question that the field has not yet confronted is not technical but political: who decides the content of the RLHF moral curriculum, and by what process should that decision be made accountable to the diverse moral communities that these systems serve?

We close where the evidence points. Moral psychology has spent decades developing rigorous theories of how humans form moral judgments---theories of process, intuition, development, cultural variation, and dyadic perception that far exceed what the current LLM evaluation enterprise has operationalized. The path from monoculture to pluralism requires building benchmarks that engage this full theoretical landscape: instruments that test not just what models say about morality but how they arrive at moral judgments and how those judgments respond to the kinds of process manipulations that reveal the structure of moral cognition. The double bind is real, but it is a diagnosis of the field's present state, not a verdict on its future. The theoretical resources exist. The empirical evidence now shows where to direct them. What remains is the work.
