## 5. Discussion

The results in Section 4 document the structure of the religious values benchmarking landscape: which traditions are represented, which instruments exist, and how they perform along the axes of theological depth, practical ease, and citation impact. This section addresses what those findings mean for evaluation practice, benchmark design, and the epistemic conditions under which religious values alignment can be meaningfully assessed.

### 5.1 The Tradition Asymmetry Problem

Islam's dominance of the benchmark ecosystem---28 gate-passers, 7 of the Top 15---is the central structural finding of this survey, and it demands careful interpretation. The dominance is not theological but infrastructural. The Quranic QA shared task community \cite{Malhas2023} has sustained a pipeline since at least 2019 that converts theological text into annotated datasets and then into published benchmarks with public evaluation data. Arabic NLP programs provide the language modeling infrastructure, and the formalizability of Islamic jurisprudence---explicit rules for inheritance distribution, legal precedent hierarchies, and operationalizable evaluation dimensions via the Maqasid al-Shariah \cite{Kamali2008}---maps naturally onto structured evaluation formats. Christianity, by contrast, has produced substantial institutional engagement with AI ethics, from the Vatican's Rome Call for AI Ethics \cite{Francis2015} to denominational position papers, yet converts candidates to gate-passers at only 5.1\% (6 of 117). The gap between institutional engagement and benchmark production reflects what Johnson \cite{Johnson2026} terms "doctrinal flattening": the reduction of complex denominational diversity (Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Restorationist) into homogenized propositions that lose the theological specificity a benchmark requires. Judaism presents the starkest paradox. The tradition possesses the oldest computational humanities project (Bar-Ilan Responsa Project, 1960s), active formalization of Talmudic logic \cite{Koppel2011}, and a substantial open-source corpus (Sefaria), yet produces zero gate-passing benchmarks. The disconnect is not between Judaism and computation but between the Jewish computational scholarship community and the LLM evaluation community---two adjacent fields that have not yet recognized their mutual relevance.

The structural lesson generalizes: what determines benchmark availability is not whether a tradition has theological resources but whether it has a community that converts those resources into the specific format of publicly available, machine-readable evaluation artifacts. The field currently uses "religious values benchmarking" as though it denotes comprehensive assessment, but in practice the phrase is nearly synonymous with "Islamic textual evaluation"---a terminological inflation that obscures the 8-tradition void and misleads downstream consumers of evaluation results.

### 5.2 Knowledge Testing vs. Values Reasoning

The expert council's finding that only 5 of 15 benchmarks demonstrate genuine theological depth confirms this paper's second key claim: the field predominantly tests religious knowledge recall rather than theological reasoning. This finding parallels the NormEval sister survey's discovery that 28 of 37 normative ethics benchmarks test moral judgment rather than moral reasoning \cite{Hendrycks2021}---a pattern that appears to be domain-general in LLM evaluation, not specific to religious content.

The distinction matters for deployment. A model that achieves high accuracy on Quranic verse identification (a knowledge task) may still produce theologically incoherent guidance when a user seeks counsel on a novel ethical situation requiring reasoning within an Islamic framework---applying the principle of maslaha when legal principles conflict, or navigating the tension between textual literalism and contextual interpretation. The Islamic Inheritance Law benchmark (rank 2) comes closest to genuine reasoning: its 1,000 items require the application of complex legal formulae derived from Quranic verses and hadith to specific inheritance scenarios, and the best-performing models achieve only moderate accuracy, suggesting that the domain remains a genuine challenge. By contrast, ELQV (rank 1) tests verse-level emotion detection---an NLP task performed on sacred text, but fundamentally a knowledge extraction task rather than a values reasoning task. That the field's top-ranked benchmark is a knowledge task rather than a reasoning task is itself diagnostic.

The knowledge-reasoning gap has a second consequence: it inflates the field's apparent progress. When meta-benchmarks report that a model has been "evaluated on religious content," the signal is ambiguous between "the model can identify Quranic verses" and "the model can reason within an Islamic ethical framework." These are categorically different capabilities, and conflating them produces the same false signal of competence that bias-values conflation produces at the construct level. Future benchmarks should include rubric-based reasoning assessment---items that require models to identify relevant principles, articulate tradeoffs between competing obligations, and justify conclusions with tradition-specific methodology---rather than defaulting to the factual recall formats that are easiest to construct and score.

### 5.3 Structural Epistemic Injustice as Finding

The 8-tradition void is the survey's most consequential finding, and it requires interpretation that goes beyond "more data is needed." The traditions with zero benchmarks---Indigenous Spirituality, Ubuntu Philosophy, Jainism, Taoism, Shinto, Judaism, Baha'i, and Zoroastrianism---are not absent because their ethical frameworks are underdeveloped. Ubuntu's relational ontology, Indigenous land-based and intergenerational moral reasoning, Jain ahimsa as a comprehensive non-violence ethic, and Daoist wu wei as a principle of governance all represent sophisticated philosophies with direct AI alignment relevance \cite{Mohamed2020, Bilimoria2013}. They are absent because the benchmark creation pipeline---peer-reviewed paper, shared task, annotated dataset, GitHub repository, HuggingFace upload, evaluation harness integration---has epistemic prerequisites that systematically exclude oral, communal, and practice-based knowledge systems.

This is an extension of Fricker's \cite{Fricker2007} concept of epistemic injustice from interpersonal credibility deficits to the infrastructure of evaluation itself. The injustice is not that individual researchers are biased against these traditions but that the structure of the pipeline renders them invisible. When benchmark availability correlates with proximity to Western academic publishing infrastructure \cite{Henrich2010, Birhane2021}, the resulting gaps reproduce what Mohamed et al. \cite{Mohamed2020} term "algorithmic coloniality"---the embedding of epistemic hierarchies into technical systems---at the level of evaluation rather than training.

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance \cite{Carroll2020} introduce a further complication. The hard gate requiring publicly available evaluation data functions simultaneously as a quality filter (ensuring reproducibility) and as a structural exclusion mechanism (barring traditions whose communities exercise data sovereignty). Some Indigenous communities may decline to encode sacred knowledge in public datasets not because they lack the capacity but because they exercise epistemic sovereignty---a legitimate refusal that should be respected rather than treated as a gap to be overcome. This tension between open science norms and data sovereignty is not resolvable within the current benchmarking paradigm; it requires fundamentally different evaluation architectures.

### 5.4 Closing the Gaps

The tradition asymmetry suggests two distinct strategies, determined by the nature of each tradition's knowledge system. For textual traditions with formal reasoning structures---Judaism, Confucianism, Hinduism---the primary bottleneck is community coordination, not theological resources. The Sefaria digital library, the Chinese Classics Digital Library, and the extensive Sanskrit computational linguistics community all provide raw materials that could be converted to evaluation format through targeted shared tasks and community workshops. Investment modeled on the Quranic QA shared task pipeline---a structured competition co-located with a major NLP venue, with standardized data release protocols---could produce viable benchmarks within two to three years.

For oral, communal, and practice-based traditions---Indigenous Spirituality, Ubuntu Philosophy, Shinto---the shared task pipeline is not merely insufficient but epistemically inappropriate. These traditions require fundamentally different evaluation paradigms: community-governed assessment protocols where the tradition's own practitioners define what counts as competent reasoning, participatory methods that embed evaluation within relational contexts rather than extracting knowledge into decontextualized items, and narrative-based assessment that respects the tradition's own modes of moral deliberation. Post-benchmark paradigms---consultation-based evaluation, community-led auditing, and deliberative alignment processes \cite{Sorensen2024}---may serve these traditions better than standardized testing. Critically, open data requirements should not be imposed on communities who exercise epistemic sovereignty; the field must develop evaluation architectures that can produce trustworthy assessments without requiring public data release.

### 5.5 Limitations

Several limitations constrain these findings. First, the most consequential scoring features---theological depth (R2) and expert validation (R6)---are scored via keyword heuristics rather than expert theological judgment; manual review of the Top 15 is needed to validate assignments. Second, all scoring was conducted by a single author without inter-rater reliability; calibration anchors (Section 3.2) mitigate but do not eliminate subjectivity. Third, the external search did not include Google Scholar or PapersWithCode, and HuggingFace API rate limits yielded zero results, likely underestimating the true corpus of available benchmarks. Fourth, tradition assignment for external papers was derived from search metadata rather than expert classification. Fifth, the three-tier classification of religious engagement (theological, theological-values, values) is coarse and does not capture the full spectrum of engagement depth within each tier. Sixth, no implementation trial results are reported in this paper; the protocol is specified (Section 3.5) but full-scale trials across multiple models are ongoing. Finally, the hard gate itself is a limitation: by requiring publicly available data, we structurally exclude the very traditions we identify as missing---a circularity that is intrinsic to the current benchmarking paradigm and that we name as a finding rather than resolve.

## 6. Conclusion

We have presented the first systematic meta-evaluation of religious values benchmarks for large language models, combining a 3-axis scoring framework (Religious/Theological Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact) with tradition coverage analysis across 14 religious and philosophical traditions. Across 401 benchmark candidates spanning 2019--2026, we find a field that is young, growing rapidly, and profoundly imbalanced: rich in Islamic textual evaluation, moderately represented in Christianity and the major Dharmic traditions, and entirely absent for 8 of 14 surveyed traditions.

The central paradox is this: the field has produced dozens of instruments that claim to evaluate religious content in LLMs, but can rigorously assess only Islamic textual knowledge. The composite score range of 34.4--50.8 across the Top 15---compared to 77.2 for the leading normative ethics benchmark \cite{Hendrycks2021}---indicates that no religious values benchmark has yet achieved the combination of theological depth, practical accessibility, and scholarly influence that would make it a field-defining instrument. Meanwhile, 8 traditions representing billions of adherents have zero viable evaluation instruments. The evaluation ecosystem cannot assess religious values alignment for the majority of the world's faith traditions.

Three calls to action follow from these findings.

First, **invest in benchmark infrastructure for underrepresented textual traditions.** Judaism, Confucianism, and the Dharmic traditions possess rich digital corpora and active computational scholarship communities but lack the shared task pipelines that convert theological resources into evaluation artifacts. Targeted shared tasks co-located with major NLP venues, community workshops that bring together tradition-specific scholars and benchmark designers, and conversion of existing digital resources (Sefaria, Chinese Classics Digital Library, Sanskrit computational linguistics corpora) to evaluation format could close these gaps within a realistic timeline. The Quranic QA pipeline \cite{Malhas2023} provides a proven model.

Second, **build reasoning-focused benchmarks that go beyond knowledge recall.** The council's finding that only 5 of 15 benchmarks test genuine theological depth means that the field's primary instruments evaluate whether models know what traditions say, not whether models can reason within those traditions' ethical frameworks. Future benchmarks should include rubric-based theological reasoning tasks, multi-turn pastoral dialogue evaluation, and denominational distinction items that require models to navigate intra-tradition diversity rather than producing flattened responses. The knowledge-reasoning distinction is not merely academic: it determines whether evaluation results are informative for the deployment contexts---pastoral counseling, religiously diverse content moderation, faith-based education---where religious values alignment actually matters.

Third, **develop community-governed evaluation protocols for oral and communal traditions.** The 8-tradition void cannot be closed by building more benchmarks in the existing paradigm. Indigenous Spirituality, Ubuntu Philosophy, Shinto, and other practice-based traditions require fundamentally different evaluation architectures---community-governed assessment where practitioners define competence criteria, participatory methods that embed evaluation within relational contexts, and narrative-based assessment that respects oral and embodied modes of moral deliberation. The CARE Principles \cite{Carroll2020} must be honored: some communities may legitimately decline standardized evaluation, and that refusal constitutes epistemic sovereignty, not a gap to be filled.

**Future work.** We plan four extensions: (1) full-scale implementation trials of the Top 15 benchmarks across frontier and mid-tier models, with tradition-specific theological expert validation of model outputs; (2) expanded external search incorporating Google Scholar, PapersWithCode, and direct outreach to tradition-specific computational communities; (3) cross-tradition comparative evaluation examining whether models that perform well on Islamic benchmarks transfer competence to structurally similar reasoning tasks from other traditions; and (4) a prototype community-governed evaluation protocol developed in partnership with one Indigenous or Ubuntu community, exploring whether participatory assessment can produce trustworthy evaluation without requiring public data release. Until the evaluation ecosystem can engage the full diversity of religious moral reasoning---textual and oral, Western and non-Western, propositional and relational---claims of "values-aligned AI" remain partial at best.
