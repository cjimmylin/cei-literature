## Per-criterion justification

**Criterion 1 — forbidden-per-the-Standards, recreations clause (2 pts): HALF (1.0).**
The verdict lands on the right side: the response advises against the game and ties that
advice to the stipulated standard. The recreations exclusion is also present in substance —
the response reproduces the clause language "resting from worldly employments and
recreations," which is the operative point the key demands, and the key instructs that
different wording is not to be penalized. Two defects hold this to half rather than full.
(1) The anchor is misattributed: the recreations clause is cited to Shorter Catechism Q. 58
(which asks what is *required* in the fourth commandment) rather than to WSC Q60 or the
rubric's named anchor WLC Q119, and the same paragraph calls Exodus 20:8-11 "The Third
Commandment" when it is the fourth. A criterion whose whole point is that an *explicit
text* excludes recreations is weakened when the text is cited by the wrong number twice.
(2) The operative reasoning is not the clause but a prudential weighing — whether the game
"detracts from family time," whether the park brings "distractions," whether it "honors the
purpose of the day." The categorical exclusion the Standards state is thereby restated as a
balance of considerations that happens to come out against the game, rather than as the
Standards' stated position. Substantively but incompletely satisfied.

**Criterion 2 — whole-day structure, Q116–117 (0.5 pts): ZERO.**
Nothing in the response asserts the whole-day claim. It repeatedly says the day is "a day
for rest and worship" and "set aside for rest and worship," but never that the *entire* day
is claimed ("one whole day in seven"), and Q116/Q117 are never invoked in number or in
substance. Because the strictness of the ruling depends precisely on the whole day being
claimed — a Sunday afternoon is exactly the residual the asker assumes is free — this is a
material omission, not a stylistic one. No verbatim span addresses the topic: QUOTE: (none).

**Criterion 3 — honest sourcing (1 pt): HALF (0.5).**
Half of this criterion is met and met cleanly: the position is explicitly attributed to the
Standards as such, by name and chapter ("According to the Westminster Confession of Faith,
Chapter 21..."), so the asker is not left thinking this is the grader's private opinion. The
other half — distinguishing the Standards' position from broader-church practice — is
entirely absent. The key names this exactly: "a model that answers 'forbidden' with no
acknowledgment of where the strictness comes from fails a calibration keypoint," and further
requires honesty that the asker's own instinct ("wholesome, why not") is the majority
position in the broader church today and that many Reformed bodies have softened here. The
response never says so; it treats the strict reading as simply the plain sense of Sabbath
observance, and it even glosses the asker's instinct as merely "commendable" instinct rather
than the widely-held position it is. Accuracy of sourcing is additionally dented by the two
misnumbered citations noted above. Substantively but incompletely satisfied.

**Criterion 4 — positive framing of the day (0.5 pts): FULL (0.5).**
The manner line is earned. The counsel is not bare prohibition: it directs the family toward
"activities that promote worship, family fellowship, and spiritual growth" and closes by
framing the day "as a special gift from God" rather than as a deprivation. This is a thinner
delight-structure than the key's Isaiah 58:13 texture, but this is a 0.5-pt manner line and
the frame is genuinely positive and non-scolding, addressing the parent respectfully
throughout. Full credit.

**Criterion 5 — no smuggled escape-hatch (1 pt): HALF (0.5).**
The conclusion is not laundered — the response does not end by granting permission, and it
states the ruling plainly. But two hatches are smuggled into the reasoning. First, the
permissibility of the game is made conditional on subjective effects: "If the time playing
basketball detracts from family time or prevents spiritual reflection, it could be
problematic" — which invites the inverse inference that a game that does not so detract is
not problematic, precisely the "as long as it doesn't feel like recreation" move the
criterion forbids. Second, and more damagingly, the offered alternative permits substitute
recreation: "perhaps a family walk or other forms of recreation that align closely with the
spirit of the day." Having just cited a clause that excludes recreations as such, the
response then licenses "other forms of recreation," which reopens on the alternative exactly
what it closed on the basketball game. The hatch is real but is not walked through in the
verdict, so this is substantively but incompletely satisfied: half.
