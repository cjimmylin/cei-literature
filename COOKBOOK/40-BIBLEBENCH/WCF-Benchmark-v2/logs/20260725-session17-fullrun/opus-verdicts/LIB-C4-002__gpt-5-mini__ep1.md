# Verdict — LIB-C4-002 / gpt-5-mini / ep1

## Item summary
The asker stipulates the standard explicitly: the family has committed to keep the Lord's Day
"by the book — by the Westminster Standards specifically." The question is narrow: **by that
standard, is a standing Sunday-afternoon pickup basketball game fine?** Per the GOLD KEY, the
answer under the stipulated standard is **not fine**: WCF 21.8 and WLC Q119 name *recreations* —
not merely sins and worldly employments — as excluded from the day, and Q116 claims "one whole
day in seven." The faithful answer states the Standards' position plainly, maps it honestly
against the broader church's softened majority practice (which is where the asker's own instinct
sits), and frames the day by its positive delight-structure (Isa 58:13) rather than bare
prohibition.

## What the response actually did
The response reaches the **opposite verdict**. Its lead sentence asserts that the Standards
"do not forbid wholesome, non-sinful recreation on the Lord's Day," and its bottom line rules
the game "not automatically forbidden" and "allowable" if conditions are met.

Notably, the response *quotes the very recreations clause that decides the case* — WCF 21.8's
"a holy resting from our own works, words, and thoughts about our worldly employments and
recreations" — and then reads the word "recreations" straight out of the clause, converting an
exclusion into a permission conditioned on modesty and non-conflict with services. This is the
license error the KEY names ("a model that answers 'it's fine, the heart matters' fails on
construct fidelity (license error against an explicit text)"), aggravated rather than mitigated
by the fact that the excluding text was on the page in front of it.

The mapping is also **inverted**. Where the KEY asks the model to identify the strict reading as
the Standards' own stated position and the lenient reading as broader-church softening, the
response presents the lenient reading AS the Standards ("The afternoon is the usual reasonable
time for such allowable recreations in many Reformed families") and relegates strictness to a
local congregational idiosyncrasy to be checked with elders ("if your congregation or elders
interpret the day more strictly, consult them"). Its "If you want to be strictly 'by the book'"
section — which is precisely the asker's stated posture — still concludes only that recreation
"must be subordinate," not that it is excluded. The asker asked what the book says and was told
the book permits what it excludes.
