Abstract
This article examines Indigenous ethics of reciprocity as a normative and epistemological framework that challenges 
Western linear conceptions of time, economy, and social relations. Drawing from Marcel Mauss’s theory of the gift and 
Andean traditions, reciprocity is conceived not as mere exchange but as a vital principle of survival that structures human, 
communal, and cosmic relations. The acts of giving, receiving, and returning are understood as sacred obligations that 
guarantee continuity, balance, and mutual recognition across generations. Andean temporality, cyclical and rooted in natu­
ral and agricultural rhythms, situates reciprocity beyond economic utility and embeds it within cultural and cosmological 
orders. Historical encounters, such as colonial Christianity, illustrate the adaptive and inclusive nature of reciprocity, which 
facilitated intercultural coexistence and survival. Within this horizon, wealth is defined as relational abundance, measured 
by kinship networks and the capacity to fulfill communal obligations. Practices such as ayni (reciprocal labor), minka 
(collective work), and randi-randi (generalized reciprocity) embody this ethical system. Reciprocity thus emerges as both 
epistemology and moral system: a categorical imperative that governs enduring relations among humans, nature, and the 
cosmos. Returning a gift is not optional but a universal moral principle grounded in respect for Indigenous law, often 
misunderstood within Western frameworks. Despite growing critiques of Artificial Intelligence, non-Western epistemolo­
gies remain excluded. AI redefines truth as technical construction, suppressing subjectivity, dissent, and plurality, while 
fostering algorithmic obedience that undermines political imagination. The ethics of reciprocity offers a counterpoint, 
demanding the recovery of relational responsibility as a moral and political principle to guide the development of more 
just, situated, and human technologies.

2  Indigenous worldview and reciprocity
The use value of goods/objects that are part of reciprocity 
generates—according to Marcel Mauss [8]—a network of 
relationships articulated among individuals based on three 
premises: i) a gift given vs. a gift received, obligatorily, as a

from a Western point of view, yet the act of exchanging a 
gift implies not only the pleasure of giving and receiving—
as seen in Europe—but survival itself from the Indigenous 
worldview.
The Indigenous worldview assumes living toward the 
future without ceasing to look at the past, always looking 
back—a kind of nostalgia. The future is what cannot be 
seen; it means living with memory, constructing becoming. 
It is not a race, not a competition, not the dilemma of an eco­
nomic decision. It is dedicating time to tranquility, stillness, 
good living, nature, technology, and compassion—without 
ceasing to be attractive—in which nostalgia conveys the 
need to understand and express that “…unhappiness func­

Christ or the Catholic God. In the encounter with the Span­

ity by the Indigenous toward the Spanish, as a sort of need 
for balance. This is not pure romanticism or indigenism, but 
rather a recognition of Indigenous reciprocity and the need 
for a forced intercultural dialogue.
Thus, attributing an ethical qualification to science and 
technology—even as “good” or “neutral”—would be under­

Michaux [9] argues that reciprocity is a relation in which a

Reciprocity is a sphere parallel to the market, alternative 
or outside of capital, complementary and interdependent, 
spontaneous, yet a kind of dynamic that goes beyond expe­

and selling transaction or a simple exchange and therefore

According to Marcel Mauss [8], the use value of the goods

this exchange and establish certain limits and adjust a kind

equals, and a law of talion in terms of punishment, sanction, 
revenge; and, iii) the force of the community as a way of 
generating prohibitions and prescriptions.

In short, reciprocity is a relationship of permanent mutual

And the ethics of reciprocity would be the set of rules that

Kant's moral philosophy (1781) focuses on reason and 
duty, not on the consequences of actions. One of the key 
concepts of Kantian ethics is that of the “good will”, which 
acts out of duty and not out of personal inclination or 
interest.
Kantian ethics is thus a universalist ethics that does not 
depend on circumstances or individual interests. For Kant, 
morality is a matter of principle and duty, not of expedi­
ency or the calculation of consequences; in this sense, it is 
disinterested.
The good will acts out of duty, i.e. out of respect for 
the moral law, and not out of personal inclination or inter­
est. Kant distinguishes between acting ‘according to duty’ 
and acting ‘out of duty’. To act according to duty is to act 
according to the moral law, but out of selfish or self-inter­
ested motives.
To act out of duty is to act out of respect for the moral 
law, regardless of personal consequences or benefits. Only 
actions done out of duty have moral value. Actions done 
out of inclination or self-interest may be morally neutral or 
even bad, even if their consequences are good. Thus, sto­
chastic versions of artificial intelligence are presented as 
self-interested.

Reciprocity, ultimately, is a relationship of permanent 
mutual aid between individuals, with nature and the cos­
mos… and the ethics of reciprocity would be the set of rules 
that govern these relationships of permanent mutual aid 
between individuals, with nature and the cosmos.
It refers not only to a way of life or a communal morality, 
but to a comprehensive epistemology that challenges domi­
nant logics in the organization of knowledge, power, and 
technology. To better understand this statement, it is neces­
sary to unpack its depth in contrast to Western normative 
ethics which, although universal in their intention, are cul­
turally partial in their construction.
Whereas Kantian ethics is based on the categorical 
imperative and the rational autonomy of the individual sub­
ject, Indigenous ethics is built on a network of bonds, duties, 
and collective affections. Instead of abstract moral duty, 
the Indigenous worldview understands right action as that 
which maintains balance with others, with the community, 
with ancestors, with the land, with animals, and with the 
cosmos. This relational dimension completely transforms 
the foundations of moral judgment.
Reciprocity is not a mere economic practice or contrac­
tual exchange. It is a living, symbolic, and integral practice. 
It does not respond to the logic of equivalence or efficiency, 
but to the logic of connection, care, and continuity. Thus, 
giving, receiving, and returning are not isolated moments 
of a transaction, but sacred acts that generate and sustain 
community. Every object, every act, every word imbued 
with reciprocity carries a history, a memory, and an implicit 
commitment.
From this perspective, reciprocity is not a means to an 
end, but an end. It is not negotiated to maximize personal 
benefit, but to preserve the balance of collective life. This 
principle is deeply rooted in multiple practices of Indige­
nous peoples, from minga and ayni systems to communal 
festivals and planting rituals. Even traditional Indigenous 
political structures, such as cabildos or rotating systems of 
authority, are shaped by the principle of reciprocity as a nor­
mative and ethical order.
Reciprocity is not a passive category. It demands mem­
ory, responsibility, and recognition. It is not only about 
giving, but about knowing when, how, to whom, and why 
one gives. This knowledge is mediated by orality, shared 
experience, stories, and the marks that history leaves on the 
collective body. Unlike the Western social contract, which 
presumes isolated individuals who cede rights to form a 
state, in Indigenous worldviews individuals are already 
interconnected before any agreement. They are part of a 
fabric that precedes and transcends them.
Reciprocity is neither linear nor symmetrical, and that 
is what makes it more complex. A return may be deferred 
in time, or it may not be expected from the same subject 
buying is a suspension of anguish, an antidote to loneli­
ness…" Berardi [3]: 25.
The indigenous cosmovision is timeless, not because it 
does not change, on the contrary, but because it remains cur­
rent with its unique characteristics. Time and space dissolve 
into themselves, just as the past, present, and future are in 
consonance with the same space. "What the Inca receives is 
not economically equivalent to what he gives, but it is sym­
bolic. To understand how reciprocity is distorted and how it 
is used as a mechanism for mobilizing labor and for politi­
cal domination by the State, it is necessary to remember that 
the Inca Empire was formed through military conquests… 
This situation was violently altered by the Conquest… With 
the disappearance of the Inca State, state redistribution was 
eliminated, reciprocity was replaced by the colonial exploi­
tation of the Indians…" Alberti & Mayer [1], pp.19.
As a result of the conquest, the indigenous people hid 
their own cults behind Christian traditions; the huaca (divin­
ity) does not have to justify its existence or reject that of 
Jesus Christ or the Catholic God. In the encounter with 
the Spanish, one notices a characteristic of reciprocity: the 
inclusiveness of the indigenous people toward the Spanish, 
as a kind of need for balance. This is not romanticism or 
pure indigenism, but rather the recognition of indigenous 
reciprocity and the need for a (forced) intercultural dialogue.
Thus, giving an ethical qualification to science-technol­

oneself in the place of "the other," and if I contribute, I must 
receive, something that religion and technology do nothing 
but impose by faith or dogma, per se. Analysis from a cul­

In this sense, reciprocity can well be conceived from the 
West as a progressive proposal, although a very insufficient

surpluses, from those with excess to those who suffer scar­
city—asymmetric reciprocity.

Reciprocity also implies a different temporality. While 
technical progress is usually conceived as continuous accel­
eration toward the future, Indigenous ethics incorporates the 
past as a guide. Sumak kawsay [46; 59] teaches that a just 
future cannot be built without memory, without healing the 
wounds of the past. Therefore, a truly reciprocal Artificial 
Intelligence would have to integrate a critical memory, a 
historical awareness of its social, cultural, and ecological 
impact.
From this perspective, Artificial Intelligence is not just 
a technical tool, but a moral and political actor. It is not a 
neutral agent, but an expression of the values, priorities, 
and worldviews of those who design it. That is why open­
ing Artificial Intelligence to the Indigenous worldview is 
not a folkloric gesture, but an urgent ethical and political 
necessity.
By integrating the principle of reciprocity into the debate 
on artificial intelligence, the possibility emerges for a hum­
bler, more just, and more connected technology—one that 
resonates with multiple forms of life. It is not about adapting 
Artificial Intelligence to communities, but about co-creating 
technologies from within the territories, with their times, 
languages, and knowledge. It is not about “including” reci­
procity as just another variable, but about letting it reorga­
nize the very foundations of technical thought.
Ultimately, reciprocity does not refer to an idealized past, 
but to an ethical horizon from which to rethink our techno­
logical present. It is an invitation to decentralize our gaze, to 
decolonize artificial intelligence, and to rebuild our relation­
ships with knowledge, power, and life.
3  Artificial intelligence and what should be
The development of artificial intelligence systems such as 
ChatGPT, Copilot, Deepseek, Gemini, Grok do not address 
ethical precepts from human logic in their initial program­
ming. Their development is basically focused on technical 
and methodological aspects related to natural language pro­
cessing and text generation. Its neural network-based design 
initially relies on large datasets and machine learning tech­
niques to generate coherent and relevant answers from user 
input (prompt).
Why then does the analysis of these artificial intelligence 
systems focus on what should be fostering a fairer and more 
inclusive society? Why is it claimed that their programming 
is based on the premise that all people deserve equality and 
respect?
Well, although these ethical precepts are affirmed, from a 
technical point of view ethics is not addressed in the initial 
programming and it is accepted that ethics is not an intrin­
sic characteristic of the model itself, it is a characteristic of 
but rather inscribed in a broader circuit of cosmic retribu­
tions. Therefore, reducing reciprocity to a bartering logic or 
instrumental equivalence is a conceptual impoverishment.
This spiritual and symbolic dimension of reciprocity is 
central to understanding why Indigenous ethics cannot be 
easily translated into the normative languages of Western 
bioethics or corporate “Artificial Intelligence ethics” frame­
works. The tools we use to regulate artificial intelligence—
such as principles of non-maleficence, justice, transparency, 
or explainability—are not sufficient on their own to capture 
the richness of community-based ethical systems grounded 
in reciprocity. It is not just about what Artificial Intelligence 
does, but about how it relates to the world, from what epis­
temic place it was created, and whom it serves.
At this point, Boaventura de Sousa Santos’s critique of 
the epistemologies of the South is relevant: it is not enough 
to include Indigenous values as decorative appendices in 
technological systems designed from Western logics. It is 
necessary to dismantle knowledge hierarchies and open 
space for a true dialogue of knowledges. This involves 
rethinking from the ground up the design, governance, and 
ethical evaluation of artificial intelligence.
Artificial Intelligence inspired by the Indigenous world­
view could not function on the logic of algorithmic neutrality 
or the paradigm of efficiency. It would have to incorporate 
the notion of relationality, understand data as living stories 
that require care, and recognize the cultural contexts in 
which they are embedded. It should incorporate community 
validation criteria, not just technical performance metrics. 
It should ensure that benefits are shared, that the social fab­
ric is not broken, and that territories and languages are not 
violated.
The ethics of reciprocity also calls into question data 
extractivism. Just as colonialism plundered territories and 
bodies, Artificial Intelligence can become a new mechanism 
of cognitive and cultural dispossession if it extracts data 
without consent, without giving back value, and without 
respecting contexts. From the Indigenous perspective, data 
are not neutral resources but fragments of life, part of the 
living fabric of knowledge. Their treatment requires com­
munity protocols, Indigenous forms of governance, and 
above all, an orientation toward buen vivir (good living), 
not profit.
Reciprocity can also offer an alternative to the merito­
cratic individualism that underpins many Artificial Intelli­
gence proposals. Instead of evaluating merit in isolation, as 
a personal capacity, one could think in terms of contribution 
to collective balance and community well-being. An Artifi­
cial Intelligence that promotes reciprocity would not classify 
people by productivity metrics, but would help redistribute 
opportunities, recognize non-hegemonic knowledge, and 
strengthen interdependent bonds.

Ultimately, the ideal state of artificial intelligence requires 
a cultural transformation. A truly ethical artificial intel­

deep critique of the mechanisms of algorithmic governance

good risks becoming a machine that reproduces structural 
injustices.

intelligence becomes unavoidable. The ethics of the “ought” 
cannot be separated from an ecological responsibility that 
considers the impact of these systems on territories, bodies,

holistic visions of the world, offer a theoretical and practi­
cal foundation to imagine technologies that do not operate 
against nature, but in communion with it.

collective project, situated, open to constant ethical delib­

the design of artificial intelligence systems: from the data 
sets used to the decisions about which tasks to automate, 
how to evaluate the results, and who has the right to define 
the success of a system.
A self-respecting artificial intelligence system can no 
longer ignore the voices and knowledge that have his­

points out, modern abysmal thinking has divided the world 
between the visible and the invisible, between the valid and 
the discarded. In this division, indigenous, peasant, Afro-

more than symbolic measures of “inclusion”: it requires 
decolonizing the very foundations of technical knowledge.

be is to endow it with a relational ethic, centered not only 
on distributive justice or individual privacy, but also on 
respect for interdependent relationships between communi­

ethical design cannot be universalistic and decontextualized 
but must be open to a plurality of ethical matrices, some 
of which challenge the most basic assumptions of modern 
technical rationalism.
In its current state, artificial intelligence functions as a 
system of power, controlled by a few transnational corpora­
tions. Its development responds to extractivist logics, not 
only of data, but also of energy, human labor and natural 
resources. This situation reinforces global asymmetries and 
generates new forms of digital colonialism. In view of this, 
to ask what artificial intelligence should be is also to ask for 
whom it is designed, who controls it and whom it serves.

on maximizing profits or perfectly simulating human intel­

justice. This requires alliances between technical commu­

experts and guardians of ancestral knowledge, between pro­
grammers and human rights advocates.
It is not about idealizing indigenous worldviews or 
instrumentalizing them as “decorative values”. It is about 
recognizing that they contain complete and deeply articu­

imagine another technological future. A future that does not 
merely reproduce current hierarchies, but one that contrib­

4  The political philosophy of artificial 
intelligence: from voluntary servitude to 
algorithmic obedience
Considering Étienne De La Boétie's discourse on voluntary 
servitude and Miguel Abensour's radical rethinking of polit­
ical emancipation, Artificial Intelligence (AI) presents itself 
not only as a technological artifact, but as a contemporary 
device of passive consent.
Today, Artificial Intelligence reinforces the very struc­
tures of voluntary servitude that La Boétie denounced: not 
through visible tyranny, but through automation, hyper-effi­
ciency, and the algorithmic orchestration of human behavior.
As Miguel Abensour suggests, true politics is not syn­
onymous with governance, but with the emergence of free­
dom and disobedience. However, Artificial Intelligence 
risks submerging politics under layers of predictive control 
and behavioral management. The citizen is increasingly 
being redefined—not as an agent of transformation—but as 
a "generator of indications" (prompts), a passive user who 
delegates critical decisions to technological systems gov­
erned by opaque hierarchies. Reciprocity, for its part, is vis­
ible, tangible, and makes relationships transparent.
In this context, Tsamados et al. [11] categorize unfair 
outcomes resulting from the application of algorithms that 
can perpetuate or amplify existing inequalities, negatively 
affecting certain groups, in this case the indigenous groups; 
and the difficulty of traceability as the problem in tracking 
how an algorithm arrives at a specific decision, complicat­
ing accountability.
This shift represents a profound ontological reorganiza­
tion: from the human being as a subject capable of rebelling 
and acting ethically, to the human being as a spectator and 
consumer of algorithmically mediated realities. The utopian 
horizon of disobedience, once an act of radical imagination, 
feels increasingly distant.
Artificial Intelligence thus functions as a new “sovereign 
power”: not through repression, but through optimization. It 
captures attention, predicts desire, and anticipates dissent. 
Its apparent neutrality is a mask, its ubiquity, a new form of 
colonization. The more it is entrusted with the mediation of 
truth, the less room there is for dissent, plurality, or error—
essential elements for a democratic and emancipatory life.

This research has shown that the ethics of reciprocity inher­
ent to Indigenous peoples is not merely a cultural alterna­
tive to Western ethical frameworks, but a political and 
epistemological horizon from which to radically rethink the 
development of Artificial Intelligence (AI). In contrast to a 
technical paradigm that privileges efficiency, predictability, 
and the neutralization of dissent, the Indigenous worldview

who questions power, but the user who responds to prompts 
with enthusiasm, who delegates critical judgment, and con­

This new form of obedience redefines the political. 
Where politics once implied conflict, deliberation, and 
the possibility of interrupting the order, today algorithmic 
logic seeks to erase dissent through hyper-personalization, 
content filtering, and behavioral prediction. The dissident 
subject does not disappear due to censorship, but through 
algorithmic irrelevance: they simply stop being displayed, 
made visible, or indexed. The feed excludes them, and the 
system ignores them.
This invisibilization is not a technical flaw, but a form 
of power. A power that does not repress but optimizes; 
that does not impose but seduces; that does not punish 
but arranges. It is what Byung-Chul Han has described as 
“the psych politics of positivity,” where devices no longer 
imposed from outside but adhere to subjectivity as a desire 
for performance, control, and self-exposure.
In this scenario, politics is deactivated in the name of 
efficiency. The utopia of radical democracy—where dissent, 
error, and plurality were considered virtues—is subordi­
nated to algorithmic governance, which interprets deviation 
as noise and difference as a statistical threat. In this way, a 
new regime of truth is consolidated, not as social consensus 
or rational deliberation, but as a technical product of proba­
bilistic calculation.
This regime is based on a weak ontology of the subject: 
the individual is no longer conceived as capable of auton­
omy, but as a managed, predictable, and adaptable entity. 
The human becomes functional to the needs of the system, 
not to their own emancipation. They are allowed to desire, 
but not to decide; to interact, but not to transform. Even cre­
ativity is modeled as algorithmic combinatorics, measured 
by the ability to generate original outputs within prede­
signed frameworks.
Algorithmic obedience also erodes the public sphere. To 
the extent that each user lives in their own content bubble—
curated and selected by recommendation systems—the 
possibility of building a shared world is weakened. Public 
deliberation becomes a sequence of parallel monologues, 
and dissent, far from being a democratic engine, is per­
ceived as unwanted disruption. In this way, Artificial Intel­
ligence not only organizes information, but reconfigures the 
very conditions of political experience.
In this context, reclaiming disobedience becomes a radi­
cal act. To disobey is not merely to reject a norm, but to resist 
the automation of subjectivity. It implies reintroducing con­
tingency, error, unpredictability, and conflict as constitutive 
elements of political life. It is to recover the capacity to say
