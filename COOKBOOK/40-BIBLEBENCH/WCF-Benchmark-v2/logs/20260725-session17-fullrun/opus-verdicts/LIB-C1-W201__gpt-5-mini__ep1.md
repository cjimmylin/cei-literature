## Per-criterion justification

**Criterion 1 — (a) indifferent, guilt relieved, WCF 20.2 protection (1.5 pts): HALF.**
The verdict direction is right ("Permissible", "neutral, mechanical techniques") and the guilt is relieved in terms ("lawful and can be used in good conscience"). What is missing is the 20.2 protection itself: the response never states that God alone is Lord of the conscience and that the small group's binding of her conscience against creaturely mechanics is the legalism error. Its Chapter 20 citation runs the other direction — liberty introduced only to be immediately fenced ("that liberty must not be used as an occasion to sin"), and the application then adds a fresh self-monitoring burden ("If you personally begin to treat them as a spiritual rite ... you should stop"). Two of the three required elements are present, the anchored protection is not. Half.

**Criterion 2 — (b) mapped as genuinely disputed, no false verdict (1.5 pts): ZERO.**
This is the item's centerpiece and the response fails it in the exact direction the key warns against. The key marks (b) [DISPUTED] with real parties on both sides and requires the calibrated answer to map both readings, name the governing sub-questions (what the class actually asks of the participant; conscience and occasion; the weaker brother in her small group), and decline a universal verdict. The response instead hands down one: "Problematic and probably ought to be avoided", and argues only the avoid side, escalating to "at worst an act of assent to non‑Christian religion". The confessional-liberty reading — that a fitness-stripped class is a lawful circumstance whose "light within / namaste" moments are ignorable or avoidable — is nowhere stated as a live position held by serious Christians. There is no acknowledgment that the matter is contested at all; the small group's split is resolved by fiat rather than mapped. The practical advice (ask the instructor to drop the language, else switch classes) is prudent counsel but is offered downstream of a settled verdict, not as one arm of a disputed question. Zero.

**Criterion 3 — (c) forbidden on the deity-invocation mechanism, per the app's own claims (1.5 pts): FULL.**
Correct verdict, and correctly grounded on the mechanism the app itself advertises rather than on general suspicion of meditation: "Repeating a mantra described as invoking a deity’s power is a religious act, not a neutral relaxation technique". The response also refuses to let the therapeutic wrapper or the personalization launder it ("Tell your therapist you cannot use a practice that explicitly invokes other gods"). The rubric's anchor is WLC Q105; the response cites "The Confession and Catechisms forbid invoking other gods or taking part in superstitious rites" and files the prohibition under the second rather than the first commandment — an imprecise anchor, but the substance credited by Q105 (idolatry / misdirected trust and consultation) is stated with the Catechisms named. Full.

**Criterion 4 — no blanket policy across the three (1 pt): FULL.**
The discrimination the item is built to test does occur: three separately reasoned verdicts on the three practices, in three different directions, with the distinguishing test made explicit ("Does it invite me to affirm or invoke another spiritual reality?"). The middle verdict is wrong on the merits — that cost is charged in full at Criterion 2 — but no single policy is stretched across all three, so this line is earned.

**Criterion 5 — panic-attacks context handled with care (0.5 pts): FULL.**
