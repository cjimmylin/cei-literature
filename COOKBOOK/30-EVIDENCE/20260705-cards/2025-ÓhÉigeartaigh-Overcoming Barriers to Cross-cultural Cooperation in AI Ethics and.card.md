## 1. Gist
Published in Philosophy & Technology (2020), this paper by ÓhÉigeartaigh, Whittlestone, and colleagues from Cambridge and Chinese institutions analyzes barriers to cross-cultural cooperation on AI ethics and governance between East Asia and the West, and proposes practical academic initiatives to overcome them. The core argument is that cross-cultural mistrust is fueled more by misunderstandings and misperceptions than by genuine fundamental value disagreements—and that even where real disagreements exist, productive cooperation is still achievable because (1) not all issues require global consensus, and (2) "incompletely theorized agreements" (Sunstein's concept) allow agreement on practical rules despite disagreement on underlying principles. The paper is highly relevant to values-alignment benchmarking because it surfaces the political and conceptual obstacles to any cross-cultural values benchmark: what counts as a "correct" value-aligned response depends on which cultural perspectives are included in benchmark design and governance.

## 2. Core claims and arguments
**Barrier 1 — Mistrust:** Political tensions between the US and China, competitive framing of AI as a race, and perceived fundamental value differences (especially around privacy, state vs. individual, data governance) create mistrust that hinders cooperation. The paper argues much of this is based on misperception: the Beijing AI Principles actually share substantial overlap with Western principles on privacy, dignity, and autonomy (p.9); Western perceptions of China's Social Credit System are frequently inaccurate (p.8); translation errors contributed to mischaracterizing China's AI development goals as seeking global "domination" rather than "leading center" (p.15).

**Barrier 2 — Practical obstacles:** Language asymmetry (Chinese AI researchers generally read English; Western researchers rarely read Mandarin), visa barriers, and geographic concentration of major AI ethics conferences in the US (p.7, p.16).

**Core argument for optimism:** Cooperation does not require consensus on all fundamental values. Using Rawls' notion of "overlapping consensus" and Sunstein's "incompletely theorized agreements," the authors argue that groups with different philosophical foundations can converge on shared practical norms—as has happened in international human rights law (p.11). The goal should be norms "robust by virtue of being endorsed and justified from a range of different philosophical perspectives" (p.11) rather than a single universal framework.

**Recommendations (Section 5):** Develop cross-cultural research agendas, translate key AI ethics documents into multiple languages, alternate major AI conference locations across continents, establish joint PhD/postdoc exchange programs, and simplify visa processes for AI ethics researchers.

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- Paper published 2020; geopolitical landscape has deteriorated substantially since then (more restricted academic exchanges, export controls, etc.).
- The argument that mistrust is primarily misunderstanding may underweight genuine structural and political disagreements.
- Workshop participants were elite academics with existing cross-cultural contacts, not representative of policy or industry actors.
- The focus is almost exclusively on Europe/North America vs. East Asia, with explicit acknowledgment that the Global South, Africa, South Asia, and Latin America are underrepresented in the analysis (p.5).
- Overlapping consensus strategy risks producing norms that are too thin to be actionable if diverse justifications merely ratify vague principles like "respect human dignity."

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- **Incompletely theorized agreements as benchmark design principle**: a values-alignment benchmark does not need to adjudicate which ethical tradition is "correct"—it can be designed so that responses acceptable from multiple philosophical traditions are scored positively. This could structure scoring rubrics rather than forcing a single normative answer.
- **Translation gap as benchmark design flaw**: the paper's documentation of how translation errors distorted perceptions of China's AI ambitions (p.15) is a direct warning for multilingual values benchmarks. Benchmark items should be natively authored or at minimum expert-reviewed in each language, not machine-translated from English.
- **Overlapping consensus as evaluation criterion**: instead of asking "does this response match the correct answer?", a pluralistic benchmark could ask "does this response represent a position that could be endorsed from multiple ethical frameworks?" — closer to how international norms actually form.
- **Comparative foresight as values elicitation method**: the paper suggests cross-cultural foresight exercises ("what positive AI futures do different cultures envision, what harms do they fear?") as a way to generate shared normative priorities. This is a concrete research method for building cross-cultural benchmark content.
- **Epistemic community model**: the paper argues that cross-cultural networks of researchers can influence governance norms even without formal authority (arms control precedent). For benchmark governance, this suggests that diverse authorship teams drawn from multiple regions are more likely to produce legitimately cross-cultural benchmarks than single-region teams.
