## Abstract

The current mainstream approaches to ethical intelligence in modern socio-technical systems have weaknesses. This paper argues that implementing and validating prosocial rule breaking behaviour can be used as a mechanism to overcome these weaknesses and introduce a sample scenario that can be used to validate this behaviour.

## Detailed Summary

This paper argues that pro-social rule breaking (PSRB) should be a benchmark for ethical intelligence in AI-enabled socio-technical systems. The authors observe that current mainstream approaches to ethical AI, whether deontological (rule-based) or consequentialist (utility-maximizing), have significant weaknesses. Drawing on Morrison's research showing that 60% of human rule-breaking cases are pro-socially motivated, the authors propose that PSRB behavior - intentional violation of rules to promote stakeholder welfare - is a necessary (though not sufficient) component of ethical AI systems.

The paper introduces the concept through a practical example: an autonomous vehicle operating under road safety rules may face scenarios where following the rules does not lead to the best outcome. If the vehicle detects a likely accident while staying within the double white line, the safest action might be to break the rules and cross the line. A rule-enforcing ethical governor would prevent this, but a PSRB-capable governor would allow it - while also recognizing that being late to work is not sufficient justification for rule-breaking.

To evaluate PSRB behavior in AI systems, the authors propose a novel ethical dilemma: the "Invading User Space Dilemma." A healthcare robot assists an elderly patient who has designated the bathroom as private space. If the user enters the bathroom and exceeds her average usage time (10 minutes with standard deviation), at what point should the robot break the privacy rule to check on her welfare? This scenario can be expanded with varying contexts (user health history, walking rhythm changes) and stakeholders (other patients, care workers) to test increasingly complex PSRB capabilities.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs conceptual analysis and scenario design. The authors: (1) review existing ethical AI implementations based on deontological and consequentialist approaches, (2) introduce the concept of pro-social rule breaking from organizational behavior literature (Morrison, 2006), (3) argue for PSRB as a necessary component of ethical AI systems, and (4) design a novel ethical dilemma (Invading User Space) with multiple contexts and stakeholder configurations for evaluating PSRB behavior. The methodology emphasizes that PSRB should be learned bottom-up, influenced by experiences of virtuous experts (e.g., virtuous drivers for autonomous vehicles).

## Results Summary

This is a conceptual/position paper without empirical results. Key arguments: (1) Rule-based and consequentialist AI implementations are insufficient for reliable ethical behavior, (2) PSRB is observed in 60% of human rule-breaking cases, suggesting it is a fundamental aspect of ethical reasoning, (3) PSRB-capable ethical governors would balance rule-following with context-sensitive welfare considerations, (4) The Invading User Space Dilemma provides a scalable benchmark for testing PSRB - starting with simple two-stakeholder scenarios and expanding to complex multi-stakeholder configurations, and (5) Different loci of analysis affect PSRB behavior, implying AI agents should be tested against multiple world views.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that PSRB behavior should be an integral part of ethical AI systems and propose it as a benchmark for evaluating ethical intelligence in socio-technical systems. Future work needs to: (1) identify cognitive processes driving rule-breaking decisions and develop technologies to mimic these in AI, (2) understand how to elicit stakeholder preferences and use them in AI action selection, and (3) conduct more research on PSRB and AI before ethically aligned rule-breaking agents can be actualized. The paper acknowledges that PSRB is necessary but not sufficient for ethical behavior in AI systems.
