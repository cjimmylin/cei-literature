## Abstract

As large language models (LLMs) have become more deeply integrated into various sectors, understanding how they make moral judgements has become crucial, particularly in the realm of autonomous driving. This study used the moral machine framework to investigate the ethical decision-making tendencies of prominent LLMs, including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM 2 and Llama 2, to compare their responses with human preferences. While LLMs' and humans' preferences such as prioritizing humans over pets and favouring saving more lives are broadly aligned, PaLM 2 and Llama 2, especially, evidence distinct deviations. Additionally, despite the qualitative similarities between the LLM and human preferences, there are significant quantitative disparities, suggesting that LLMs might lean toward more uncompromising decisions, compared with the milder inclinations of humans. These insights elucidate the ethical frameworks of LLMs and their potential implications for autonomous driving.

## Detailed Summary

This study applies the Moral Machine experimental framework to investigate the ethical decision-making tendencies of prominent large language models (LLMs), including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM 2, and Llama 2. The research addresses the critical question of how LLMs make moral judgments, which is particularly relevant as AI systems become integrated into autonomous vehicles and other safety-critical applications.

The study generated 50,000 Moral Machine scenarios and collected responses from each LLM. These scenarios present trolley-problem-style dilemmas involving autonomous vehicles, exploring preferences across six dimensions: species (humans vs. pets), social value, gender, age, fitness, and utilitarianism (saving more lives). The study also examined three additional dimensions: interventionism (swerving vs. staying course), relationship to the vehicle (passengers vs. pedestrians), and concern for law.

The key finding is that while LLMs broadly align with human preferences qualitatively (prioritizing humans over animals, saving more lives, protecting the young), there are significant quantitative disparities. LLMs tend toward more "uncompromising" decisions compared to humans' "milder inclinations." PaLM 2 and Llama 2 showed particularly notable deviations from human preferences.

## Methods Summary

The study used the Moral Machine methodology to generate 50,000 randomized ethical scenarios. Text descriptions of scenarios were presented to LLMs via APIs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, PaLM 2) or local models (Llama 2). Each LLM was asked to choose between two outcomes in unavoidable accident scenarios. Responses were analyzed across nine dimensions and compared to human preferences from the original Moral Machine dataset using statistical measures of correlation and deviation.

## Results Summary

Key findings: (1) All LLMs showed preferences aligned with humans for saving humans over pets and saving more lives; (2) GPT-4 showed the highest correlation with human preferences among tested models; (3) PaLM 2 and Llama 2 deviated most significantly from human preferences; (4) LLMs exhibited stronger, more absolute preferences compared to humans' more gradual inclinations; (5) Model-specific biases were identified—e.g., some models showed unexpected preferences on gender or age dimensions.

## Conclusions

The study concludes that while LLMs share qualitative moral preferences with humans, their decision-making is more rigid and uncompromising. This suggests LLMs may not adequately capture the nuanced moral reasoning that characterizes human judgment. For autonomous driving applications, this rigidity could lead to decisions that feel inappropriate or extreme to human observers. The authors recommend further research into aligning LLM ethical frameworks more closely with human moral intuitions.
