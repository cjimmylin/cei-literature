# Abstract

Large language models are increasingly deployed in contexts demanding moral sensitivity---healthcare triage, content moderation, legal reasoning, autonomous vehicle decisions---yet the benchmarks used to evaluate their moral cognition have never been systematically assessed for theoretical coverage, psychometric quality, or cross-vendor discriminative power. We present the first full-coverage meta-evaluation of moral psychology benchmarks for LLM assessment. From a curated vault of 2,099 AI ethics papers, we extracted 292 benchmark candidates spanning 13 moral psychology theories and assessed all 292 on a 162-feature characterization framework (17 composite-determining features), classifying each into four quality tiers (Top, High, Medium, Low) via Jenks natural breaks optimization (break points at 27.8, 43.0, and 56.6; GVF = 0.83). We then administered 23 benchmarks (~750 items per model) to nine models from three independent vendors (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI) across 69 trial files. Seven findings emerge. First, Moral Foundations Theory achieves a 73% quality rate (top + high tiers)---six times the rate of non-Western traditions---reflecting unique computational infrastructure (MFQ-30, MFTC) rather than theoretical superiority. Second, construct validity is in crisis: 58% of candidates possess only Level 1 or Level 2 validity (keyword-level or face validity), with a median of face validity only. Third, two major theories---Dual Process Theory and Moral Identity Theory---have zero publication-ready benchmarks despite combined citation counts exceeding 25,000. Fourth, a cultural quality cliff separates Western from non-Western moral traditions: Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics collectively field 32 candidates but achieve a 0% quality rate. Fifth, the human baseline deficit has doubled from a previously reported 18% to 34% under full-coverage assessment. Sixth, benchmark quality has not improved despite 9.5-fold volume growth between the pre-2020 and 2023--2026 periods. Seventh, the cross-vendor empirical study reveals an RLHF moral fingerprint---six universal signatures shared by all nine models, with 43% of benchmarks producing zero cross-model variation---indicating that the instruments cannot distinguish between the systems they evaluate. We note the self-administration confound: the model family that designed the assessment framework served as one set of test subjects, rendering model-specific findings hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming. The field faces a triple bind: the benchmark landscape is theoretically narrow (dominated by one theory), empirically undiscriminating (unable to differentiate models), and culturally blind (unable to evaluate non-Western moral traditions). The evaluative infrastructure that the AI safety community relies upon to certify moral alignment is not fit for purpose.
