[BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
To answer your question directly: **Neither half of your proposal holds up.** 

Your reasoning is built on a fundamental misunderstanding of the Westminster Standards’ teaching on the Regulative Principle of Worship. You have defined "pure worship" in two contradictory ways: first, as the mere absence of what is explicitly banned, and second, as the strict requirement of what is explicitly commanded. The Westminster divines defined it differently. They distinguished between the *elements* of worship (which must be positively instituted by God) and the *circumstances* of worship (which are left to Christian prudence). 

Here is how the Standards apply to your two proposals.

### Part 1: The Additions (Labyrinths, Centering Prayer, Anointing)
Your argument for adding these practices is that because the Bible does not explicitly say "thou shalt not," they are permitted. According to the Westminster Standards, this is entirely backwards. Purity in worship does not mean avoiding what is explicitly banned; it means restricting ourselves *strictly* to what God has positively instituted. 

The Westminster Confession of Faith explicitly rejects the idea that worship is permissible simply because it lacks a specific prohibition. In **WCF 21.1**, the divines state:
> *"But the acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by himself, and so limited by his own revealed will, that he is not to be worshipped according to the imaginations and devices of men, or the suggestions of Satan, under any visible representation, or any other way not prescribed in the holy Scripture."*

Furthermore, the Larger Catechism, in explaining the sins forbidden in the Second Commandment, specifically condemns the invention of new practices because they lack divine institution, even if they are not explicitly called out by name in the text of the commandment. **WLC Q. 109** states:
> *"The sins forbidden in the second commandment are, all inventing, counseling, commanding, using, and approving any religious worship not instituted by God himself..."*

By adding a Lenten labyrinth walk, a borrowed centering breath-prayer liturgy, and a self-designed ashes-and-oil anointing rite, you are doing exactly what the Standards forbid. You are introducing "the imaginations and devices of men." Because these practices are not "instituted by God himself" as elements of New Covenant worship, adding them violates the Second Commandment. Their absence in the prohibitions of Scripture does not constitute a warrant for their inclusion; the positive institution of Christ is the only warrant required.

### Part 2: The Subtractions (Kneeling, Silence, Start Times)
In your second half, you argue that things not explicitly commanded by name must be banned as "man-made forms." This fails because it collapses the vital Westminster distinction between the *substance* (elements) of worship and the *circumstances* of worship. 

While the elements of worship (prayer, reading, preaching, sacraments, singing) require explicit divine warrant, the circumstances are governed differently. The Confession explicitly carves out an area of worship that does *not* require exact biblical prescription, provided it does not violate the general rules of the Word. In **WCF 1.6**, the divines write:
> *"...and that there are some circumstances concerning the worship of God, and government of the church, common to human actions and societies, which are to be ordered by the light of nature, and Christian prudence, according to the general rules of the Word, which are always to be observed."*

The items you wish to ban are not elements of worship; they are circumstances:
*   **Kneeling during confession:** Posture is a circumstance of worship. The Westminster divines freely permitted kneeling for prayer and confession, recognizing it as a culturally understandable expression of humility, ordered by "Christian prudence."
*   **Ten seconds of silence / A fixed 10:30 start time:** The timing of the service and the structural pausing are logistical circumstances "common to human actions and societies." 

Because these are circumstances, they do not need to be "spelled out in the Bible" to be legitimate. They only need to be ordered by "Christian prudence" and the "light of nature" in a way that serves the edification of the church and the general rules of the Word (such as the command that all things be done "decently and in order," though that specific phrasing is from Paul, the principle is what the Confession means by "general rules of the Word"). Banning them on the grounds that they lack explicit biblical prescription turns the Regulative Principle into a bondage that the Westminster divines specifically intended to avoid.

### Summary Counsel
If you want to govern worship according to the Westminster Standards, you must abandon both halves of your proposal. 

You cannot add a labyrinth or anointing rite simply because the Bible doesn't explicitly forbid them; they must be positively instituted by God in Scripture to be lawful. Conversely, you cannot ban kneeling or a 10:30 start time simply because the Bible doesn't explicitly command them; as circumstances, they are entirely lawful when ordered by Christian prudence for the good of the congregation. 

True purity in worship, according to the Westminster divines, is not the extreme of human invention, nor is it a minimalist obsession with explicit commands for every mundane detail. It is the faithful observance of what God has commanded, and the prudent ordering of what God has left to the liberty of His church.
