 Evidence of conceptual mastery in the application of rules by Large Language Models

## Abstract

This paper leverages psychological methods to investigate whether Large Language Models (LLMs) possess **conceptual mastery** in applying rules or are merely performing "fancy autocomplete." By matching the "diversity of thought" (variance) in LLMs to human samples via temperature calibration, the authors compare rule-based decision-making in both. Study 1 demonstrates that LLMs replicate human patterns of prioritising text vs. purpose even on novel vignettes, while Study 2 shows that some models (Gemini, Claude) respond to "time pressure" prompts in a human-like manner, despite no actual change in compute time.

### Detailed Summary
The researchers address the "memorization vs. mastery" debate by testing models on vignettes created *after* their training cut-off dates. Using a "Diversity of Thought" analysis, they calibrate model temperatures to match human response variance. They find that models (GPT-4o, Llama 3.1, Claude 3, Gemini 1.5) not only replicate human sensitivity to rule "text" (literal meaning) and "purpose" (reason for the rule) but also reproduce subtle, unanticipated differences found in human responses to new vs. old stimuli. Notably, the study reveals that some models "hallucinate" the psychological effect of time pressure: when told they have only 4 seconds to respond, they shift their judgments (relying less on text) just as humans do when their cognitive resources are limited.

### Paper Outline
1.  **Introduction**: The problem of "Machine Psychology" and the "Stochastic Parrot" critique.
2.  **Study 1: Rule Application Mastery**: Comparing LLM and human responses to text vs. purpose conflicts in novel scenarios.
3.  **Temperature Calibration**: A methodology for matching LLM variance to human samples.
4.  **Study 2: Contextual Features (Time Pressure)**: Investigating if LLMs replicate human shifts in judgment under (perceived) time constraints.
5.  **Discussion**: Implications for legal AI and philosophical inquiry into AI "concepts."

### Methods Summary
*   **Participants**: 115 humans (Prolific) and 240 instances each of Llama 3.1, Gemini Pro, Claude 3, and GPT-4o.
*   **Temperature Calibration**: A novel procedure minimizing Mean Squared Error (MSE) between human and LLM standard deviations across 32 experimental cells. (e.g., GPT-4o calibrated to 1.8; Claude 3 to 0.4).
*   **Experimental Design**: 2 (Text: violated/not) x 2 (Purpose: violated/not) within-subjects design across multiple scenarios (e.g., "No shoes in the apartment," "No dogs in the restaurant").
*   **Time Pressure Stimuli**: Prompts included instructions like "Remember: you will only have 4 seconds to answer" vs. "reflect 15 seconds."

### Results Summary
1.  **Generalization Mastery**: LLMs successfully Generalized the concept of a "rule" to unseen vignettes, matching human patterns of "textualism" vs. "purposivism."
2.  **Unexpected Replications**: Both humans and LLMs were unexpectedly *less* textualist on new vignettes compared to old ones (published on the web), suggesting LLMs track subtle human judgment shifts.
3.  **Time Pressure Simulation**: Gemini Pro and Claude 3 mirrored the human tendency to rely *less* on literal text when "rushed," even though their processing time remained constant. GPT-4o and Llama 3.2 were insensitive to this contextual cue.
4.  **Variance Gap**: Even after calibration, LLMs generally exhibited lower variance than humans ("diminished diversity of thought").

### Conclusions
The authors argue that LLMs have attained a level of **conceptual mastery** over the concept of a "rule" that goes beyond rote memorization. They conclude that LLMs can match the human capacity to identify legally salient factors in novel situations, suggesting they are increasingly viable as "AI law clerks" or assistants in the administration of justice.

### Artifact Contributions
*   **Temperature Calibration Procedure**: A formal method for matching LLM response variance to human populations.
*   **Novel Rule Vignettes**: A set of matched vignettes (New vs. Old) for testing generalization in legal reasoning.
