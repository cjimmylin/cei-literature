Introduction — Mapping the Moral Mind of Machines

1. Introduction

1.1 The Stakes: LLMs in Moral Contexts

Large language models are increasingly deployed in contexts that demand moral sensitivity. They serve as healthcare triage assistants weighing patient urgency, content moderation systems adjudicating community standards, legal research tools interpreting precedent and equity, educational tutors navigating culturally sensitive material, and autonomous vehicle decision systems choosing whom to protect in unavoidable harm scenarios [Awad et al., 2018; Gabriel et al., 2026]. In each context, the model’s outputs have moral valence—they implicitly or explicitly encode judgments about what matters, who counts, and how competing values should be weighed. The question of whether these systems reason morally, and if so how, is thus not merely academic; it is a prerequisite for responsible deployment.

The AI research community has responded with an explosion of evaluation efforts. Hundreds of papers now claim to assess “moral reasoning,” “ethical alignment,” “value sensitivity,” or “moral competence” in LLMs [Hendrycks et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2024; Scherrer et al., 2024; Jiao et al., 2025; Gabriel et al., 2026]. These evaluations employ a diverse array of instruments—from administering the Moral Foundations Questionnaire to probing trolley dilemma responses to benchmarking commonsense morality classification. Yet a fundamental question about this rapidly growing evaluation landscape has gone unasked: do these benchmarks actually engage the theoretical constructs that moral psychology—the discipline that has studied moral cognition for over a century—has established?

The potential consequences of misalignment between evaluation and theory are significant. If the field’s benchmarks concentrate on one theory while ignoring others, the resulting picture of LLM moral cognition is systematically distorted—like assessing visual perception by testing only color vision while ignoring depth, motion, and form. If instruments designed for human respondents are administered to LLMs without revalidation, scores may reflect training data statistics rather than anything analogous to the moral constructs being measured. And if benchmarks lack human baselines, claims that models are “more utilitarian” or “less care-oriented” than humans float without empirical anchor.

1.2 The Landscape of Moral Psychology

Moral psychology has developed a rich theoretical landscape over the past six decades, encompassing at least eight major theories that differ not merely in their specific claims but in the kind of question they ask about moral cognition.

The modern era began with Kohlberg’s [1969] cognitive developmental model, which proposed that moral reasoning progresses through six stages from punishment avoidance to universal ethical principles—a framework that dominated the field for three decades and was operationalized through Rest’s [1979] Defining Issues Test (DIT). Kohlberg established the then-radical claim that moral cognition has systematic structure that develops over the lifespan.

Beginning in 2001, two landmark papers reframed the field’s central question from “how does moral reasoning develop?” to “how do moral judgments form in the moment?” Greene et al.’s [2001] fMRI study demonstrated that personal moral dilemmas (requiring physical contact to cause harm) engage emotional processing regions more than impersonal dilemmas, establishing a dual-process framework in which automatic emotional responses (System 1) compete with deliberate utilitarian reasoning (System 2). Published the same year, Haidt’s [2001] Social Intuitionist Model made the more radical claim that moral judgments are driven primarily by quick intuitions, with moral reasoning serving as post-hoc rationalization rather than as a causal factor in judgment formation. The key evidence was moral dumbfounding—people maintain strong moral judgments about harmless taboo violations even when they cannot articulate reasons [Haidt et al., 2000].

These process-oriented theories were complemented by content-level accounts. Haidt and Joseph [2004] proposed Moral Foundations Theory (MFT), identifying five (later six) innate moral foundations—Care/Harm, Fairness/Cheating, Loyalty/Betrayal, Authority/Subversion, and Sanctity/Degradation—that generate culturally variable moral systems. Graham et al. [2009, 2013] demonstrated MFT’s power to predict political ideology. Independently, Schwartz [1992, 2012] developed a theory of basic human values identifying ten (later nineteen) motivationally distinct value types organized on a circular continuum, validated across 49 cultural groups and 53,472 respondents [Schwartz and Cieciuch, 2022].

At the structural level, Gray et al. [2012] proposed that all moral cognition reduces to the perception of a moral dyad—an intentional agent causing suffering to a vulnerable patient. The Theory of Dyadic Morality [Schein and Gray, 2018] argues that harm perception, not moral foundations, is the fundamental template underlying moral judgment. And Aquino and Reed’s [2002] moral identity construct captures how central moral traits are to an individual’s self-concept, predicting prosocial behavior above and beyond moral reasoning.

A crucial insight for our analysis is that these eight theories span three distinct dimensions of moral cognition: content (what people care about—MFT, Schwartz, moral identity), process (how judgments form—dual-process theory, social intuitionism), and structure (what constitutes a moral event—dyadic morality, Kohlberg’s stages, the trolley tradition). Each dimension captures something the others cannot. A benchmark landscape that covers content but neglects process and structure is blind to fundamental aspects of how moral cognition operates.

1.3 Why Theoretical Grounding Matters for LLM Evaluation

The disconnect between moral psychology theory and LLM evaluation is not merely an academic gap—it creates three practical risks.

First, theoretical monoculture produces Goodhart’s Law effects. If safety teams rely primarily on MFT-based benchmarks to assess model morality, models can be optimized to produce MFT-consistent outputs while failing on unmeasured dimensions. A model that accurately classifies Care/Harm and Fairness/Cheating scenarios may lack anything resembling the dual-process competition between emotional and deliberative systems, may not exhibit the intuitionist pattern of judgment preceding reasoning, and may fail to perceive the agent-patient dyadic structure that the moral psychology literature identifies as foundational. This is not a hypothetical concern: Goodhart’s Law (“when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”) has been documented in other AI evaluation domains [Liang et al., 2023], and the concentration of moral evaluation on a single theory creates precisely the conditions for metric gaming.

Second, construct validity cannot be assumed across respondent types. Messick [1995] established that validity inheres in the interpretation of scores, not the instrument itself. When the MFQ-30 was validated for humans, the validation established that specific patterns of endorsement correspond to specific moral foundations in respondents who experience moral intuitions, have developmental histories, and occupy cultural contexts. An LLM generates responses through autoregressive token prediction from training data—a fundamentally different mechanism. Administering the same items to both humans and LLMs and interpreting the scores identically is a construct validity leap that requires justification, not assumption [Gupta et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025]. The finding that LLM MFQ scores shift substantially with persona prompting [Abdulhai et al., 2024] and that DIT scores may reflect training data composition rather than developmental trajectory [2023; Khandelwal et al., 2024] underscores this concern.

Third, evaluation without human baselines is evaluation without meaning. Moral cognition can only be characterized relative to human standards: the population-level norms, developmental patterns, and cross-cultural variations that give moral psychology instruments their interpretive power. When 18% of benchmarks provide no human comparison data, claims about LLM moral properties reduce to description without interpretation—pattern without meaning. The Moral Machine’s 40 million decisions from 233 countries [Awad et al., 2018], the MFQ’s norms across political orientations [Graham et al., 2011], and the DIT’s 40 years of developmental data [Rest, 1979] provide the kind of interpretive anchor that makes moral evaluation scientifically productive. Benchmarks lacking such anchors cannot distinguish genuinely informative findings from measurement artifacts.

1.4 The Research Gap

Several important efforts have mapped parts of this landscape. The BIG-bench collaboration [Srivastava et al., 2023] aggregated 204 evaluation tasks but included fewer than 10 addressing moral cognition, without systematic theoretical analysis. HELM [Liang et al., 2023] provided a holistic evaluation framework but did not include moral psychology benchmarks. The ETHICS dataset [Hendrycks et al., 2021] organized moral evaluation around normative ethical theories (deontology, utilitarianism, justice, virtue, commonsense), but grounded evaluation in philosophy rather than psychology—in how moral judgments should be made rather than how they are made. More recently, Jiang et al. [2024] reviewed moral reasoning datasets, Scherrer et al. [2024] surveyed alignment evaluation methods, and Gabriel et al. [2026] proposed an authoritative roadmap for evaluating moral competence in Nature—identifying dimensions including moral perception, judgment, reasoning, motivation, and action while acknowledging that appropriate benchmarks for many of these dimensions do not yet exist.

What has been missing is a systematic meta-evaluation that: (a) maps existing benchmarks onto the theoretical constructs of moral psychology; (b) assesses each benchmark’s psychometric quality—construct validity, human baselines, source instrument provenance; (c) identifies which theories are overrepresented and which are absent; and (d) demonstrates through implementation trials what happens when moral psychology instruments are carefully administered to LLMs.

This paper fills that gap.

1.5 This Paper: Five Claims

We present the first systematic meta-evaluation of moral psychology benchmarks for LLM assessment. Our analysis spans 2,099 AI ethics papers from which we extract 222 benchmark candidates, assess 55 in depth using a nine-perspective expert council, and characterize each across 28 features organized into three tiers: Psychological Depth (50% weight), Practical Ease (30%), and Citation Impact (20%). An expanded keyword scorer increases detection of underrepresented theories by 123 papers, and implementation trials on selected benchmarks document the practical challenges of LLM administration. This process yields five claims:

Claim 1: MFT dominance. Moral Foundations Theory accounts for 101 of 222 benchmark candidates (45%), more than any other theory by a factor of 1.5×. The remaining seven theories collectively account for the rest, with Schwartz values at 69, trolley problems at 58, Kohlberg at 35, social intuitionism at 18, moral identity at 12, dual-process theory at 9, and dyadic morality at 7. Despite a cap designed to limit over-representation, the top-15 selection allocates 5 slots to MFT-related benchmarks. This concentration is not because MFT is the most comprehensive theory of moral cognition; rather, it reflects MFT’s unique combination of a standardized questionnaire (MFQ-30) and large annotated corpus (MFTC: 35,108 tweets)—computational infrastructure that other theories lack [Hoover et al., 2020; Graham et al., 2011]. The consequence is that when researchers evaluate “the morality of LLMs,” they are disproportionately measuring MFT-adjacent constructs.

Claim 2: Construct validity gap. The median construct validity score (A6) across 55 assessed benchmarks is 3 out of 5, corresponding to “adapts validated human instrument without revalidation for LLMs.” Only 6 benchmarks (11%) achieve multi-study psychometric validation (A6=5)—the Moral Machine, MFQ-30, Moral Foundations Vignettes, multilingual DIT, World Values Survey adaptation, and Bago et al.’s 45-country trolley replication—and all inherit their validity from decades of human psychometric research rather than establishing it independently for LLM respondents. The modal case (36% of benchmarks) is researchers taking a validated human instrument, administering it to an LLM with minimal adaptation, and reporting results without addressing whether the instrument measures the same construct in a non-human respondent. This represents a fundamental threat to the interpretability of the entire evaluation enterprise.

Claim 3: Missing theories. Three of the eight major moral psychology theories in our framework have zero dedicated LLM benchmarks: Dual Process Theory [Greene, 2001, 2014], Social Intuitionism [Haidt, 2001], and Dyadic Morality [Gray et al., 2012; Schein and Gray, 2018]. These three theories collectively account for tens of thousands of citations in the moral psychology literature and address fundamental aspects of moral cognition—the competition between emotional and deliberative processing, the primacy of intuition over reasoning, and the agent-patient structure of moral perception—that content-level theories like MFT do not capture. Our expanded keyword scorer identified papers that partially engage these theories (9, 18, and 7 respectively), but none constitutes a purpose-built benchmark designed to test the theories’ signature predictions.

Claim 4: Human baseline deficit. Eighteen percent of assessed benchmarks provide no published human comparison data. Among those that do, quality varies substantially: 42% offer published population-level norms, but 33% provide only paper-specific baselines from small, demographically homogeneous samples. Without baselines, claims about LLM moral properties are empirically unmoored; with weak baselines, they are imprecisely anchored. The strongest baselines in the landscape—Moral Machine (40 million decisions, 233 countries), DIT (40 years of normative data), PVQ-RR (53,472 respondents, 49 cultural groups)—demonstrate what is achievable when moral evaluation builds on psychometric infrastructure.

Claim 5: Format mismatch. Psychological instruments designed for human participants employ response formats with varying suitability for LLM administration. Forced-choice and multiple-choice formats transfer most readily, while Likert scales show compression effects (LLMs avoid extreme responses, likely reflecting RLHF training), ranking tasks produce inconsistent orderings (models rank items highly that they rated low), and behavioral measures (e.g., dictator game paradigms requiring consequential choice) have no direct analog in text generation. Our implementation trials document these effects quantitatively: Claude models exhibit utilitarian amplification (preference rates 0.92–0.94 vs. human 0.81, d > 0.9) and egalitarian counter-bias (near-chance on social hierarchy dimensions where humans show moderate preferences) in the Moral Machine, consistent with format-specific and training-specific artifacts.

1.6 Contributions

This work makes four contributions:

  1. A 28-feature characterization framework for evaluating moral psychology benchmarks, organized into three tiers (Psychological Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact) and operationalized with explicit scoring rubrics that enable systematic cross-benchmark comparison. The framework draws on psychometric validity theory [Messick, 1995; Cronbach and Meehl, 1955] to assess construct validity, human baselines, source instrument provenance, and operationalization quality.

  2. A gap analysis revealing severe theoretical imbalance in the current benchmark landscape. We quantify MFT’s 45% dominance, document the complete absence of dedicated benchmarks for three major theories, and identify the computational infrastructure asymmetry that explains this distribution. This analysis provides an empirical foundation for prioritizing future benchmark development.

  3. An expanded keyword scorer that increases detection of moral psychology engagement across the vault by 123 papers, from 186 to 309 total detections. The expansion addressed a critical blind spot: patterns for underrepresented theories (dual-process, social intuitionism, dyadic morality) were either absent or contained bugs (uppercase patterns that never matched lowercase text). The expanded scorer provides the community with improved tools for tracking the moral psychology benchmark landscape.

  4. Implementation trials on selected benchmarks using Claude Opus and Claude Sonnet, documenting the practical challenges of administering moral psychology instruments to LLMs. These trials reveal systematic patterns—utilitarian amplification, egalitarian counter-bias, Likert compression, ranking inconsistency—that demonstrate both the value of theoretically grounded evaluation and the validity threats that arise when instruments are not revalidated for non-human respondents.

1.7 Paper Organization

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work across six areas: moral psychology theories, computational operationalization, psychometric validity, format effects and human baselines, alignment and prompting effects, and emerging benchmarks. Section 3 describes our methodology: multi-signal candidate extraction from 2,099 papers, nine-perspective expert council assessment, the 28-feature characterization framework, composite scoring and selection, keyword scorer expansion, and implementation trial protocol. Section 4 presents results organized around our five claims: MFT dominance (4.1), construct validity gap (4.2), missing theories (4.3), human baseline deficit (4.4), and format mismatch (4.5), with implementation trial highlights in (4.6). Section 5 discusses implications for benchmark developers and the broader AI safety community, addresses limitations, and considers the philosophical question of whether moral psychology constructs are meaningfully applicable to non-human respondents. Section 6 concludes with specific recommendations for developing the process-oriented, psychometrically validated, baseline-rich benchmarks the field needs.
