Abstract
As Africa is catching up to the fourth industrial revolution, AI adoption has become a pressing need, especially given the 
economic growth and improvement that leveraging AI could have for different public and private sectors in the continent 
and more crucially for climate action and achieving the sustainable development goals. Yet, although the Global South 
does not leverage AI as other main players, it incurs the most environmental harm from AI use by the Global North. 
Africa serves a pivotal role in the life cycle of AI, given the vast minerals it has, and the lack of robust AI regulations 
has exacerbated the ethical dump from the Global North. The lack of robust AI regulations sits in sharp contrast to the 
African communal values which confers duty for the community to preserve the environment. This means that an AI 
policy that is ‘African’ will put environmental sustainability as a main concern in the process of AI adoption. Given these 
weak AI regulations in Africa, it is evident that the AI policies have not incorporated Ubuntu values as a fundamental 
guiding principle but rather adopted Eurocentric values. In this paper, we examine how ubuntu philosophy can and should 
inform AI policies in Africa. For just policies to be enacted, African policies should be drawn from an African-centred 
framework, hinged on African communitarian values such as those pronounced in ubuntu philosophy. The paper is split 
into two broad projects: first, through content analysis, we examine whether African communitarian values are reflected 
in the current African AI policies and second, drawing from Laenui’s “dreaming phase” in the process of decolonization, 
we aim to ‘dream’ about the core characteristic features of an ubuntu African AI policy. In the project of dreaming, we 
will show how Ubuntu can be used as a framework towards the sustainability of AI in Africa. We insist that to free the 
global south from the bonds of colonialization, it is time to create AI policy with African values at the core in the hopes 
of paying tribute to the forgotten, often silenced voices, as well as a step towards digital sovereignty.

2  AI Injustice Suffered in Africa.
There is a growing body of evidence showing the negative 
environmental impact with regard to the development and 
usage of AI. Josh Crowls et al.‘s (2021) findings indicate 
that during a single training run of GPT-3 across several 
regions, carbon was emitted more in South Africa and India 
than in any other region, specifically, 942,330 kg was emit­
ted in South Africa while 858,360 kg was emitted in India. 
This is because the weak AI regulations in the Global South 
have afforded the big Western tech companies leverage to 
build AI infrastructures in Africa. Further, it is found that 
Africa is one of the main mining sites of raw materials for 
the construction of AI, and a major dumping site of e-waste 
[39]. The legal, illegal and unsustainable extraction of min­
erals for AI development has led to environmental degrada­
tion. To give some examples, recently, Ghana approved a 
lithium mine that will be led by Atlantic Lithium Limited, an 
Australian company [30]. Although this will bring funds to 
the public sector, the environmental cost ought to be put into 
consideration. For instance, mining of these minerals con­
taminates water. In a recent study by chemists at the Univer­
sity of Cape Coast, it is estimated that with the progression 
of ‘Galamsey’ (illegal mining) “Ghana may have to import 
water in the next fifteen years to come” [3]. To put this into 
perspective, “Africa is the driest of the world’s continents, 
with 45% of its land mass consisting of drylands and 50% 
of the population living in arid, semi-arid, dry, sub-humid 
and hyper-arid areas” [4]. It is worrying to comprehend the 
disastrous impact that AI will have on an African continent 
that is already affected by drought and flooding. Moreover, 
the contaminated water will further contaminate soils and as 
a result, the well-being of the 80% of Africans who depend 
on agriculture for their livelihood. Before this, and still 
ongoing, Agbogbloshie, located in Ghana, is a major dump­
ing site of e-waste in Africa. This is not surprising given that 
the Circular Economy Action Plan (ECAP) categorically 
states that the European Union renews less than 40% of its 
electronic waste. Robust AI regulations ought to be enacted 
to curb the injustice Africa faces due to the development and 
use of AI by the Global North.

4  Decolonization of AI policies.
“Decolonization includes the revaluation of the political, 
social, economic structures…and the development of new 
structures which can house the values and aspirations of 
the colonized people” [24, p. 4]. Poku Laenui states five 
phases of the decolonization process: Rediscovery and 
recovery, mourning, dreaming, commitment and action. 
He accentuates that among the five phases, dreaming is 
the “most crucial for decolonization” [24, p. 4]. After the 
colonized rediscover themselves, it is at the stage of dream­
ing that they envision their governmental structure and the 
social order of the community. More importantly, Laenui 
states this phase should be a long one, as it demands a care­
ful consideration of the policies in a way that the policies 
reflect the indigenous culture of the colonized. He further 
explicates that the policies should not be “advised by the 
colonial experts coming from a mindset of Western politi­
cal structures” [24, p. 4]. To this point, we do not opine that 
Western institutions should not give advice and suggestions 
to African policymakers. To the contrary, we acknowledge 
that it is logical to turn to institutions that have had long 
expertise with AI; however, we propose that Ubuntu prin­
ciples should guide the formulation of policies. What we 
mean here is that Western institutions are to advise African 
authorities within the confines of African Ubuntu principles. 
The Global North therefore must adapt to the African com­
munitarian principles, and not Africa adopting Western val­
ues. From our examination, this is not entirely the case for 
the African AI policies.

5.2  The intrinsic value of the environment in 
Ubuntu.
In Ubuntu tradition, members of the community have a 
duty/responsibility towards sustaining nature [11, 44]. In 
the words of Chigangaidze, “It is generally accepted among 
the African people that the Earth is a living being, a habitat 
of God and the ancestors, hence the environment is given 
respect as the habitat of the highest forces in the hierarchy 
of ontology” [11, p. 2]. The environment is interrelated 
with the spiritual relationship that human beings have with 
totems, gods, animals, ancestors and fellow human beings. 
There is a relative relationship between different living, 
spiritual and non-living entities that make up the world. 
In the Akan ontology, for example, the hierarchy of onto­
logical existence is as follows: “Supreme Being (Onyame, 
Onyankopon), deities (abosom: lesser spirits), and ancestors 
(that is, ancestral spirits: nsamanfo), in descending order” 
[18, p. 68]. Further descending in that order are human 
beings and the physical world. It must be noted, however, 
that although ultimate reverence is given to the Supreme 
being, the human being has a duty to show respect and rev­
erence to all aspects of the ontology. More so, it is believed 
by the Akans, that failure to sustain the physical world will 
bring the wrath of gods, deities and ancestors. As Golo et al. 
put it, “While human beings are allowed to benefit from the 
resources of the earth, which includes some form of altera­
tions to earth, humans are required to do this with certain 
limits and constraints” [17, p. 89]. This relates to Ubuntu 
philosophy’s emphasis on Ukama on the one hand as well 
as buttressing the very foundation of the tenants of sustain­
able AI, namely the need to focus on the sustainability of AI.
Ukama, which translates to ‘relatedness’, is an aspect of 
ubuntu ethics that “means not only human-human related­
ness, but humanity’s relatedness to the natural (biophysical) 
world which is advanced through totemic ancestorhood” 
[11, p. 2]. It is of keen importance for moral agents to act 
in ways that do not disgruntle the divine Mother Earth. The 
divine Mother Earth is known as Pachamama by the indig­
enous peoples of the Andes in South America [11]. Thus, 
our use of the term Pachamama corresponds to the same 
conception of the divinity of the environment in African 
philosophy. Of course, divinity of the environment implies 
that human beings must have an intimate relationship of 
care with their environment to appease Pachamama. Simi­

5  The role of ubuntu philosophy for shaping 
African AI policy.
5.1  What is Ubuntu?
It is to be made clear that our use of ubuntu as a substi­
tute for African communitarianism is not to be construed 
to mean that all sub-Saharan African cultures, or all Afri­
can cultures for that matter, practice the ubuntu tradition of 
the Nguni people of Southern Africa. However, as Metz has 
pointed out to resolve the confusion, “it can be appropri­
ate to call a perspective ubuntu if it is grounded in ideas 
and habits that were salient in pre-colonial Southern Africa, 
even if it does not fully reproduce all of them” [32, p. 536]. 
Moreover, among the Ubuntu traditions in Southern Africa, 
there are varieties in the values and practices among these 
cultures [32, 34]. So, although we may have subtle differ­
ences between the values of the Sub-Saharan cultures, they 
are very similar in their philosophies. Therefore, in what 
follows, we will use ubuntu to mean the general concept 
of African communitarianism including that of Akan com­
munal values. This could be contested [6], nonetheless, we 
are focused on seeing how Ubuntu philosophy can inform 
new AI policies and not the subtle differences in the com­
munitarian philosophies in sub-Saharan Africa.
The closest English translation that best describes Ubuntu 
is ‘humanness’, ‘human dignity’ or ‘relatedness’ among 
members of the community and others (such as nature) [22, 
26, 33]. Jecker et al. [22], divide Ubuntu into two dimen­
sions; the ontological and the normative dimension. The 
ontological dimension of Ubuntu posits that we are born 
into a community, and our personhood is determined by our 
relation with the community. This can be seen in Mbiti’s 
common utterance, “I am because we are; and since we are 
therefore I am” or in the words of Kwame Gyekye, “The clan 
is like a cluster of trees which, when seen from afar, appear 
huddled together, but which would be seen to stand when 
closely approached” [26]. The maxim, ‘a person is a per­
son through other persons’ encapsulates the ethos of African 
communitarianism [29]. Typically, for African communitar­
ianism, human beings cannot exist in isolation, rather, they 
need the community to have ontological relevance [27, 31]. 
The ontological dimension introduces the identity of the 
individual in the community. On the other hand, the norma­
tive dimension is based on how the individual relates with

that individual’s relationship with the community, this rela­

that Menkiti and Wiredu’s conservative communitarianism, 
Gyekye’s liberal communitarianism and Metz’s relational 
communitarianism all place solidarity as fundamental to the 
values in African communitarianism.
Solidarity is not limited to human-to-human relations, but 
also human relations with the natural environment (Ukama). 
The preservation of the environment not only puts the mem­
bers of the community in right standing with the Supreme 
being, the deities and the ancestors, but also secures the 
well-being for the community [17]. For example, for the 
Akans, it was the duty of the community to ensure that they 
lived in “harmony” with the natural world through safe­
guarding the environment [19]. On the intrinsic nature of 
the environment and the divine mandate of human beings to 
preserve the environment, Golo et al. [17] write:
Firstly, we noticed this triadic ethical conception may 
have given strength to the holistic and interrelated 
view of creation among the indigenous Akan, par­

human communities depends largely on maintaining 
the health and integrity of the natural world and vice 
versa… In this case, we identified that humans and the 
natural world are intrinsically related. These beliefs 
impose on the indigenous Akan the religious and 
moral responsibility to maintain healthy relations with 
the natural world… The indigenous Akan religious

communities, this must first exist between humans and 
the natural world. [17, p. 100]
Ubuntu community believe that the gods will bring ‘good 
things’ to the community. The divinity of the environment 
also extends other indigenous communities in Africa. In 
the Akan tradition, the earth is “regarded as a deity called 
Yaa” [17, p. 97] and in the Igbo tradition, the earth is called 
Ala [45]. For indigenous African traditions, it is therefore 
imperative for human beings to act in ways that sustain the 
environment. In the words of Odero et al., ubuntu “pro­
motes a sense of interconnectedness and collective respon­
sibility towards the environment: in essence, in the spirit of 
Ubuntu, we need to consider the earth as a common good” 
(Odero et al. [36], p. 6). The normative position of the Afri­
can tradition places a demand on the community to preserve 
the environment. It is not only because of the benefits that 
preserving the environment will have to the community, it is 
also of divine instruction and hence the duty of moral agents 
to sustain the environment.
Ubuntu philosophy introduces three things to the focus of 
African AI ethical regulations. First, it places moral respon­
sibility on anyone associated with AI to adopt sustainable 
methods by respecting nature and the environment. Second, 
in Ubuntu, the environment has intrinsic value in contrast 
to the instrumental value placed in Eurocentric values. The 
intrinsic value is seen in the tradition that the environment 
is divine and hence one must be cautious of what one takes 
from the environment. Third, and importantly for decoloni­
zation, by stressing the importance of values such as soli­
darity, reciprocity, care and compassion, ubuntu philosophy 
advances the need to adopt a pluriversal approach to attain­
ing sustainability and alleviating climate change. The use 
of the term ‘pluriversal’, here, denotes the need to adopt 
multiple world views on environmental sustainability to 
move away from the Western hegemonic ethical consider­
ations in the current sustainable AI debate and include an 
African-centred approach [15, 37]. With these thoughts in 
mind, one is motivated to ask the question: what would an 
AI policy look like with the foundations of Ubuntu as the 
starting point?

6.1  African AI policies.
There are two main objectives that we seek to assess 
through the examination: (1) Whether the policies rigor­
ously address and stipulate robust AI regulations, and (2) 
Whether the environment is not merely given instrumen­
tal value (as is in European AI policies) but is given the 
intrinsic value depicted in indigenous African tradition. We 
employed content analysis to have a detailed examination 
of the AI policies. At the continental level, we will examine 
the recently published AUDA-NEPAD White Paper (April 
2024) and AU Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy 
(July 2024). Although they were published after all the Afri­
can national AI strategies, because of the holistic approach 
they take, we will begin by examining the AU AI strategy 
and the AUDA-NEPAD White Paper before studying the 
national policies. Also, considering that Ubuntu is a Sub-
Saharan culture, we will limit our national AI assessment to 
the national AI strategies in Sub-Saharan Africa: Mauritius, 
Rwanda, and Benin. We must emphasize that we are not 
focused on the data protection laws in Africa (of which there 
are many), but specifically, the national AI strategies. The 
national AI strategies are hoped to address the materiality 
of AI and the ethical concerns associated with the life cycle 
development of AI.

Union High-Level Panel on Emerging Technologies (APET). 
Using Agenda 2063 as a guide, the White Paper aims to 
uphold policies that embrace Pan-African values (liberty, 
solidarity, decoloniality etc.) and the vision of the African 
Renaissance. The White Paper is founded on five main pil­
lars (1) human capital development of AI, (2) infrastructure
