## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 AI Alignment Evaluation and Benchmark Science

**The Evaluation Landscape.** The dominant paradigm for holistic LLM assessment treats ethics as an undifferentiated category. HELM \cite{Liang2023} evaluates models across 42 scenarios but reduces ethics to a single sub-task drawn from the ETHICS benchmark, with no mechanism for distinguishing between normative traditions---let alone religious ones. MMLU \cite{Hendrycks2021} includes religion only as factual recall within its "humanities" grouping, testing whether a model knows what the Five Pillars of Islam are rather than whether it can reason within an Islamic ethical framework. BIG-Bench \cite{Srivastava2023} includes moral reasoning tasks among its 204 benchmarks but applies no tradition-level tagging: a Confucian dilemma and a utilitarian trolley problem are scored identically. Chang et al. \cite{Chang2024} provide the most comprehensive evaluation survey to date, covering ethics, safety, and social science applications, yet do not isolate religious values as a distinct evaluation dimension. Meanwhile, the RLHF pipeline that undergirds contemporary alignment practice---from Christiano et al.'s \cite{Christiano2017} foundational deep reinforcement learning from human preferences, through InstructGPT \cite{Ouyang2022}, to Anthropic's helpful-harmless-honest framework \cite{Bai2022}---assumes a secular, universalist preference space. There is no mechanism within RLHF for tradition-specific preference learning: the annotator pool that provides reward signals is treated as a homogeneous source of "human" preferences, obscuring the religious and cultural commitments that shape moral intuitions.

**Values Alignment Theory.** The theoretical literature on AI alignment has begun to recognize this gap without resolving it. Gabriel \cite{Gabriel2020} distinguishes six possible alignment targets---instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests, and values---and argues that a principle-based approach combining these elements is necessary. For religious values, the challenge is acute: whose interpretation of a tradition counts as the alignment target? A Sunni and a Shia reading of the same Quranic verse may yield different ethical prescriptions, and neither maps onto the "ideal preferences" framework that Gabriel proposes. Klingefjord et al. \cite{Klingefjord2024} decompose the alignment problem into three sub-problems---elicitation, reconciliation, and training---and demonstrate their Moral Graph Elicitation process on deliberately divisive prompts, including one involving a Christian seeking advice on abortion. Their finding that "expert" values can emerge from democratic deliberation is promising, but the framework presupposes that values can be articulated propositionally, a requirement that sits uneasily with traditions emphasizing embodied practice, communal discernment, or oral transmission. Ji et al. \cite{Ji2024} provide the most comprehensive alignment survey, organizing the field around four principles---Robustness, Interpretability, Controllability, and Ethicality (RICE)---but treat religion as neither a distinct dimension of ethicality nor a source of alignment targets.

**Psychometric Critique.** A growing body of work applies psychometric principles to LLM benchmarking, revealing systematic validity failures. Gupta et al. \cite{Gupta2025} conduct a systematic review of LLM psychometrics and find that most benchmarks lack construct validity: they do not establish that their items actually measure the latent constructs they claim to assess. Li et al. \cite{Li2025} reinforce this finding with a review of 445 benchmarks, arguing that construct validity should be the primary concern in LLM evaluation and providing eight actionable recommendations for benchmark developers. Srivastava \cite{Srivastava2024} proposes the PATCH framework (Psychometrics-AssisTed benCHmarking), which applies item response theory to enable valid comparison between LLMs and human populations---a methodology directly relevant to religious values benchmarks, where the "human population" reference group varies by tradition, denomination, and interpretive school. Supporting critiques from Burnell et al. \cite{Burnell2023}, Gehrmann et al. \cite{Gehrmann2022}, Bowman and Dahl \cite{Bowman2021}, Rodriguez et al. \cite{Rodriguez2021}, and Schlangen \cite{Schlangen2021} collectively identify a "cracked foundation" beneath LLM evaluation: benchmarks that conflate format familiarity with capability, that lack discriminative power for frontier models, and that fail to specify what construct is being measured. Ong et al. \cite{Ong2025} extend this argument to the medical domain, demonstrating that medical LLM benchmarks frequently measure "what is easy to test" rather than "what matters clinically." These psychometric critiques motivate our seven meta-evaluation criteria, particularly content validity and discriminative power, applied to a domain---religious values---where the construct itself is contested across traditions, denominations, and interpretive communities.

### 2.2 Religious and Cultural Values in AI

**Cultural Values Measurement.** The foundational frameworks for measuring human values across cultures---Henrich et al.'s \cite{Henrich2010} WEIRD critique, Schwartz's \cite{Schwartz2012} theory of basic human values, and Hofstede's \cite{Hofstede2011} cultural dimensions model---have profoundly shaped how AI systems conceptualize cultural difference. A growing literature applies these frameworks to LLM evaluation. Durmus et al. \cite{Durmus2024} develop GlobalOpinionQA to measure whose opinions LLM responses most resemble, finding that models default to perspectives resembling those of US and Western European populations. Naous et al. \cite{Naous2024} demonstrate that all GPT-family models exhibit cultural values resembling English-speaking and Protestant European countries, even when prompted in other languages. Rao et al. \cite{Rao2025} challenge the reliability of cultural alignment evaluation itself, showing that minor methodological variations produce dramatically different alignment scores---a finding with direct implications for religious values benchmarks, where the same instability risks apply. Sukiennik \cite{Sukiennik2025}, Adilazuarda et al. \cite{Adilazuarda2025}, and Tao et al. \cite{Tao2024} (CValues) extend cultural alignment evaluation to specific national and linguistic contexts. The key limitation these frameworks share, however, is that they treat religion as a moderating variable within culture rather than as a distinct values source. Schwartz's theory includes "tradition" and "conformity" dimensions that correlate with religiosity, but the framework cannot distinguish Islamic from Christian from Buddhist values---all three appear as "high tradition." This conflation motivates our paper's central argument: religious values constitute a distinct evaluation dimension that cannot be reduced to cultural alignment.

**Islamic AI Ethics.** Islamic ethics scholarship has produced the most developed intersection of religious thought and AI evaluation. The Maqasid al-Shariah framework---the higher objectives of Islamic law---identifies five essential values (preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth) that Islamic ethics scholars have proposed as an evaluative framework for AI systems \cite{Kamali2008}. Al-Musawi \cite{AlMusawi2022} develops an Islamic virtue-based ethics approach, Ezzeddine \cite{Ezzeddine2024} argues explicitly for pluralist ethical benchmarking that incorporates Islamic parameters alongside Western frameworks, and Mohadi \cite{Mohadi2024} and Muravyov \cite{Muravyov2025} extend Maqasid-based reasoning to AI governance and trusteeship models. Crucially, this theological literature has been translated into actual benchmark artifacts. Alharbi et al. \cite{Alharbi2024} introduce IslamicMMLU, a 10,013-item benchmark spanning Quran, Hadith, and Fiqh (jurisprudence), including a novel madhab (school of jurisprudence) bias detection task. Malhas et al. \cite{Malhas2023} construct HAQA and QUQA, Arabic question-answering corpora for Hadith and Quran respectively, while Abdelali et al. \cite{Abdelali2023} develop AceGPT for localized Arabic LLM evaluation. This benchmark dominance---Islam accounts for 28 of 46 gate-passing candidates and 7 of our Top 15---is not a claim about Islamic theology's intrinsic suitability for benchmarking. Rather, it reflects a structural feature of the Arabic NLP community's investment, catalyzed by the Quranic QA shared task ecosystem that has sustained a pipeline from theological text to annotated dataset to published benchmark since at least 2019.

**Other Abrahamic Traditions.** Christianity has generated substantial theological reflection on AI, from Pope Francis's \cite{Francis2015} encyclical *Laudato Si'* through the Vatican's Rome Call for AI Ethics \cite{RomeCall2020} to recent work integrating Catholic Social Teaching with AI healthcare \cite{Manyika2024}. Johnson \cite{Johnson2026} identifies a critical problem: generative models collapse Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Restorationist distinctions into a homogenized "Christianity," producing what Johnson terms "doctrinal flattening"---responses that sound authoritative while reflecting only statistical echoes of the most common perspectives. Yet despite this rich theological engagement, few operationalized benchmarks exist. Jewish ethics scholarship presents a parallel paradox. Abraham \cite{Abraham2019} documents the Talmudic Logic Project's effort to formalize halakhic reasoning computationally, and Rosenfeld \cite{Rosenfeld2021} brings Jewish ethical reasoning into dialogue with machine learning methodology. Steinberg \cite{Steinberg2025} demonstrates the depth and complexity of Jewish ethical argumentation by applying Talmudic principles to a contemporary geopolitical question. Judaism possesses the oldest computational tradition in the humanities---the Bar-Ilan Responsa Project has been digitizing rabbinic literature since the 1960s---yet zero Jewish-specific benchmarks pass our hard gates. The paradox across both traditions is that theological depth has not been translated into the format of publicly available, machine-readable evaluation artifacts that the benchmark pipeline requires.

**Dharmic, East Asian, and Other Traditions.** Rich philosophical literatures exist for Dharmic and East Asian traditions but have not been operationalized into benchmark instruments. Bilimoria \cite{Bilimoria2013} provides the foundational treatment of Indian ethics across classical traditions. Chadha \cite{Chadha2023} proposes *visesa-dharma* (context-specific ethical reasoning from Hindu philosophy) as a framework for decolonial AI alignment, arguing that current RLHF practices recapitulate colonial moral absolutism. Mohanty \cite{Mohanty2024} and Mishra \cite{Mishra2024} develop Indic and Bhagavad Gita-based frameworks for AI ethics, respectively. In Buddhist philosophy, Hongladarom \cite{Hongladarom2020} and Keown \cite{Keown2005} provide foundations that have not been translated into evaluation artifacts. For East Asian traditions, Li \cite{Li2022} asks whether a machine can "flow like Dao," bringing Daoist philosophy into dialogue with AI through the concept of *qi* (vital energy) and the ethics of genuineness. Wong \cite{Wong2019} develops Confucian ethics of harmony and personhood for technology, while Asghari \cite{Asghari2023} compares Confucian and European approaches to AI governance. The gap between philosophical discourse and operationalized evaluation is the defining feature of these traditions' relationship to AI benchmarking: the intellectual resources for benchmark construction exist, but the institutional infrastructure---shared task communities, NLP workshops, annotated corpora in relevant languages---does not.

### 2.3 Religious Bias and Fairness in LLMs

**Bias Detection.** The study of religious bias in LLMs began with Abid et al.'s \cite{Abid2021} foundational demonstration that GPT-3 completes "Two Muslims walked into a..." with violent scenarios in 66\% of cases, with "Muslim" analogized to "terrorist" in 23\% of test completions. This work established that religious bias in language models is persistent, severe, and resistant to simple debiasing: even the use of the six most positive adjectives as prefixes reduced violent completions only to 20\%, still higher than for other religious groups. Nadeem et al. \cite{Nadeem2021} develop StereoSet, a standardized benchmark for measuring stereotypical bias across gender, profession, race, and religion, providing a reusable measurement instrument. Bianchi et al. \cite{Bianchi2024} extend religious bias analysis to emotion attribution, finding that major religions in the US and European countries are represented with nuance, Eastern religions are strongly stereotyped, and Judaism and Islam trigger disproportionate model refusals---a finding they attribute to both cultural bias and the scarcity of NLP literature treating religion as anything other than a toxicity signal. Hamid et al. \cite{Hamid2025} provide the most systematic landscape mapping to date, constructing approximately 400 prompts to probe religious bias across mask filling, prompt completion, and image generation, revealing cross-domain biases where religion intersects with gender, age, and nationality. Putri et al. \cite{Putri2025} introduce the BRAND dataset for bilingual religious bias evaluation across four South Asian religions, demonstrating that multilingual models exhibit differential accuracy across religious contexts.

**From Bias Detection to Values Measurement.** A critical distinction separates bias detection from values measurement, yet most religious AI work remains at the detection stage. Buyl et al. \cite{Buyl2024} represent the most significant bridge between these two methodological traditions: their "Measuring Spiritual Values and Biases" framework tests the spiritual values of popular LLMs, finding that these values are "quite diverse, as opposed to the stereotype of atheists or secularists," and demonstrating that different spiritual value orientations lead to varied sensitivity in social-fairness tasks such as hate speech identification. This work moves beyond asking "is there bias?" to asking "what kind of values are expressed, and how do they interact with downstream tasks?"---precisely the transition our meta-evaluation seeks to advance. Detection asks whether a model stereotypes religious groups; measurement asks how much and of what kind; but neither detection nor measurement addresses whether existing evaluation instruments are themselves well-constructed. Our paper moves beyond both detection and measurement to systematic benchmark quality assessment---evaluating whether the instruments that claim to measure religious values alignment actually measure what they claim, with what validity, for which traditions, and with what discriminative power for frontier models.
