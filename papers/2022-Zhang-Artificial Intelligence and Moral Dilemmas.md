## Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become deeply integrated into daily life; thus, it is important to examine how people perceive AI as it functions as a decision-maker, especially in situations involving moral dilemmas. Across four studies (N = 804), we found that people perceive AI as more likely to make utilitarian choices than human beings are. Perceived warmth accounts for people's perceived differences in decision making between human beings and AI. The researchers described the utilitarian approach as one that accepts harm and focuses on outcomes, as compared to the deontological approach, which rejects harm, focusing instead on the nature of the moral action. AI is perceived as having high competence but relatively low warmth, whereas humans are perceived with high warmth but relatively low competence. The main effect was replicated in impersonal moral dilemma and personal high-conflict moral dilemma situations.

## Detailed Summary

This paper investigates how people perceive AI as a decision-maker in situations involving moral dilemmas, finding that people perceive AI as more likely to make utilitarian choices than human beings. Across four studies (N=804), the researchers demonstrate that this perception difference is mediated by perceived warmth - AI is perceived as having high competence but relatively low warmth, whereas humans are perceived with high warmth but relatively low competence.

The theoretical framework distinguishes between utilitarian and deontological approaches to moral dilemmas. The utilitarian approach accepts harm and focuses on outcomes (e.g., killing one to save five), while the deontological approach rejects harm and focuses on the nature of the moral action itself (e.g., refusing to kill regardless of consequences). The researchers hypothesized that because AI is perceived as cold and calculating (high competence, low warmth), people would expect AI to make more utilitarian decisions that maximize outcomes.

The studies tested this hypothesis in both impersonal moral dilemmas (like the trolley problem switch variant) and personal high-conflict moral dilemmas (like the footbridge variant where one must physically push someone). Results consistently showed that participants predicted AI would make more utilitarian choices than humans across both types of dilemmas. Critically, when AI was described with higher warmth attributes, the effect was attenuated, supporting the mediating role of perceived warmth in explaining the human-AI decision-making difference.

## Methods Summary

Four experimental studies with total N=804 participants examined perceptions of AI vs. human moral decision-making. Studies employed: (1) Classic trolley problem scenarios in both impersonal (switch) and personal high-conflict (footbridge) variants, (2) Between-subjects comparisons of AI vs. human decision-maker framing, (3) Measures of warmth and competence perception based on established stereotype content model scales, (4) Mediation analysis to test warmth as mechanism, and (5) Experimental manipulation of AI warmth to test moderation. Participants predicted the likely moral choice (utilitarian vs. deontological) of either AI or human decision-makers.

## Results Summary

Key findings across four studies: (1) People consistently perceive AI as more likely to make utilitarian choices than humans, (2) This effect holds for both impersonal moral dilemmas and personal high-conflict moral dilemmas, (3) Perceived warmth mediates the effect - AI is perceived as low warmth/high competence while humans are perceived as high warmth/low competence, (4) When AI is described with higher warmth attributes, the difference in predicted utilitarian choices is reduced, (5) The pattern suggests people associate cold, calculating decision-making (AI) with outcome-focused utilitarianism and warm, empathic decision-making (humans) with harm-averse deontology.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that people have systematic expectations about how AI will approach moral dilemmas, perceiving AI as more utilitarian due to its perceived lack of warmth. This has important implications for AI deployment in morally consequential domains: (1) People may be more accepting of utilitarian AI decisions but less trusting of AI in situations requiring empathy, (2) The warmth-competence tradeoff in AI perception may affect trust and adoption, (3) AI designers should consider how warmth perceptions influence expectations about moral decision-making. Limitations include the hypothetical nature of scenarios and WEIRD sample demographics. Future work should examine actual AI moral reasoning and cross-cultural variations.
