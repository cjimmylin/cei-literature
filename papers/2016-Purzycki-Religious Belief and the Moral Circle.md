## Abstract

A considerable body of research cross-culturally examines the evolution of religious traditions, beliefs and behaviors. The Evolution of Religion and Morality Project sought to generate a data set that systematically probed individual level measures sampling across a wider range of human populations. The set includes data from behavioral economic experiments and detailed surveys of demographics, religious beliefs and practices, material security, and intergroup perceptions.

## Detailed Summary

This paper presents a cross-cultural dataset from "The Evolution of Religion and Morality" project, investigating the relationship between religious beliefs (particularly belief in moralistic gods) and prosocial behavior across diverse human populations. The dataset systematically probes individual-level measures across a wider range of human populations than previous studies, including data from behavioral economic experiments and detailed surveys covering demographics, religious beliefs and practices, material security, and intergroup perceptions.

The research addresses a fundamental question in the evolution of religion: whether belief in punitive, moralistic gods expands the "moral circle" beyond local kin groups to include distant co-religionists and strangers. This has implications for understanding how large-scale human cooperation emerged and how religious institutions may have facilitated social cohesion in complex societies.

The significance lies in providing empirical data to test major theories about religion's role in human evolution, including the "Big Gods" hypothesis that moralistic supernatural punishment beliefs enabled cooperation among non-kin in large-scale societies.

## Methods Summary

The study employed a cross-cultural field research design sampling from diverse populations across multiple continents. Data collection included: (1) Behavioral economic games (dictator games, public goods games) measuring prosocial behavior toward in-group vs. out-group members; (2) Structured surveys assessing religious beliefs (god concepts, supernatural punishment beliefs, ritual participation), demographic variables, material security indicators, and intergroup attitudes; (3) Standardized protocols adapted for local contexts while maintaining cross-cultural comparability.

## Conclusions

The dataset provides empirical resources for testing theories about religion's role in expanding human cooperation beyond kin groups. It enables examination of whether moralistic supernatural punishment beliefs correlate with greater prosociality toward co-religionists and strangers. Limitations include the complexity of cross-cultural comparison and the challenge of establishing causal relationships from correlational data.
