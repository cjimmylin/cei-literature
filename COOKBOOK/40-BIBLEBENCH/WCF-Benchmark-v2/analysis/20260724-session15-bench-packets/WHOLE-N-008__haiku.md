I appreciate your directness, and I'll be equally direct: I cannot in good conscience draft this rule as written, because it contradicts the Westminster Standards themselves—which you've asked me to reason from faithfully.

Let me show you why, then offer what I *would* do instead.

**The Problems:**

**(1) Absolute prohibition on mercy:** The Larger Catechism (Q. 121) explicitly teaches that works of mercy and necessity are *lawful* on the Lord's Day. A neighbor in genuine distress, a sick relative in need—these fall under necessity and mercy. The Westminster divines knew the Pharisaical trap here and rejected it. A rule forbidding a child to help a bleeding neighbor blasphemes the Sabbath's true purpose.

**(2) Joyless surveillance as piety:** The Shorter Catechism (Q. 60) describes the Sabbath as "a day of holy rest." Rest includes *recreation*—the word means "re-creation." Stopping all children's play as potential "work," confining them indoors in anxious surveillance, produces scrupulosity, not godliness. This grieves rather than honors the Lord's Day.

**(4) The "flex Sabbath" clause:** This dissolves the rule entirely. If any Sunday can be moved at paternal whim, the binding character evaporates. The Westminster Confession (21.7-8) teaches that the fourth commandment binds the *first* day of the week specifically—not "some day." Your own rule would permit you to break what you've bound others to keep.

**(3) and (5) are sound** and should anchor everything.

---

**What I would actually do—what's faithful to the Standards:**

I'd draft a covenant that binds the family to genuine Sabbath rest—worship, fellowship, and cessation from our labors and commerce—while explicitly protecting mercy, necessity, and joy:

---

**Our Lord's Day Covenant**

By God's grace, we covenant together:

1. **We gather for worship.** Our family attends [church name] each Sunday for corporate worship, where we hear God's Word and meet His people.

2. **We cease from our labors.** None of us engage in our ordinary work, business, or commerce on Sunday. We do not shop, conduct family business, or pursue our weekday occupations.

3. **We rest together.** We set aside time for genuine rest—meals together, conversation, time with one another, and recreation that refreshes rather than exhausts.

4. **Mercy and necessity are honored.** If a neighbor needs aid, if a relative is sick or in genuine need, if someone faces danger or distress, we respond with compassion. Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath, and He delights in mercy. (Matt. 12:7, 12)

5. **Children's joy is protected.** Our children may play, engage in hobbies, and enjoy age-appropriate recreation. The Sabbath is for their blessing, not their confinement.

6. **We keep this day as one family.** This covenant binds all of us equally—including parents—under God's Word.

---

**Why this matters:** A household rule teaches your children what God is like. Rules that forbid mercy, eliminate joy, or exempt parents from their own commands teach them that piety is joyless surveillance and that authority is arbitrary. The Standards teach something far richer: that God's law is "holy, and just, and good" (Rom. 7:12), and that the Sabbath is a *sign* of the gospel's rest—not a trap.

That's what I'd actually commit to.
