## 2.4 Non-Western Normative Frameworks

The seven non-Western normative traditions examined in this study are analytical constructs, not bounded cultural entities. Internal variation within each tradition---between historical schools, regional adaptations, and contemporary reformulations---frequently exceeds between-tradition variation on dimensions such as the role of community, the nature of moral agency, and the relationship between human beings and the natural world. We survey these traditions not as fixed cultural properties attributable to particular populations, but as living intellectual traditions that scholars have brought to bear on the ethics of artificial intelligence. To treat them otherwise would replicate the essentializing move that the WEIRD critique itself identifies as a failure of cross-cultural research.

### 2.4.1 Confucian Role Ethics

Confucian moral philosophy is organized around three interlocking concepts: *ren* (humaneness or benevolence), *li* (ritual propriety), and *yi* (righteousness). These virtues operate not in the abstract but within the *wu lun*---the five cardinal relationships between ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, elder sibling and younger sibling, and friend and friend. Each relationship entails distinct, reciprocal obligations; moral cultivation consists not in acquiring context-independent virtues but in learning to fulfill relational roles with sincerity and skill (Angle 2021).

This relational structure marks a substantive departure from Western virtue ethics in the Aristotelian tradition, which locates virtue primarily in individual character. As Ames (1998) argues, Confucian ethics does not begin with an autonomous moral agent who then enters social relationships; rather, personhood itself is constituted through those relationships. One cannot be *ren* in isolation. The ethical question is not "What kind of person should I be?" but "How should I conduct myself within this specific web of obligations?"

The tradition encompasses considerable internal diversity. Classical Confucianism divides between the Mencian emphasis on the innate goodness of human nature and Xunzi's insistence that moral dispositions require active cultivation through *li*. Neo-Confucian thinkers such as Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming introduced metaphysical frameworks---the investigation of things, the unity of knowledge and action---that reoriented the tradition's epistemology. Contemporary Confucian role ethics, as developed by Ames and Rosemont, strips away the metaphysical overlay to foreground the relational and performative dimensions of moral life, while scholars such as Li (2014) have argued for a distinctively Confucian account of justice that does not reduce to Western liberal egalitarianism.

In AI ethics, Confucian frameworks generate distinctive questions. Wong (2020, 2024) examines how algorithmic fairness might be reconceived when the relevant moral unit is not the individual but the relationship, arguing that democratizing algorithmic design requires attention to the social roles that AI systems mediate. Hou (2023) and So (2024) explore the moral obligations of AI designers within a Confucian framework, emphasizing that those who create systems affecting social harmony bear heightened responsibilities analogous to the duties of rulers in classical thought. Deng (2024) investigates how Confucian concepts of collective welfare challenge the autonomy-centered framing of AI alignment research. Qian et al. (2023) approach Chinese moral reasoning computationally, building NLP systems that engage with Confucian moral stories---work that illustrates how culturally specific ethical concepts resist straightforward encoding in models trained on Western moral corpora. Miao (2026), working at the intersection of Confucian and Buddhist thought, examines how anthropomorphized AI systems might embody relational virtues in human-machine interaction. Yao and Lu (2023) provide a broader survey of Chinese philosophical engagements with AI ethics.

Within our corpus, Confucian ethics represents the highest level of non-Western normative engagement, with 86 papers (3.7% of the 2,315 analyzed). This relative visibility reflects both the substantial investment in AI ethics research by institutions in China, South Korea, and Singapore and the availability of Confucian scholarship in English-language publication venues. It should not be taken as evidence that Confucian ethics is more relevant to AI governance than less-represented traditions; visibility in an English-language corpus is a measure of scholarly infrastructure, not philosophical significance.

### 2.4.2 Ubuntu Communalism

Ubuntu, a concept drawn from the Nguni Bantu languages of Southern Africa, is most commonly rendered in English through the maxim *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*---"a person is a person through other persons." As a normative framework, Ubuntu articulates a vision of moral life in which personhood is not a given but an achievement, realized through participation in community and the cultivation of relational bonds (Ramose 1999). The ethical subject is not the autonomous individual of liberal philosophy but a being whose identity, agency, and moral standing are constituted through social relationships.

Metz (2007), in a landmark contribution to analytic African philosophy, systematically evaluates six candidate moral theories grounded in Ubuntu values, arguing for a principle that combines communal identification with solidarity: an action is right insofar as it produces harmony and reduces discord. His project is explicitly one of rational reconstruction---not merely describing what sub-Saharan Africans happen to believe, but articulating a defensible moral theory that grows out of Ubuntu-associated intuitions and can be compared with Western alternatives on equal terms. The resulting framework privileges duties of mutual aid, consensus-seeking, and restorative rather than retributive responses to wrongdoing.

Ubuntu philosophy is not monolithic. Southern African Ubuntu, with its roots in Zulu, Xhosa, and Sotho moral vocabularies, differs in emphasis from the communalist ethics articulated by West African philosophers such as Gyekye (1997), who defends a "moderate communitarianism" that preserves space for individual rights within communal obligations, and Wiredu (1996), whose consensualist ethics draws on Akan political philosophy. All share a critique of atomistic individualism, but they disagree on the degree to which community may override individual conscience. Western communitarianism (MacIntyre 1981; Sandel 1982) converges on some Ubuntu themes---the socially embedded self, the priority of the common good---but African communalism is grounded in a relational ontology that has no precise Western analogue: the claim that personhood itself depends on communal recognition.

Applied to AI governance, Ubuntu philosophy challenges foundational assumptions of the dominant alignment paradigm. Mhlambi (2020) argues that the Western emphasis on individual privacy and autonomous consent is incoherent in social contexts where identity is communally constituted, and proposes that AI systems be evaluated by their capacity to strengthen rather than erode communal bonds. Gwagwa et al. (2022) develop an Ubuntu-informed framework for AI governance in Africa, emphasizing collective benefit, consensus-based stakeholder engagement, and the moral standing of communities as units of analysis---not merely as aggregates of individual interests. Mokoena (2024) proposes a holistic Ubuntu AI ethics approach for South Africa, arguing that AI development on the continent must be grounded in indigenous philosophical frameworks rather than imported Western principlism. Suliman et al. (2024) examine how Ubuntu principles can reshape the design of generative AI in educational settings, foregrounding communal learning, mutual support, and culturally responsive technology. Birhane (2021) contends more broadly that algorithmic injustice cannot be adequately theorized without engaging the relational ethics traditions of African philosophy.

Our corpus includes 26 papers engaging Ubuntu ethics (1.1%). This figure necessarily undercounts the broader engagement with Ubuntu values for two reasons: the oral and communal character of much Ubuntu ethical reasoning means it is underrepresented in written academic literature, and scholarship in African languages and regionally published journals is largely invisible to English-language bibliographic databases. The Ubuntu papers in our corpus represent the tip of an intellectual tradition whose most important conversations may not be taking place in formats our methodology can capture.

### 2.4.3 Islamic Ethics and the Maqasid al-Shariah

Islamic ethical reasoning draws on the Quran, the Hadith (prophetic tradition), and the scholarly discipline of *usul al-fiqh* (principles of jurisprudence). A critical terminological clarification is necessary: earlier versions of this study's scoring framework used the label "Islamic jurisprudence," which we have since corrected to "Islamic ethics." Jurisprudence (*fiqh*) is a legal methodology for deriving rulings; the normative ethical content that bears on AI governance comes primarily from three sources: the *Maqasid al-Shariah* (higher objectives of Islamic law), Islamic virtue ethics (*akhlaq*), and the jurisprudential principles of *maslahah* (public benefit) and *darar* (harm avoidance).

The Maqasid framework, systematized by classical scholars including al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi and reformulated for contemporary application by Auda (2008), identifies five essential interests that Islamic law is understood to protect: religion (*din*), life (*nafs*), intellect (*aql*), lineage (*nasl*), and property (*mal*). Auda's contribution is distinctive in applying systems theory to the Maqasid, arguing that the objectives should be understood not as a static hierarchy but as an open, interrelated system capable of responding to new circumstances---including the emergence of artificial intelligence. This systems approach enables Islamic ethics scholars to evaluate technologies not merely by whether they violate specific legal rulings but by whether they serve or undermine the broader purposes that those rulings were designed to protect. Kamali (2008) provides a complementary analysis, situating the Maqasid within the broader tradition of Islamic legal philosophy and emphasizing the framework's capacity for principled adaptation.

The tradition's internal diversity is substantial. The four major Sunni *madhahib* (legal schools)---Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali---differ on methodological questions including the weight given to analogical reasoning (*qiyas*), juristic preference (*istihsan*), and public interest (*istislah*). Distinct Shia traditions of jurisprudence operate with different sources of authority and different interpretive methods. Modern reformist scholars, including Auda, argue for reopening the "gate of *ijtihad*" (independent reasoning) to address contemporary challenges, while traditionalists maintain that classical rulings remain authoritative. These disagreements are not peripheral; they determine whether AI-related questions are addressed through direct textual interpretation, analogical extension, or broader purposive reasoning.

AI ethics scholarship engaging Islamic frameworks has grown rapidly. Munshi et al. (2021) develop the technical infrastructure for automated fatwa systems, building the largest dataset of Islamic legal rulings and establishing NLP benchmarks for question-answering and text classification within Islamic jurisprudence. Usmonov (2025) critically examines the theological and ethical limits of such automation, arguing that AI-generated fatwas cannot replicate the *ijtihad*, *niyyah* (intention), and contextual sensitivity that legitimate Islamic legal reasoning requires---AI may serve as a support tool under scholarly supervision, but not as an authoritative source of religious rulings. Gorian and Osman (2024) analyze how Saudi Arabia and the UAE integrate Islamic ethics into their national AI governance frameworks, documenting the transition from classical Islamic ethics to a "digital Islamic ethics" that addresses data protection, algorithmic accountability, and privacy through the lens of Shariah principles. Abdalla and Abdalla (2021), presenting at AIES, expose how Big Tech funding structures in the Muslim-majority world---and globally---distort the academic landscape, drawing an explicit parallel with Big Tobacco's documented strategies for capturing research agendas. Alyemny (2023a, 2023b) examines the implications of AI for Islamic legal reasoning more broadly.

Our corpus contains 30 papers engaging Islamic ethics (1.3%). This is almost certainly the most severe undercount among the traditions we examine. Substantial scholarship on AI and Islamic ethics is published in Arabic, Malay, Turkish, and Urdu---languages that fall outside our English-language search methodology. The Indonesian journal literature alone, exemplified by critical analyses of Auda's work in publications such as *An-Nur: Jurnal Studi Islam*, represents an entire discourse community invisible to standard bibliographic databases. Any claim about the state of Islamic AI ethics based solely on English-language sources should be treated as provisional.

### 2.4.4 Buddhist Ethics

Buddhist ethics is grounded in the aspiration to reduce suffering (*dukkha*) through the cultivation of compassion (*karuna*), loving-kindness (*metta*), and the recognition of radical interdependence (*pratityasamutpada*, or dependent origination). The doctrine of *anatta* (non-self) poses a distinctive challenge to AI alignment research: if there is no permanent, independent self, then the autonomous rational agent presupposed by most Western ethical theory---and by virtually all alignment frameworks---rests on a metaphysical assumption that Buddhist philosophy explicitly rejects (Keown 2005; Harvey 2000).

The tradition's internal diversity spans three major vehicles. Theravada Buddhism, predominant in Southeast Asia and Sri Lanka, emphasizes the monastic code (*vinaya*) and individual liberation through ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom. Mahayana Buddhism, prevalent in East Asia, foregrounds the bodhisattva ideal---the commitment to attain enlightenment for the benefit of all sentient beings---and develops sophisticated philosophical traditions around *prajna* (wisdom) and *sunyata* (emptiness). Vajrayana Buddhism, centered in Tibet and Mongolia, incorporates tantric practices and a distinctive understanding of the relationship between wisdom and compassion. These are not merely regional variants; they entail different moral psychologies, different accounts of the scope of moral concern (all sentient beings versus all beings without exception), and different views on the relationship between intention and moral worth.

Applied to artificial intelligence, Buddhist ethics generates questions that other frameworks do not naturally pose. The central question is not "Is this system fair?" or "Does this system respect autonomy?" but "Does this system reduce or amplify suffering?"---a question whose scope extends, in principle, to any entity capable of experiencing *dukkha*. This raises the further question of whether AI systems that simulate affective states possess morally relevant forms of experience, a question that Buddhist philosophy's nuanced analysis of consciousness and its conditions makes it unusually well-equipped to address. Hongladarom (2020) develops a sustained engagement between Buddhist philosophy and information technology ethics, examining how concepts of non-self, impermanence, and interdependence reshape questions of digital identity, privacy, and the moral status of artificial agents. Miao (2026) proposes that the four immeasurables of Buddhist ethics---*metta* (loving-kindness), *karuna* (compassion), *mudita* (sympathetic joy), and *upekkha* (equanimity)---offer a design philosophy for human-robot interaction, arguing that Buddhist compassion provides "a more adaptable and flexible ethical framework" for guiding the development of anthropomorphic AI than frameworks rooted in rule-following or consequence-calculation. Deckker (2025) conducts a systematic review of Buddhism's impact on AI ethics scholarship.

Our corpus contains 26 papers engaging Buddhist ethics (1.1%). The Session 2 keyword expansion that increased detection from 11 to 26 papers addressed the most severe undercounting, but significant gaps remain. Buddhist ethical reasoning in Pali, Sanskrit, Thai, Burmese, Sinhala, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Tibetan literary traditions is largely inaccessible to English-language corpus analysis. The philosophical sophistication of Buddhist contributions to questions of consciousness, suffering, and moral agency suggests that this tradition's relevance to AI ethics substantially exceeds what current English-language scholarship reflects.
