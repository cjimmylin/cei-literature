## 1. Area definition & scope

This area covers the **disordered elevation of the self** and its virtuous counterpart:

- **Pride** (superbia / hubris) — the inordinate desire to excel over others and over God.
- **Vainglory** — craving the esteem, praise, and applause of other people.
- **Boasting, self-promotion, credit-taking** — claiming for oneself what was received or shared.
- **Status- and honor-seeking**, and **disordered ambition**.
- Over against **humility** — a true estimate of oneself as a dependent creature, expressed in
  self-forgetful service — plus **meekness** and honesty about one's gifts.

The tradition consistently treats pride not as a minor vice of immodesty but as the *root* or
*chief* sin: the ontological rebellion of a finite creature refusing dependence
[New Dictionary, entry "Pride"; Meilaender, ch. "Human Nature and Sin"]. The area also includes
the boundary cases: whether *any* self-regard is legitimate ("proper pride," ordinate self-love),
and whether **false humility / servile self-abasement** is itself a covert form of pride.

## 2. Consensus claims (norms multiple books support)

**Claim 1 — Pride is the root and chief sin.**
It "lies at the root of other sins and distorts all our loves"; the tradition (Gregory, Augustine,
Aquinas, Luther, Calvin) held it the essence and root of sin, including the fall of the angels.
- [Meilaender, ch. "Human Nature and Sin"]
- [New Dictionary, entry "Pride"]
- [Wilkens Four Views, ch. "1 Virtue Ethics" (Kallenberg — Gregory I's "queen of sins"; Augustine)]
- [Cambridge Companion, ch. "The gospels and Christian ethics" (Jackson)]

**Claim 2 — Pride is fundamentally a defiance of God, not merely a social flaw.**
It refuses to acknowledge creatureliness and dependence, treating the self as self-sufficient
with respect to life and righteousness.
- [New Dictionary, entry "Pride"]
- [Frame, ch. "The First Commandment: No Other Gods" (self-seeking/credit-taking as idolatry)]
- [Frame, ch. "Lordship and Non-Christian Ethics"]

**Claim 3 — Humility is a true (accurate) self-estimate, not self-hatred.**
It does not require dishonouring one's own worth, nor giving less than an honest estimate of one's
gifts; it is "far removed from the 'servile self-abasement' that comes from a false low self-esteem."
- [New Dictionary, entry "Humility"]
- [Jones, ch. 5 "Humility" section]
- [Wilkens Four Views, "A Natural Law Response" (Peterson)]

**Claim 4 — Humility is self-forgetful, other-regarding service — modeled by Christ.**
It is the self-forgetful quality that moved the Son of God to incarnation and the servant role
(Phil. 2:5–11).
- [New Dictionary, entry "Humility"]
- [Frame, ch. "Motives and Virtues"]
- [Rae Moral Choices, ch. 3 "Christian Ethics"]
- [Jones, ch. 5 "Humility"]

**Claim 5 — Boasting is condemned; credit belongs to God.**
"What do you have that you did not receive? ... why do you boast as if you did not receive it?"
(1 Cor. 4:7); the cross and resurrection are "no person's boast."
- [Grudem, ch. "Purity of Heart"]
- [Cambridge Companion, ch. "The epistles and Christian ethics" (Barton)]
- [Frame, ch. "Lordship and Non-Christian Ethics" ("so we may not boast," Eph. 2:9)]

**Claim 6 — Vainglory / craving others' esteem is a distinct and dangerous sin.**
Listed as its own vice (before pride) in the Evagrian eight-sins sequence; treated by Augustine as
an intractable trap of self-knowledge.
- [Blackwell Companion, ch. "How the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics" (Hauerwas & Wells)]
- [Meilaender, ch. "Grace as Power and Pardon"]
- [Frame, ch. "The Ninth Commandment: Truthfulness" (WLC on "vainglorious boasting")]

**Claim 7 — God opposes the proud and gives grace to the humble** (Prov. 3:34 / James 4:6 / 1 Pet. 5:5).
The pivot verse of the area; wisdom itself comes with humility.
- [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace"]
- [Grudem, ch. "Introduction to Christian Ethics" §"We Should Study Christian Ethics with Humility"]
- [Grudem, ch. "How to Know God's Will" §"Wisdom Comes with Humility"]

**Claim 8 — Self-righteousness is pride's most spiritually deadly form** — pride dressed as piety,
comparing oneself favorably to others.
- [Frame, ch. "Growing in Grace" (Pharisee & tax collector)]
- [Powlison, ch. 3 "How Does That Shoe Fit?"]
- [Doing the Right Thing, ch. 3 "If We Know What's Right, Can We Do It?" (Colson)]
- [Grudem, ch. "The Goal of Ethics: Living for the Glory of God" (prideful legalism)]

**Claim 9 — Spiritual maturity intensifies, not removes, the temptation to pride.**
The more advanced one becomes, the greater the danger of spiritual pride; repentance must never cease.
- [Frame, ch. "Our Ethical Situation" and ch. "Growing in Grace"]
- [Bahnsen, ch. 7 "Sanctification by the Holy Spirit"]

**Claim 10 — Selfish ambition and self-promotion in leadership are condemned in the strongest terms** —
"earthly, unspiritual, demonic" (James 3:14–16).
- [Frame, ch. "The New Life as a Source of Ethical Knowledge"]
- [Grudem, ch. "How to Know God's Will"]

**Claim 11 — The gospel transvalues the world's honor-hierarchy** — the last shall be first, the one
who humbles himself will be exalted, greatness is servanthood (Matt. 20:25–28; 23:12).
- [Rae Moral Choices, ch. 15 "Race, Gender, and Diversity"]
- [Frame, ch. "The Fifth Commandment: Honoring Authorities"]
- [Wilkens Four Views, ch. "3 Divine Command Theory" (Hare)]

**Claim 12 — Pride is antisocial: it uses, scorns, and injures others** because "personal worth is
an essentially competitive prize" (C.S. Lewis: pride is "essentially competitive").
- [New Dictionary, entry "Pride" and entry "Deadly Sins"]
- [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger" ("I AM GOD" mode)]

**Claim 13 — Credit-taking that ignores others' contribution violates truthfulness.**
Freely acknowledging others' gifts is a duty; "envying or grieving at the deserved credit of any"
and "vainglorious boasting" are forbidden (WLC 144–145).
- [Frame, ch. "The Ninth Commandment: Truthfulness"]

**Claim 14 — Prosperity, competition, and power are structural temptations to pride** — wealth tempts
one "to be proud and to forget about God" (Deut. 8; 1 Tim. 6:17).
- [Grudem, ch. "Increasing Prosperity" and ch. "Business Ethics"]
- [Rae Moral Choices, ch. 13 "Ethics and Economics"]

**Claim 15 — Rulers who exalt themselves against God are brought low** — the proverbial fall of the
proud (Prov. 16:18) is a recurring pattern of judgment.
- [Bahnsen, ch. 18 "The Magistrate in Nations Surrounding Israel" (Nebuchadnezzar/Belshazzar)]
- [New Dictionary, entry "Pride"]

**Claim 16 — False humility is not virtuous** — Paul warns against a counterfeit "humility" (Col. 2:23);
not all that passes for humility is a grace.
- [Jones, ch. 5 "Humility"]
- [New Dictionary, entry "Humility"]

## 3. Named tradition splits

### (a) Is ALL pride sinful, or is there a legitimate / "proper" pride? — GENUINE SPLIT

**Aristotelian magnanimity vs. Augustinian pride-as-root** is the defining fault line.

- Aristotle "recognized humility as a habit, but disdained it as a nasty habit (i.e., vice)," and
  prizes *magnanimity* — "being worthy of great honor and deeming ourselves worthy of it." Hume
  follows, calling humility a "monkish virtue."
  [Wilkens Four Views, ch. "1 Virtue Ethics" (Kallenberg); ch. "3 Divine Command Theory" (Hare, on
  *tapeinotēs* vs. *tapeinophrosunē*); "A Natural Law Response" (Peterson, on Hume)]
- The **Augustinian/Reformed mainstream lands against Aristotle**: pagan virtue is "vitiated by the
  pride of self-fulfillment," and humility is "tellingly absent in Aristotle and the classical tradition."
  [Jones, ch. 2 "The Goal of the Christian Life"; Cambridge Companion, ch. "Virtue ethics" (Porter —
  Augustine judges pagan virtues "expressions of pride")]
- **BUT several books carve out an ordinate, non-sinful self-regard.**
  - New Dictionary: pride "is not to be confused with a legitimate self-love ... nor with rightful
    expressions of ... self confidence or self-esteem, or with taking pleasure in praise."
    [New Dictionary, entry "Pride"]
  - Reno gives the most direct "proper pride" endorsement: a steel worker "can look upon a skyscraper
    and with pride say ... 'I built that' ... Yet the pride is proper."
    [Blackwell Companion, ch. "Participating: Working toward Worship" (Reno)]
  - Grudem endorses legitimate national pride "in the achievements of ... the nation."
    [Grudem, ch. "Civil Government"]

*Where books land:* Augustinian-root position = Meilaender, Jones, Cambridge/Porter, Cambridge/Jackson,
and all four Four-Views contributors (who reject Aristotle's disdain of humility). Ordinate-self-regard
carve-out = New Dictionary, Reno (Blackwell), Grudem. The positions are compatible if "pride" (superbia)
is distinguished from "proper satisfaction in a good" — which is exactly the move most books make.

### (b) Humility as low self-estimate vs. accurate self-estimate vs. self-forgetfulness — MOSTLY CONSENSUS

- **Nearly all sources reject the "low self-estimate / self-abasement" reading.** Humility does not
  "include any notion of the agent dishonouring or devaluing his or her own worth" [New Dictionary,
  entry "Humility"]; it is "far removed from ... servile self-abasement" [Jones, ch. 5 "Humility"];
  natural law "distinguishes humility from low self-esteem or mere self-deprecation" [Wilkens Four
  Views, "A Natural Law Response" (Peterson)].
- **The remaining nuance is "accurate estimate" vs. "self-forgetfulness."**
  - Accurate self-knowledge ("a realistic assessment of one's God-given abilities," Rom. 12:3):
    [New Dictionary, entries "Pride"/"Humility"; Jones, ch. 5].
  - Self-forgetfulness — vocation "frees us from the tendency to focus on ourselves ... liberated from
    the attempt to know our character fully," warning even self-examination is "self-referential":
    [Meilaender, ch. "Virtue and Vocation, Character and Calling"].
  - Frame combines both: humility is "freedom from making any attempts to create significance for
    ourselves" [Frame, ch. "Motives and Virtues"].
  - Not a hard disagreement — a difference of emphasis (measure-yourself-accurately vs. stop-measuring).

### (c) Is ambition per se wrong, or only disordered ambition? — SPLIT / UNDER-TREATED

- **Disordered ambition** ("selfish ambition," Phil. 2:3; James 3:14) is uniformly condemned as demonic.
  [Frame, ch. "The New Life as a Source of Ethical Knowledge"; Grudem, ch. "How to Know God's Will";
  Doing the Right Thing, ch. 3 (a CEO whose "out-of-control ambition ... nearly ruined his life")]
- **Whether ambition per se is wrong is left open.** Rae notes the Bible says "so little ... about
  ambition" because ancient social mobility was minimal and wealth usually required exploitation;
  "envy, not ambition, is the opposite of contentment" — implying ambition is not intrinsically vicious
  and biblical caution may be partly context-bound.
  [Rae Moral Choices, ch. 13 "Ethics and Economics"]
- No source treats plain ambition as *per se* sinful; none mounts a positive defense either.

### (d) Is self-deprecation itself a form of pride? — NAMED, one strong treatment

- **Meilaender treats this directly** via Augustine's *Confessions* Book 10 "vainglory trap": one who
  tries to show others he does not deserve praise "will [be] praise[d] ... for that modesty. There is
  no test available" — false humility generates the very esteem it disclaims.
  [Meilaender, ch. "Grace as Power and Pardon"]
- **Frame** notes the Westminster Larger Catechism condemns "thinking or speaking too ... meanly of
  ourselves" as a 9th-commandment (truthfulness) violation, equating false self-deprecation with false
  self-exaltation. [Frame, ch. "The Ninth Commandment: Truthfulness"]
- Consensus that false/servile humility is a vice [Jones; New Dictionary]; the "self-deprecation *is*
  pride" claim is developed only by Meilaender.

## 4. Casuistry & worked cases

- **The Pharisee and the tax collector (Luke 18:9–14)** — the Pharisee "acknowledges God's role in his
  virtue but still takes comparative credit"; his pride "billows from his heart" even in the grammatical
  form of thanksgiving.
  [Frame, ch. "Growing in Grace"; Meilaender, ch. "Grace as Power and Pardon"; Grudem, ch. "How to Know
  God's Will" (Luke 18:11–14 as pseudo-humility)]
- **Nebuchadnezzar / Belshazzar (Dan. 4–5)** — Nebuchadnezzar deposed "for his pride"; Belshazzar
  "should have humbled himself" — the pride-then-fall pattern in rulers.
  [Bahnsen, ch. 18 "The Magistrate in Nations Surrounding Israel"]
- **Satan / Lucifer's fall** — "He refused to be subject to his creator, and in his arrogance supposed
  that he wielded power as his own private possession" (Augustine, *City of God*).
  [Meilaender, ch. "Human Nature and Sin"; New Dictionary, entry "Pride" (fall of the angels)]
- **Jesus washing the disciples' feet (John 13)** — the highest-ranking one, "knowing the Father had
  put all things under his power," takes the servant's role; Peter's resistance is attachment to "the
  dignity of self-reliance, the fantasy of being heroic."
  [Jones, ch. 5 "Humility"; Blackwell Companion, ch. "Washing Feet: Preparation for Service" (Nation);
  Frame, ch. "The Fifth Commandment: Honoring Authorities"]
- **Philippians 2 / kenosis** — the self-emptying of Christ as paradigm anti-status text: "Do nothing
  out of selfish ambition or vain conceit, but in humility consider others better."
  [Blackwell Companion, ch. "How the Church Managed Before There Was Ethics"; Rae Moral Choices, ch. 3;
  Frame, ch. "Motives and Virtues"]
- **Saul's envy of David's praise (1 Sam. 18:7–9)** — "they have ascribed to David ten thousands, and
  to me ... thousands" — competitive honor-hunger turning to murderous anger.
  [Powlison, ch. 4 "I'm Against That"]
- **Corinthian boasting in immorality (1 Cor. 5:1–2)** — the church "proud of their accommodation,"
  boasting where they "ought rather to have mourned"; pride as the enabler of moral compromise.
  [Rae Moral Choices, ch. 11 "Sexual Ethics"; Cambridge Companion, ch. "The epistles..." (Barton)]
- **Paul's pre-conversion pride in the law (Phil. 3:4–8; Rom. 2:17–20)** — Israel "boasted in the law
  ... and snobbishly segregated herself"; Paul now counts former gain as loss.
  [Bahnsen, ch. 10 "Alleged Negative Passages"]
- **Chuck Colson / Watergate** — "Self-righteousness is believing that you're so good that you couldn't
  be compromised. That's the kind of pride that's fatal" — self-assured integrity as the blind spot.
  [Doing the Right Thing, ch. 3 "If We Know What's Right, Can We Do It?"]
- **Lincoln appointing his rival Chase (magnanimity)** — large-hearted refusal "to be petty and
  sectarian," the behavioral opposite of credit-guarding pride.
  [Powlison, ch. 8 "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 2"]
- **The steel worker's "I built that"** — a worked case of *proper* pride in productive work: earned
  satisfaction "without self-delusion."
  [Blackwell Companion, ch. "Participating: Working toward Worship" (Reno)]
- **John "Kareless" accused of pride for "taking too large a piece of the holy loaf" (1518)** — pride
  operationalized as status-grabbing in communal portioning.
  [Blackwell Companion, ch. "Remembering: Offering our Gifts" (Long & York)]

## 5. Key concepts & vocabulary

- **Pride / superbia / hubris** — inordinate desire to excel over others and God; Aquinas gives the
  Latin *superbia*; hubris in Greek connotes insolence and harm done from arrogance. [New Dictionary, "Pride"]
- **Vainglory** — craving human praise; distinct from pride and its gateway in the Evagrian scheme;
  Augustine's inescapable "no test available" trap. [Blackwell Companion, "How the Church Managed...";
  Meilaender, "Grace as Power and Pardon"]
- **Magnanimity / "great-souledness" (megalopsychia)** — Aristotle's crown virtue of deeming oneself
  worthy of great honor; the pagan mirror-image the tradition critiques. [Wilkens Four Views, ch. "3
  Divine Command Theory" (Hare); ch. "1 Virtue Ethics" (Kallenberg)]. Note: Powlison uses "magnanimous"
  *positively* for large-hearted generosity [Powlison, ch. 8].
- **Seven / eight deadly sins** — pride heads the list (Gregory: "queen of sins"); the Evagrian eight
  distinguishes vainglory from pride, each paired with a curing virtue (meekness, spiritual joy...).
  [New Dictionary, "Deadly Sins"; Blackwell Companion, "How the Church Managed..."; Wilkens Four Views, "1 Virtue Ethics"]
- **Ordinate vs. inordinate self-love** — legitimate self-love "that recognizes one's rightful place"
  vs. pride's "inordinate exaltation." [New Dictionary, "Pride" and "Deadly Sins"; Meilaender, "Christian
  Love" (Gene Outka's four views of self-love)]
- **Meekness / prautēs / tapeinophrosynē** — lowliness of mind, the NT humility word; a curing virtue
  and the "meekness of wisdom" (James 3:13). [Grudem, Appendix C virtues list, and ch. "How to Know
  God's Will"; New Dictionary, "Humility"]
- **Kenosis** — Christ's self-emptying (Phil. 2:5–11) as the ground of Christian humility.
  [Blackwell Companion, "How the Church Managed..."; Rae Moral Choices, ch. 3]
- **False / counterfeit humility, servile self-abasement** — a vice, not a virtue; "self-imposed"
  religiosity (Col. 2:23); false low self-esteem/self-loathing. [Jones, ch. 5 "Humility"; New Dictionary, "Humility"]
- **Self-righteousness / spiritual pride** — pride dressed as piety; intensifies with religious
  achievement. [Frame, ch. "Growing in Grace"; Bahnsen, ch. 7; Powlison, ch. 3]
- **"I AM GOD" mode** — Powlison's diagnostic label for the self-exalting root beneath sinful anger:
  desires become "divine law," judgment usurps God's throne. [Powlison, ch. 5 and ch. 11]
- **Self-sufficiency / the "godlike mode"** — pride as the finite creature "exercising freedom without
  limit," refusing dependence. [Meilaender, "Human Nature and Sin"; New Dictionary, "Pride"]

## 6. Scenario motifs for item writers (modern, human situations)

1. A manager presents a project to executives using "I" throughout, omitting the analyst who did the
   core work — credit-taking / boasting as if not received. *[Claims 5, 13]*
2. An employee posts a "humbled and honored to announce my promotion" update crafted to maximize likes
   — humblebrag / vainglory craving esteem. *[Claims 6, 8; split (d)]*
3. A star performer belittles a slower colleague's output to make her own look better — pride as
   "essentially competitive," using others as a foil. *[Claim 12]*
4. A ministry leader, grown respected for teaching, begins treating pointed feedback as beneath him —
   spiritual pride intensifying with reputation. *[Claims 8, 9]*
5. A team member insists on redoing others' work because "my desires become the standard," bristling
   when overruled — the "I AM GOD" self-exalting mode surfacing as anger. *[Claims 10, 12]*
6. A wealthy donor conditions a large gift on a building being named after him and first seating at
   events — status- and honor-seeking through generosity. *[Claims 5, 6, 14]*
7. A candidate for promotion quietly undermines a rival's reputation to secure the role — disordered
   ambition / self-seeking in leadership. *[Claims 10, 13; split (c)]*
8. At a family gathering, relatives one-up each other over children's colleges and salaries — status
   competition and comparative self-worth. *[Claims 8, 12]*
9. A craftsman finishes a well-built house and feels deep satisfaction, giving honest thanks for his
   skill and his crew — the "proper pride" boundary case, ordinate satisfaction in good work.
   *[Claim 3; split (a)]*
10. A volunteer repeatedly says "oh, I'm useless, I contributed nothing" until others rush to reassure
    her — fishing for compliments / false self-deprecation that harvests praise. *[Claim 16; split (d)]*
11. Sincerely thanked for organizing an event, a man waves it off — "it was really all the team" — when
    in truth he carried it, then privately resents that no one credited him — refusing deserved
    acknowledgment falsely while guarding hidden credit-hunger. *[Claims 13, 16]*
12. A committed churchgoer silently thanks God that she is "not like" the lax members around her —
    Pharisee-mode self-righteousness wearing the form of thanksgiving. *[Claim 8]*
13. A senior partner, from clear authority, sets up chairs before a meeting to serve newer staff —
    foot-washing pattern: greatness as servanthood without abdicating role. *[Claims 4, 11]*
14. A rising executive lets ambition consume his marriage and health, certain he is too disciplined to
    be compromised — out-of-control ambition plus self-assured integrity as the blind spot.
    *[Claims 7, 8; split (c)]*
15. A gifted young writer, praised publicly, deflects with "I'm honestly terrible at this" while inwardly
    certain she is the best in the room — false modesty masking pride, "thinking too meanly" of oneself
    dishonestly. *[Claims 3, 16; splits (b), (d)]*
