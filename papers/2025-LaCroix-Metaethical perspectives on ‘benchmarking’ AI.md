## Abstract

Benchmarks are seen as the cornerstone for measuring technical progress in artificial intelligence (AI) research and have been developed for a variety of tasks ranging from question answering to emotion recognition. An increasingly prominent research area in AI is ethics, which currently has no set of benchmarks nor commonly accepted way for measuring the ‘ethicality’ of an AI system. In this paper, drawing upon research in moral philosophy and metaethics, we argue that it is impossible to develop such a benchmark. As such, alternative mechanisms are necessary for evaluating whether an AI system is ‘ethical’. This is especially pressing in light of the prevalence of applied, industrial AI research. We argue that it makes more sense to talk about ‘values’ (and ‘value alignment’) rather than ‘ethics’ when considering the possible actions of present and future AI systems. We further highlight that, because values are unambiguously relative, focusing on values forces us to consider explicitly what the values are and whose values they are. Shifting the emphasis from ethics to values therefore gives rise to several new ways of understanding how researchers might advance research programmes for robustly safe or beneficial AI.

## Detailed Summary

This paper argues that it is impossible to develop benchmarks for measuring the ethicality of AI systems. Drawing on research in moral philosophy and metaethics, the authors contend that alternative mechanisms are necessary for evaluating whether AI systems are ethical. The paper proposes discussing values and value alignment rather than ethics when considering AI systems, because values are unambiguously relative, forcing explicit consideration of what and whose values they are.

The authors identify three core problems with benchmarking ethics: First, moral dilemmas have no right answer by design - they are thought experiments meant to elicit intuitions, not provide ground truth. Second, benchmarking ethics presupposes moral realism, a contested metaethical position. Third, even if moral realism were true, the long-tail problem makes benchmarking impossible.

The paper critiques the Moral Machine Experiment as an example of problematic ethics benchmarking, noting its data are descriptive not normative, it commits the is-ought fallacy, and it measures accordance with opinion, not moral correctness.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs conceptual analysis from analytic philosophy, surveying benchmarking issues, analyzing the Moral Machine Experiment as a case study, introducing metaethical considerations, and proposing a structural definition of value alignment based on principal-agent dynamics.

## Results Summary

Key arguments: moral dilemmas are designed without right answers; benchmarking ethics requires contested moral realism assumptions; even if realism is true, the long-tail problem prevents comprehensive testing; 87% of ML papers prioritize performance and 79% have corporate ties, showing whose values dominate.

## Conclusions

Benchmarking ethics for AI is impossible due to metaethical and practical considerations. The authors recommend shifting discourse from ethics to values and value alignment to force explicit consideration of whose values are being encoded.
