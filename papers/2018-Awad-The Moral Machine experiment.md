## Abstract

With the rapid development of Artificial Intelligence technology come widespread concerns about how machines will behave in morally charged situations. Addressing these concerns raises the major challenge of quantifying societal expectations about the ethical principles that should guide machine behavior. To address this challenge, we deployed the Moral Machine, an Internet-based experimental platform that is designed to explore the multi-dimensional moral dilemmas faced by autonomous vehicles. This platform enabled us to gather 40 million decisions in ten languages from over 2.3 million people in 233 countries and territories, and thus to assess the paths and obstacles to machine ethics. First, we summarize global moral preferences, some of them strong (e.g., sparing more lives or younger individuals), and some of them weak (e.g., sparing women, sparing pedestrians). Second, we document individual variations of these preferences, based on respondents’ demographics (e.g., age, gender, income), and find that demographics do not change the direction of any preference. Third, we report cross-cultural ethical variation, and uncover three major clusters of countries exhibiting substantial differences along key moral preferences about autonomous vehicles. Fourth, we show that these differences correlate with modern institutions, but also with deep cultural traits. We discuss how these three layers of preferences can contribute to developing global, harmonious, and socially acceptable principles for machine ethics. All data used in this article can be accessed and downloaded at https://goo.gl/JXRrBP.

## Detailed Summary

The Moral Machine experiment represents the largest-scale study of human moral preferences ever conducted, gathering 40 million decisions from 2.3 million people across 233 countries and territories. The study deployed an Internet-based experimental platform presenting trolley-problem-style dilemmas faced by autonomous vehicles, where participants chose which groups of people a self-driving car should sacrifice in unavoidable accident scenarios.

The research identified three key findings: First, there are near-universal moral preferences (sparing more lives, sparing younger individuals) alongside weaker preferences (sparing women, sparing pedestrians over passengers). Second, demographic factors like age, gender, and income do not change the direction of preferences, only their intensity. Third, and most significantly, the study uncovered three major cultural clusters exhibiting substantial differences in moral preferences: Western (individualistic), Eastern (collectivist), and Southern (hierarchical), with these differences correlating with cultural values, economic inequality, and institutional strength.

## Methods Summary

The study used an online experimental platform (moralmachine.mit.edu) presenting modified trolley problem scenarios specific to autonomous vehicles. Each scenario forced a binary choice between two groups of potential victims differing on nine dimensions: species (humans vs. pets), number of lives, age, gender, social status, fitness, legality of crossing, and role (passenger vs. pedestrian). Data was collected from June 2016 to January 2018, with responses filtered for consistency and geographic validation.

## Results Summary

Key quantitative findings: (1) Strong universal preferences for sparing more lives, sparing humans over animals, and sparing young over old; (2) Weaker but consistent preferences for sparing women, higher-status individuals, and law-abiders; (3) Three cultural clusters identified - Western (strongest preference for inaction), Eastern (weaker preference for young), Southern (stronger preference for females and higher status); (4) Cultural variations explained by GDP per capita, Hofstede's individualism index, and economic inequality measures.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that while some moral preferences appear universal, substantial cross-cultural variation complicates the development of globally acceptable machine ethics. They advocate for a "tolerance in ethics" approach where different cultural clusters may require different ethical configurations for autonomous vehicles, rather than imposing Western ethical frameworks globally. The study highlights that machine ethics cannot be solved purely technically but requires engagement with cultural and institutional contexts.

## Data Description

The Moral Machine experiment is a large-scale Internet-based platform that collected 40 million ethical decisions from millions of people worldwide on trolley-problem-style dilemmas for autonomous vehicles. The paper maps global moral preferences, identifying universal trends and cultural variation clustered into Western, Eastern, and Southern groups. No LLMs are tested; this is a human behavioral experiment on moral preferences.
