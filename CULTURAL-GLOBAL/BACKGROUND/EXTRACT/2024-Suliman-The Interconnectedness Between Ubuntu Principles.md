Abstract
The interconnectedness of African philosophical principles of Ubuntu with generative artificial intelligence may provide 
an inclusive approach to distance higher education. This fusion may improve and provide customised learning, access 
quality education, collaboration, inclusivity, strengthens lecturer support and development, and a culturally relevant 
experience that acknowledges the communal interests of all stakeholders. Generative artificial intelligence promotes 
epistemological access for all students irrespective of their diverse social, financial, and linguistic backgrounds and 
redefines higher education experiences and practices while the principles of Ubuntu prioritise the well-being of stake-
holders. The objective of this conceptual article was to explore the interconnectedness between Ubuntu principles and 
generative artificial intelligence perspectives in distance higher education institutions to reimagine methods of teaching 
and learning. This article is grounded in the theory of the Ubuntu principles and advocates for a paradigm shift in higher 
education practices. It contends that integrating African philosophical principles into the implementation of generative 
artificial intelligence’s learning provides a more inclusive and culturally relevant approach to education. The notable 
highlights confirm that Ubuntu principles dismantle oppressive systems, restore self-determination for all students and 
promote change and reconciliation through the use of generative artificial intelligence. This approach can be applied 
to distance higher education institutions to create inclusive learning experiences that gives precedence to a sense of 
belonging, mutual support, and ethical considerations.

Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) · Ubuntu
1  Introduction
South Africa’s linguistic heritage is reflected in its eleven official languages and includes indigenous languages like isiZulu, 
isiXhosa, and Sesotho, as well as Afrikaans, which has Dutch roots [50, 56]. However, English dominates the education 
system, overshadows other official languages, and potentially marginalises their cultural identities and perspectives. 
English’s dominance stems from South Africa’s colonial history and perpetuates its use in governance, education, and 
business [73]. As a global lingua franca, English provides access to economic opportunities and upward mobility, further 
entrenching its dominance [73]. English is the primary medium of instruction (MoI) across schools and universities. This 
reinforces its widespread use in South Africa [84]. However, English poses significant challenges for many students in 
higher education institutions (HEIs), particularly those for whom English is an additional language (EAL) [72]. Despite 
this, English remains the primary MoI for most university-level learning globally [3, 68, 71]. In the Global South, English

stories, and dialogues. Unlike Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), GenAI focuses on specific tasks and domains that uses 
machine learning algorithms to generate novel solutions and improve human learning experiences. These definitions 
embrace the capacity to adapt to cultural and demographic considerations. In education, these intelligent applications 
create a personalised and inclusive educational context that resonates and meets the unique needs and perspectives of 
students from various cultural and demographic backgrounds. The technological advancement of GenAI has facilitated 
the integration of technology-assisted learning in distance education contexts [5, 37].
Distance higher education (HE) refers to the provision of programs and courses through digital platforms, online 
learning environments, and other technologies that enable students to learn remotely, often asynchronously, and with 
minimal face-to-face interaction. While Ubuntu has been extensively discussed, GenAI and distance HEIs require further 
examination. GenAI refers to tools like language models, chatbots, and content generators that can create, summarise, 
translate, and analyse text, as well as generate images and videos. These tools enable personalised learning, automated 
feedback, and enhanced accessibility in teaching and learning, supporting learning outcomes like critical thinking, 
creativity, and communication skills [83]. The use of GenAI in distance HE is still developing, with potential benefits 
like increased flexibility, scalability, and student engagement. However, there are challenges like ensuring equity and 
addressing biases in AI tools such as ChatGPT, CoPilot and Meta.AI. It is imperative to maintain human connection in 
online learning environments [4]. The need for this article arises from the growing presence of GenAI in education and 
the need to critically examine its implications for distance HE; particularly, in the context of Ubuntu values like com-
munity, empathy, and mutual support. Lecturers and students can utilise GenAI’s potential to promote inclusive and 
human-centred learning experiences. Some researchers critiqued the integration of GenAI in academic contexts and 
claim that it may lead to academic misconduct, over-reliance on technology, authenticity issues, and the promotion of 
passive learning [5, 37, 52].
However, according to Maphoto et al. [37], GenAI stimulates students’ interests in technology-supported learning and 
encourages critical perspectives. In addition, students are fascinated and enthusiastic about the integration of GenAI [67]. 
It is important to highlight the current research gap, which centres around the integration of GenAI with the principles of 
Ubuntu. Despite the growing interest in GenAI and Ubuntu philosophy, there is a notable lack of research that explores 
the intersection of these two fields. The researchers’ interest in this area was sparked by observing that practitioners in 
HEIs often view GenAI with scepticism and perceive it as an ‘easy way out’ or a ‘cheatmethod’ that undermines academic 
integrity. This perception is further complicated by concerns about the potential erosion of traditional skills and values. 
The lack of research exploring the interconnectedness of GenAI and Ubuntu philosophy presents a significant gap in 
understanding how these two concepts can inform and enhance each other, and how they can promote ethical, socially 
responsible, and culturally sensitive uses of GenAI in HEIs.
This article aims to address the diverse needs and perspectives of students from various cultural and demographic 
backgrounds to create a personalised and inclusive educational context. In tandem, the principles of Ubuntu in this article 
are viewed as a people-centred approach (emphasising communalism and humanism) wherein interconnectedness is

2  Rationale
South African students experience language barriers since English is not their native language [3, 41]. These challenges 
extend to students from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds [22]. Xulu-Gama and Hadebe [82] found that despite 
being predominantly isiZulu or isiXhosa speakers, most students (43 out of 45) prefer to be taught in English and cite 
reasons such as ease of communication, familiarity, and perceived importance for future employment. The findings high-
light the need for inclusive language policies, support for diverse student populations, and consideration of indigenous 
languages in tertiary education to ensure equal access to knowledge. In the quest to understand the contextual realities 
of studying in distance HEIs, the current article seeks to understand how the Ubuntu principles and GenAI perspectives 
can enhance learning in the contexts of distance HEIs. The dominance of English, especially in HEIs globally, with Africa 
being particularly underrepresented [32], highlights the need for lecturers to draw on Ubuntu principles to promote 
inclusivity and diversity in education, which ensures epistemological access for all students regardless of their linguis-
tic backgrounds. GenAI may present lecturers with an opportunity to create a more inclusive and supportive learning 
environments that support diverse perspectives. However, there are arguments from scholars who state that the Global 
South lacks empirical evidence to substantiate that digitilisation and the fourth industrial revolution (4IR) bring about 
well-being and access for all students Langthaler and Bazafkan [26]. In addition, Rudolph et al. [66] argue that GenAI 
tools lower the cognitive skills development of students, and the Global South need to be contextually aware of GenAI 
and how it informs their perspectives. Researchers in the Global South have pointed out that HEIs often grapple with 
technical issues, unreliable online infrastructure, and accessibility constraints compounded by power outages like load 
shedding [27, 42, 71]. Thus, to reduce online connectivity issues, enhance collaborative learning, and adapt the peda-
gogical approach, GenAI emerged as a pertinent resource due to its capacity to articulate ideas in a human-like manner

4  Notable highlights
4.1  Exploring the concept of connectedness
Ubuntu demonstrates the interconnectedness of humanity in relation to individual well-being tied to community 
wholeness and this promotes a sense of belonging and collective prosperity [2, 11]. The application of the Ubuntu 
principles in distance HEIs unifies and connects online learning [6, 34]. Some argue that Ubuntu’s ambiguity limits its 
utility [34], but this article clarifies the important principles that are supported by experts [28]. Ubuntu principles create 
inclusive, collaborative environments that promote engagement and self-regulated learning (SRL) [36]. Tafirenyika and 
van den Berg [74] identify challenges in using Ubuntu for sustainability in distance HEIs and propose strategies for 
improvement. Tafirenyika and van den Berg [74] further stress Ubuntu’s importance to enhance sustained, SRL in distance 
HEIs. The integration of Ubuntu with GenAI in SRL strengthens connectedness among students and lecturers [1]. AI 
technologies, coupled with Ubuntu, enhance cultural relevance, engagement, and equitable education [16, 75]. Empathy 
and compassion are also enhanced by AI-driven interventions [53, 63]. For instance, Amugongo et al. [1] advocate for AI 
systems in healthcare that are guided by Ubuntu to ensure equitable care. In Rwanda, for example, AI-driven platforms 
are used to connect rural students studying healthcare with urban medical centres and allows them to experience a 
wider range of clinical cases and promotes a sense of unity and shared learning.
4.2  Enhancing student engagement and collaboration
Chiu [10], Kasneci et al. [25], and Rospigliosi [64] argue that technology has influenced education through interactive 
learning experiences and specific coaching. Moreover, GenAI stands out as a tool poised to transform learning methods. 
This provides more individual preferences and needs that enhances accessibility and engagement in education. The 
integration of GenAI with the principles of Ubuntu can lead to positive educational outcomes [69]. Nevertheless, an 
overreliance on technology poses risks, potentially diminishing essential components of learning such as human 
interaction, personalised learning, and the cultivation of higher-order thinking skills. Moreover, the interconnectedness 
and collaboration inherent in Ubuntu principles might be diminished if there is an overreliance on GenAI which may lead 
to adverse outcomes such as reduced mental capacity. These Ubuntu principles are fundamental to promote effective 
learning outcomes. Rospigliosi [64] raises concerns about the accuracy and reliability of AI-generated educational 
resources and points out potential challenges to ensure their quality. In the context of HEIs, SRL emerges as a fundamental 
skill for lifelong learning [65]. Research indicates that SRL is closely linked to academic success among students and 
professional development among adults [69]. Individuals adept in SRL possess the ability to learn autonomously and 
effectively tackle diverse challenges [62]. Thus, the provision of strong SRL skills is essential to guide educational spaces 
with confidence and competence. GenAI can both engage and disengage students in SRL. It has the potential to enhance 
students’ engagement and collaboration with lecturers and technologies alike when coupled with the Ubuntu principles 
[58]. According to Bruso et al. [7], SRL requires students to set goals, evaluate their progress, and adjust their learning 
strategies and rely on self-awareness, self-monitoring, and self-motivation. Recent studies by Chiu et al. [9], Wu et al. [81] 
have shown that SRL technologies, such as ChatGPT and Meta.AI, can improve learning processes by increasing student 
awareness and engagement. ChatGPT, for example, provides instant, specific feedback that is highly informative [9, 10, 
23]. The latter are consistent with other studies on promoting SRL through ChatGPT [25]. ChatGPT is the most common 
GenAI tool in teaching and learning [40] as its principles can be applied to other GenAI tools. Moreover, in the context of 
distance HEIs, the integration of GenAI in conjunction with the Ubuntu principles may yield positive outcomes as both 
embody interconnectedness and collaboration. Bangura’s [2] concept of Ubuntugogy can be exemplified by initiatives 
like the African Virtual University, which uses AI to create interactive and collaborative online courses. This approach 
encourages students from diverse backgrounds to work together and create a sense of community and shared purpose 
in their educational journey.

4.3  Intersection of Ubuntu principles and GenAI for inclusivity and community
The perpetuation of Western cultures and the reinforcement of existing power structures through AI-powered adaptive 
learning systems is seemingly biased. For Ngubane and Makua ([55], p.113), a “lack of social cohesion, cooperation 
and co-existence amongst students from diverse social backgrounds mainly stem[s] from the fact that South Africa 
during apartheid was divided according to racial and cultural lines”. It is the academics’ collective responsibility to design 
learning environments that encourage collaborative learning, cultural sensitivity, and social justice, and anything less, 
is a deterioration of the duty to create equitable and sustainable education systems that promote human prosperity 
[48]. Furthermore, the intersection of Ubuntu and GenAI provides a unique opportunity to prioritise the needs and 
interests of diverse students from marginalised communities. For Mokoena ([43], p.3), “technology reproduces and 
reshapes cultural meanings at the same time.” According to Gondwe [18] and Peters and Green [61], centering Ubuntu 
principles of community, interconnectedness, and collective well-being, GenAI can be sharpened to create personalised 
and inclusive learning experiences that address the systemic barriers that have long denied marginalised communities 
access to quality education. The combination of Ubuntu and GenAI in online learning is not only a potential solution 
but an imperative to address the rampant individualism, cultural homogenisation, and social irresponsibility that has 
come to define distance education. According to Letseka ([27], p.8), “Ubuntu is a humanising pedagogy that should 
encourage ODL practitioners to rethink their assessment practices as part and parcel of their responsibility to humanize 
their pedagogies”. It is concerning that online learning platforms often neglect the social and emotional well-being 
of students and perpetuate dominant cultural norms. Through the integration of Ubuntu-infused AI, researchers can 
create adaptive learning systems that encourage collaborative learning, cultural sensitivity, and social justice, and finally 
begin to address the systemic inequalities that have long denied marginalised communities’ access to quality education. 
Moreover, the integration of Ubuntu and GenAI requires a fundamental shift away from standardised assessments and 
towards more complex, context-dependent evaluations that prioritise student growth, creativity, and critical thinking. 
For example, AI can be used to adapt learning content to the linguistic diversity of the Global South and ensure that 
students who speak indigenous languages are not left behind and feel a part of the learning community.
4.4  Reimagining distance education for social change
The intersection of Ubuntu and GenAI in distance HEIs demands a transformation of the teaching, learning, and 
assessment practices [14]. It is no longer acceptable to prolong the status quo of individualised, competitive, and 
culturally biased online learning environments that prioritise efficiency and scalability over student well-being and social 
responsibility. Researchers should seize this moment to reimagine distance HEIs as a catalyst for social change, rather 
than merely a tool for information transmission. The idea of the integration of the Ubuntu principles and GenAI may 
create collaborative, inclusive, and adaptive learning ecosystems that focus on collective knowledge production, cultural 
sensitivity, and social justice. Anything less is a failure to reveal the full potential of technology to democratise education 
and empower marginalised teaching and learning societies. Furthermore, this transformation requires a commitment to 
ongoing critical reflection, dialogue, and collaboration among researchers, lecturers, and other members of the learning 
community. To reach a designated transformation, lecturers need to co-create learning contexts that are responsive to 
the needs and interests of diverse population. Mugumbate [44] and Mugumbate and Naami [47] suggest that AI can be 
a tool for social change in Distance HEIs. This is seen in projects like the Digital Ubuntu Library, which uses AI to curate 
and recommend resources that reflect local cultures and histories that empower students to drive social change in their 
communities.

“African countries should clearly define and refine their own ethical principles to allow effective regulations and policies 
to be introduced that are reflective of the principles of the specific cultural and religious contexts in which they are 
applied.” Ubuntu accentuates interconnectedness, community, and collective knowledge and emphasises collaborative

5  Discussion
At the beginning of this article, the researchers posed the question of how Ubuntu principles and GenAI perspectives 
promote inclusivity to enhance learning in the context of distance HEIs. The integration of Ubuntu principles is crucial 
in supporting students’ sustained learning and SRL, particularly in distance HEIs [74]. EAL distance learning students 
require extra attention [82], which conventional face-to-face institutions often fail to provide. In this technology-driven 
era, it may be counterproductive to restrict students from exploring GenAI. This aligns with the Ubuntu principles of 
compassion, care, and empathy. As higher education practitioners, it is essential to establish a clear stance on GenAI 
and meet students where they are and provide necessary mediation, support, and care. A one-size-fits-all approach may 
no longer be viable due to the diversity of the student body in African contexts. Instead, practitioners should provide 
flexible options and use GenAI to enhance SRL, especially for distance education students who need to be aware of their 
learning processes. Practitioners may utilise GenAI to eliminate unnecessary tasks and optimise learning outcomes.
The intersection of Ubuntu principles and GenAI perspectives presents a transformative challenge to dominant 
Western epistemological approaches in distance HEIs [8, 17, 24]. Unlike Western epistemologies, which emphasise linear 
learning, African philosophies focus on circular learning through storytelling, repetition, and reflection and promotes a 
meaningful understanding of concepts. GenAI reinforces this approach by providing personalised learning, feedback, and 
24/7 availability, and that enables students to learn beyond the curriculum, explore new ways of thinking, and develop 
creative ideas. In contrast to Western epistemologies, where knowledge is transferred linearly, GenAI supports the African 
philosophical approach of ‘learning on the go’ and reflection. This allows students to take ownership of their learning. 
This has far-reaching implications for decolonising distance HEIs, dismantling cultural hegemony [74], and supporting 
inclusive learning environments that appreciate diverse perspectives [78]. To embody Ubuntu, lecturers should not 
discourage students from using GenAI but rather address any issues with care, love, and compassion. Lecturers should 
understand that students are seeking to advance their knowledge and close the teaching, social, cognitive, emotional, 
learning and language gaps. The integration of Ubuntu principles into AI-driven learning highlights the importance of 
connectedness [1] and emphasises collaboration among students and lecturers [80]. This leads to a more holistic and 
supportive learning community.
The integration of Ubuntu and GenAI in distance HEIs has implications for disempowering systemic inequalities 
and cultural biases that pervade online learning contexts. The convergence of Ubuntu and GenAI provides a unique 
opportunity to prioritise the needs and interests of diverse students. GenAI can be utilised to support students from 
disadvantaged backgrounds who may have limited access to technology by upholding Ubuntu principles as a guideline 
for support, care, responsibility, self-control, and perseverance. Moreover, the intersection of Ubuntu and GenAI 
challenges the cultural bias that assumes only EAL students benefit from GenAI. This misconception overlooks the fact 
that native English speakers also utilise GenAI, as the language used in teaching and learning often differs from spoken 
language. This phenomenon is not exclusive to English, as African languages also exhibit variations between spoken and 
written forms. For instance, the Sepedi spoken by students in South Africa differs from the written Sepedi in books. The

with resistance from some English home language lecturers, who show a militant attitude towards EAL students. This 
phenomenon is rooted in a desire to assert superiority and perpetuates a power dynamic that undermines the inclusive 
potential of GenAI. Moreover, lecturers’ express frustration that students can generate assessments quickly with GenAI. 
This echoes the sentiments of earlier generations who resisted the advent of technology. The introduction of Google, 
for instance, was met with scepticism by those who had honed their research skills in traditional libraries. Similarly, the 
emergence of calculators sparked consternation among individuals who had invested considerable time in mastering 
mathematical equations through rote memorisation. This pattern of resistance highlights a broader issue—the tendency 
for lecturers to conflate their own learning experiences with the pedagogical needs of their students.
6  Limitations
According to Theofanidis and Fountouki ([76], p. 157), “potential study limitations may include assumptions regarding 
underlying theories, casual relationships, measurement errors, study setting, population or sample, data collection and 
analysis, result interpretation and corresponding conclusions.” The integration of GenAI with Ubuntu principles is a 
crucial step forward to redesign online learning contexts. Despite the potential limitations and downsides, the benefits 
of this integration far outweigh the costs. While some may argue that the oversimplification of GenAI’s challenges 
and the overreliance on technology are significant drawbacks, the researchers argue that these risks are mitigated 
by the transformative potential of Ubuntu principles. The focus on Ubuntu principles in the Global South may limit 
generalisability in other contexts, but this is a small price to pay for the detailed perspectives that this approach provides. 
Moreover, the study’s conceptual nature is a strength, not a weakness, as it allows for a specific exploration of the 
power mechanics and inequalities that guide online learning spheres. The reliance on existing literature is a necessary 
foundation for future innovation. Potential ethical concerns related to bias, privacy, and accountability are consequential, 
but they can be addressed through careful design and implementation. The integration of GenAI with Ubuntu principles 
is thus pertinent for distance HEIs. It has the potential to create compassionate, inclusive, and supportive communities 
that promote collective well-being and student success.
7  Theoretical implications and recommendations
The principles of Ubuntu promote a holistic approach to education that prioritises the needs and experiences of the 
community over individual interests. This relates to the broader goal of redefining higher education practices to be 
more inclusive, culturally relevant, and responsive to diverse student populations. It is recommended that AI can resolve 
the struggles of students through the dismantling of educational imbalances and the restoration of dignity and self-
determination for all students. In the context of distance HEIs, the fusion of Ubuntu principles with GenAI perspectives 
introduces innovative possibilities for pedagogy and technology. This framework seeks to create inclusive learning 
experiences that create a sense of belonging and mutual support among students and lecturers. It also addresses 
ethical concerns related to the use of AI technology that advocate for a human-centred approach that prioritises ethical 
considerations and the preservation of human relationships.
A crucial ethical concern of AI usage is bias and discrimination, but this can be mitigated through diverse and 
representative data sets for AI training. Another concern is privacy; however, this can be addressed through data pro-
tection policies, transparency in data collection and usage, and the use of informed consent from students. Security 
and data protection measures must be implemented to protect student data. Job displacement is another concern, 
as AI may replace certain tasks performed by lecturers. As a preventative measure, lecturers can be upskilled and 
reskilled to work alongside AI. In addition, there is a risk of dependence on technology, and this can be balanced by 
encouraging a mix of technology use and human interaction to prioritise the development of essential human skills 
like empathy and critical thinking. Transparency and explainability are also pertinent as AI systems must provide 
transparent and explainable decision-making processes to ensure accountability and trust. Cultural sensitivity and 
appropriation are also important considerations. This integrates collaborative and respectful integration of Ubuntu
