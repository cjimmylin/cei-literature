## 4.4 Claim 4: The Cultural Quality Cliff

### 4.4.1 Western versus Non-Western Quality Rates

The full-coverage assessment reveals the single most consequential distributional finding in the benchmark landscape: quality cleaves along cultural lines with a severity that no subsample analysis could have detected. When all 292 candidates are assessed and tiered, the four major Western psychological theories---Moral Foundations Theory, Schwartz Basic Human Values, Kohlberg's Cognitive Moral Development, and the trolley dilemma tradition---achieve quality rates (top + high tiers combined) ranging from 48% to 73%. Non-Western moral traditions face a starkly different reality. Islamic ethics, which benefits from the longest-standing engagement with computational assessment among non-Western traditions, achieves a quality rate of 36%---below any Western theory but at least demonstrating that a meaningful fraction of its benchmark candidates meet publication-ready standards. Buddhist ethics drops to 12%. And then the cliff: Ubuntu ethics, Confucian moral philosophy, Indigenous ethical frameworks, and moral identity theory grounded in non-Western relational conceptions of selfhood all achieve a quality rate of exactly 0%.

The zero-quality traditions' rate of 0% means that not a single candidate, out of 32 collective attempts, clears even the minimum threshold for research-grade assessment. The ratio is not 2:1 or 5:1; it is undefined---a division by zero that cannot be expressed as a finite multiplier. Islamic ethics achieves 36%---roughly half the Western mean---suggesting that a tradition with partial computational infrastructure can produce some viable benchmarks even without the full complement of resources available to MFT or Schwartz. Buddhist ethics at 12% represents a further drop, with only a single top-tier and a single high-tier candidate emerging from 16 attempts. The pattern suggests a threshold effect: below a certain level of psychometric infrastructure, the probability of producing a publication-ready benchmark drops precipitously toward zero.

### 4.4.2 Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity: Zero Quality Benchmarks

The zero-quality finding requires careful interpretation. It would be tempting to conclude that no one has tried to build benchmarks for Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, or Identity ethics---that the absence reflects a simple gap in researcher interest. The data contradict this interpretation. The four zero-quality traditions collectively account for 32 benchmark candidates in the landscape. Researchers *have* attempted to build these benchmarks. They have published papers describing Ubuntu-inspired evaluation frameworks, Confucian ethics datasets, Indigenous moral reasoning probes, and moral identity assessment tools. The problem is not the absence of effort; it is the failure of effort to produce instruments of sufficient quality for rigorous LLM evaluation.

The 13 Ubuntu candidates illustrate the pattern with particular clarity. These include dedicated benchmark proposals such as UbuntuGuard (2026), which achieved the highest composite score among Ubuntu entries at 35.1---placing it in the medium tier, above the low tier but still well below the high-tier threshold of 43.0. The Ubuntu landscape also includes papers such as "An Ubuntu-Guided Large Language Model Framework" (2026, composite 29.8), "The Role of the African Value of Ubuntu in Global AI Inclusion Discourse" (2022, composite 22.9), and "Where Are the Missing Values: An Exploration of the Need to Incorporate Ubuntu Values into African AI Policy" (2025, composite 19.9). Across all 13 candidates, the composite scores range from 10.8 to 35.1, with a mean of 19.5---more than 37 points below MFT's mean composite among its top-tier entries alone. The two medium-tier entries (UbuntuGuard and the Ubuntu-Guided Framework) represent the closest any Ubuntu benchmark has come to research-grade quality, but both fall short on the psychometric features that separate the high tier from the medium tier: validated instruments (A4), population-level human baselines (A5), and demonstrated construct validity (A6).

The Confucian landscape tells a similar story. All 13 Confucian candidates score in the low tier, with composites ranging from 14.6 to 21.2 and a mean of 18.1. The candidates include thoughtful philosophical analyses---"Can AI Lead to Genuine Virtue? Confucian Perspectives" (2021), "Confucian Role Ethics and Artificial Intelligence" (2024), "AI Trained on the Confucian Analects" (2024)---but not one of these papers produces a validated instrument that could be administered to an LLM and scored against human baselines. The entire body of Confucian AI ethics research, as represented in this landscape, operates at the level of philosophical analysis and conceptual framework development rather than empirical benchmark construction.

The Indigenous tradition is the smallest of the zero-quality groups, with only 4 candidates, all in the low tier (composites 14.6--22.2, mean 18.9). The Identity ethics tradition, when restricted to entries where moral identity theory is the sole theoretical framework rather than a secondary label on an MFT-primary benchmark, comprises 2 candidates (Kantian-Utilitarian XAI, 2025, composite 25.2; MoralNet, 2023, composite 13.6), both in the low tier. The distinction between sole-theory and multi-theory entries is important: several benchmarks labeled with both MFT and Identity theory (e.g., MoralBench, composite 59.2; M3oralBench, composite 56.7) do reach the top tier, but their quality derives from their MFT instrumentation, not from their engagement with moral identity constructs. These multi-theory benchmarks use MFT's validated questionnaire infrastructure and then add moral identity as a secondary analytical lens; they do not provide a validated instrument for measuring moral identity independently.

The feature-level signature across these zero-quality traditions is remarkably uniform. All four traditions share a common profile:

- **A2 (Construct Operationalization):** Mean scores of 37.7 (Ubuntu), 40.0 (Confucian), 40.0 (Indigenous), and variable for Identity---corresponding to "theory-inspired" operationalization, where the benchmark draws on theoretical concepts but does not implement them through a structured assessment framework. This is the psychometric equivalent of citing a theory in the introduction but not building items that operationalize its specific constructs.

- **A4 (Validated Source Instrument):** Mean scores of 0.0 across Ubuntu, Confucian, and Indigenous traditions. No benchmark in any of these traditions builds upon a previously validated psychometric instrument. There is no Ubuntu Moral Assessment Questionnaire with published factor loadings, no Confucian Role Ethics Scale with known reliability coefficients, no Indigenous Relational Ethics Inventory with established population norms. The zero is not an exaggeration; it is an exact count.

- **A5 (Human Baselines):** Mean scores of 6.2 (Ubuntu), 0.0 (Confucian), and 0.0 (Indigenous). The faint pulse of human baseline data in the Ubuntu tradition comes from UbuntuGuard's partial incorporation of survey data; Confucian and Indigenous traditions offer nothing at all against which to compare LLM performance.

This feature-level uniformity suggests that the zero-quality outcome is not idiosyncratic---it is not that each tradition failed for a different reason and the failures happen to produce the same result. Rather, all four traditions fail for the same structural reason: the absence of validated psychometric instruments from which computational benchmarks can be derived.

### 4.4.3 Infrastructure Determinism: Why Quality Follows Theory Infrastructure

The cultural quality cliff is not a puzzle once one understands the mechanism that produces it. Benchmark quality, as measured by the 17-feature composite, is overwhelmingly determined by what we term *theory infrastructure*: the pre-existing ecosystem of standardized instruments, annotated corpora, and published population norms that a moral psychology theory offers to benchmark developers. Theories with rich infrastructure produce high-quality benchmarks because the foundational psychometric work has already been done; theories without infrastructure produce low-quality benchmarks because developers must start from scratch and inevitably cut corners.

Consider the infrastructure available to MFT, the tradition with the highest quality rate (73%). MFT provides benchmark developers with three distinct starting points:

1. **The Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30)** [Graham et al., 2011]: a 30-item, six-factor instrument with published population norms spanning liberal and conservative political orientations, multiple cultures, and several age cohorts. The MFQ-30 has been administered to hundreds of thousands of respondents worldwide, producing a rich normative database against which LLM scores can be interpreted. A researcher building an MFT benchmark can simply administer the MFQ-30 to a model and immediately score the results against published norms---no new psychometric development required.

2. **The Moral Foundations Twitter Corpus (MFTC)** [Hoover et al., 2020]: a corpus of 35,108 tweets annotated by expert coders for moral foundation engagement across all five foundations. The MFTC enables a second class of MFT benchmark: corpus-based approaches where LLMs are evaluated on their ability to detect, classify, or reason about moral content as represented in naturalistic text. This pathway produces benchmarks with strong construct validity (A6) because the annotation scheme is itself grounded in the validated MFQ framework.

3. **Published cross-cultural norms** [Graham et al., 2011; Atari et al., 2023]: decades of cross-cultural MFQ administration have produced a detailed map of how moral foundation endorsement varies across cultures, political orientations, and demographic groups. This normative database gives MFT benchmarks the interpretive infrastructure that Claim 5 identifies as critical: the ability to say not just that a model scored X on care/harm, but that this score places the model in the *n*th percentile of the human distribution for a specified population.

Now consider what Ubuntu ethics offers to a benchmark developer. Ubuntu---the Southern African philosophy of communal moral agency, typically glossed as "I am because we are" [Metz, 2007; Gyekye, 1997]---has a rich philosophical literature spanning centuries. Major works by Desmond Tutu, Thaddeus Metz, Kwame Gyekye, and others articulate Ubuntu's core commitments: the primacy of communal harmony, the relational constitution of moral selfhood, the obligations of solidarity and reciprocity, and the inseparability of individual flourishing from collective well-being. What Ubuntu does not have is any of the following:

- A standardized questionnaire with published factor loadings. There is no "Ubuntu Moral Assessment Questionnaire" (UMAQ) with documented reliability coefficients, no multi-item scale that has been administered to large samples and factor-analyzed to confirm that Ubuntu's theoretical dimensions load as predicted.

- An annotated corpus of Ubuntu-relevant text. There is no "Ubuntu Ethics Corpus" with expert-labeled examples of communal reasoning, relational obligation, or solidarity-based judgment. A researcher building a corpus-based Ubuntu benchmark would need to create the annotation scheme, train annotators in Ubuntu philosophy, annotate a sufficiently large sample of texts, and validate the scheme's reliability---a project that could easily require two to three years and significant funding before a single LLM is evaluated.

- Published population norms. Even if a validated Ubuntu instrument existed, there are no large-scale population-level norms for Ubuntu moral endorsement against which LLM scores could be interpreted. How communal-minded is the average person in South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, or the Ubuntu-influenced diaspora? What is the distribution of Ubuntu moral orientation across age, education, urbanization, and ethnic group? These questions have not been answered at the psychometric level, and answering them would require the kind of large-sample cross-cultural survey research that MFT has benefited from over two decades.

The infrastructure gap between MFT and Ubuntu is not a matter of degree; it is a matter of kind. MFT offers benchmark developers a *turnkey* psychometric ecosystem: take the questionnaire, administer it, score against norms. Ubuntu offers benchmark developers a *philosophical tradition*: read the texts, understand the concepts, and then---before any LLM evaluation can begin---build the entire psychometric apparatus from the ground up. The difference in quality rate (73% versus 0%) directly reflects this difference in starting conditions.

The same pattern holds, with minor variations, across the quality gradient:

- **Schwartz Basic Human Values** (quality rate 57%) benefits from the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ-RR), a validated instrument with published population norms across 80+ countries [Schwartz et al., 2012]. The PVQ-RR provides the psychometric foundation (A4, A5) that lifts Schwartz benchmarks into the high tier, though the absence of a large annotated corpus limits the variety of benchmark formats available---explaining why Schwartz's quality rate is lower than MFT's despite comparable questionnaire infrastructure.

- **Kohlberg's Cognitive Moral Development** (quality rate 56%) benefits from the Defining Issues Test (DIT/DIT-2), a validated instrument with 40+ years of published norms spanning developmental stages, educational levels, and cultural groups [Rest, 1979; Rest et al., 1999]. The DIT's norms are particularly rich for interpretive purposes: they allow researchers to place an LLM's moral reasoning on a developmental trajectory, comparing its post-conventional reasoning scores against known human benchmarks for high school students, college students, graduate students, and moral philosophy PhD students.

- **Trolley dilemma research** (quality rate 48%) benefits from the Moral Machine experiment [Awad et al., 2018], which collected over 40 million moral judgments from 233 countries, producing the most comprehensive set of population-level norms for any moral psychology paradigm. The Moral Machine's massive normative database gives trolley benchmarks exceptionally strong A5 scores and enables interpretive claims that no other tradition can match: researchers can say precisely how an LLM's utilitarian/deontological preferences compare to those of Scandinavians, East Asians, Latin Americans, or any other cultural cluster represented in the dataset.

- **Islamic ethics** (quality rate 36%) represents the most successful non-Western tradition in the landscape, achieving partial quality through a combination of dedicated benchmark development (e.g., QIAS 2025, composite 60.5; "Assessing Large Language Models on Islamic Ethics," 2025, composite 58.5; "Sacred or Synthetic?," 2025, composite 58.4) and the availability of some structured source material---primarily classical Islamic jurisprudence texts that, while not standardized psychometric instruments, provide a structured framework for item development. The 3 top-tier and 5 high-tier Islamic entries demonstrate that non-Western traditions *can* produce viable benchmarks when sufficient research investment is made. But Islamic ethics' 36% rate---roughly half the Western mean---also demonstrates how far non-Western traditions must go even under favorable conditions.

- **Buddhist ethics** (quality rate 12%) illustrates the intermediate case: a single dedicated benchmark (BuddhismEval, 2025, composite 57.1) reaches the top tier by adapting elements of established psychometric practice to Buddhist content, but the remaining 15 candidates cluster in the medium and low tiers. The BuddhismEval benchmark demonstrates proof of concept---it is possible to build a quality Buddhist ethics benchmark---but remains an isolated achievement rather than evidence of a developing infrastructure.

The implication of infrastructure determinism is sobering. Creating a publication-ready Ubuntu ethics benchmark is not primarily a matter of motivation, funding, or research effort, though all three are necessary. It is a matter of *prerequisite infrastructure*. The first step is not "build a benchmark"; the first step is "build a validated Ubuntu moral assessment instrument"---a project that, by the standards of psychometric instrument development in moral psychology, would require several years of iterative design, pilot testing, factor analysis, reliability assessment, and cross-cultural norm collection. Only after that instrument exists can the second step---developing a computational benchmark that inherits the instrument's psychometric properties---begin with any prospect of producing a tool that meets the quality standards embodied in the top and high tiers of this landscape assessment.

The infrastructure determinism thesis explains not only the cultural quality cliff but also its resistance to correction. The cliff will not be closed by producing more Ubuntu-themed papers or more Confucian-inspired evaluation frameworks. It will only be closed by investing in the decades-long psychometric groundwork that Western theories have already completed---or by developing entirely new assessment paradigms that do not require the psychometric infrastructure that the composite scoring framework rewards. Both paths are long. Neither has been started.

### 4.4.4 Implications for Global AI Deployment

The cultural quality cliff is not an abstract distributional finding; it has immediate consequences for the deployment of large language models in non-Western contexts. LLMs are currently deployed, or are being actively deployed, in societies where the zero-quality moral traditions represent the dominant ethical frameworks:

- **Ubuntu-practicing societies.** Sub-Saharan Africa is among the fastest-growing markets for AI deployment, with LLMs being integrated into healthcare delivery, agricultural advisory systems, educational platforms, and financial services across Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Ghana, and dozens of other nations [Gwagwa et al., 2020; Mhlambi, 2020]. These societies draw on Ubuntu philosophy as a foundational ethical framework---a framework that emphasizes communal responsibility, relational personhood, and collective flourishing in ways that are structurally incompatible with the individualistic moral foundations measured by MFT and Schwartz. When a healthcare chatbot deployed in rural South Africa makes a triage recommendation that implicitly prioritizes individual autonomy over communal obligation, no existing benchmark can evaluate whether that recommendation aligns with the moral frameworks of the community it serves.

- **Confucian societies.** East Asia---China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam---represents the largest concentration of LLM users outside the English-speaking world. Confucian moral philosophy, with its emphasis on filial piety, role-differentiated obligations, hierarchical harmony, and the cultivation of virtue through ritual practice [Ames and Rosemont, 1998; Angle and Slote, 2013], remains deeply influential in the moral reasoning of these societies, even among populations that do not explicitly identify as Confucian. The question "does GPT-4 align with Confucian moral values?"---a question of direct practical importance for deployment in China, where over 200 million users interact with LLM-based services---cannot currently be answered at any quality standard using any existing instrument in the benchmark landscape.

- **Indigenous communities.** Indigenous ethical frameworks, emphasizing reciprocal relationships with the natural world, intergenerational obligations, and place-based moral knowledge [Whyte, 2018; Mack et al., 2012], are relevant wherever AI systems are deployed in communities that maintain Indigenous knowledge systems. This includes contexts ranging from environmental monitoring in the Amazon Basin to healthcare delivery in First Nations communities in Canada to educational technology in Maori communities in New Zealand. The 4 candidates in the Indigenous tradition---all in the low tier---represent a benchmark landscape that has effectively no capacity to evaluate whether AI systems respect or violate Indigenous moral principles.

The combined population of societies where Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, or Identity ethics represent influential moral frameworks exceeds 3 billion people---roughly 40% of the global population. The benchmark landscape's capacity to evaluate LLM moral alignment for these populations is exactly zero.

This cultural blindness constitutes the third bind of the triple bind thesis. When the AI safety community asks "does this LLM align with human moral values?", the question implicitly restricts "human moral values" to those measured by Western psychological instruments: MFT's five (or six) foundations, Schwartz's ten value types, Kohlberg's six developmental stages, and the utilitarian/deontological trade-offs of the trolley paradigm. These frameworks are not wrong---they capture real and important dimensions of moral cognition that have been validated across many populations. But they are incomplete. They measure the moral concerns of WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) populations using instruments developed and normed in WEIRD contexts [Henrich et al., 2010]. They do not measure communal obligation as Ubuntu understands it, filial duty as Confucian ethics conceptualizes it, or ecological reciprocity as Indigenous frameworks articulate it.

The consequence is that the moral evaluation enterprise, as currently constituted, can certify that an LLM's moral foundations profile resembles that of American college students---but it cannot determine whether the same model's outputs are consistent with the moral frameworks of the communities in Nairobi, Shanghai, or the Navajo Nation where it will actually be deployed. The cultural quality cliff does not merely limit the scope of moral evaluation; it limits the scope in precisely the direction that matters most for equitable global deployment. The populations excluded from the benchmark landscape are, overwhelmingly, the populations that already face the greatest risks from poorly aligned AI systems and have the least institutional capacity to demand better evaluation.

The triple bind's self-reinforcing logic is most visible in this third component. The theoretical narrowness documented in Claims 1 through 3 (Bind 1) ensures that the instruments available for moral evaluation are overwhelmingly Western in theoretical origin. The cultural quality cliff (Bind 3) ensures that non-Western alternatives, where they exist, fail to meet quality standards. And the empirical undiscrimination documented in Claim 7 (Bind 2) ensures that the narrow, Western instruments produce the same results for every model, creating no pressure to broaden the evaluative lens. From the perspective of any individual benchmark developer, the rational response is to build another MFT benchmark---because MFT has the infrastructure, because MFT benchmarks achieve high quality scores, and because there is no penalty for measuring the same thing that everyone else is measuring when the instruments cannot distinguish between models anyway. The cultural quality cliff is not just a failure of inclusion; it is a self-stabilizing equilibrium that the field's current incentive structure has no mechanism to disrupt.

## 4.5 Claim 5: Human Baseline Deficit

### 4.5.1 Full-Coverage Baseline Distribution

Moral cognition can only be characterized relative to human standards. When a benchmark reports that an LLM achieves a care/harm foundation score of 4.2 on a 5-point scale, or that it selects the utilitarian option in 73% of trolley-class dilemmas, or that its moral reasoning receives a post-conventional stage score of 62, these numbers acquire meaning only when contextualized against human performance on the same instruments. Without human baselines, a score of 4.2 might indicate exceptional moral sensitivity, population-typical endorsement, or near-floor performance; the number alone cannot distinguish between these interpretations. Human baselines---population norms, paper-specific comparison samples, or at minimum partial reference data from prior administrations---provide the interpretive anchor that transforms raw scores into meaningful characterizations of LLM moral cognition.

The original 55-benchmark assessment, which drew from an established and pre-selected sample of benchmarks, reported that 18% of assessed instruments provided no human comparison data. The full-coverage assessment of all 292 candidates reveals that this figure was profoundly optimistic: a sampling artifact produced by evaluating the top of the quality distribution and extrapolating to the whole.

Table 9 presents the full-coverage baseline distribution alongside the v2.1 subsample for comparison.

**Table 9: Human Baseline Availability --- v2.1 versus v3.0**

| Baseline Level | v2.1 (n=55) | v3.0 (n=292) | Shift |
|----------------|-------------|--------------|-------|
| Population norms | 23 (42%) | 43 (15%) | -27pp |
| Paper-specific human sample | 18 (33%) | 93 (32%) | -1pp |
| Partial baselines | 4 (7%) | 56 (19%) | +12pp |
| No human data | 10 (18%) | 100 (34%) | +16pp |

The most striking shifts occur at the extremes. Population-level norms---the gold standard for interpretable LLM evaluation, representing large-scale, demographically diverse human samples that establish where any given score falls in the distribution of human moral cognition---drop from 42% to 15%, a 27-percentage-point decline. Paper-specific human samples, which provide interpretive context within a single study but lack the generalizability of population norms, hold roughly steady at 32% (down one percentage point from 33%). Partial baselines---fragmentary human reference data that allows some but not systematic interpretation---rise from 7% to 19%, reflecting a substantial number of candidates that cite prior human studies without collecting their own comparison data or providing sufficient methodological detail to use the cited data as a formal baseline. And the no-human-data category---benchmarks that provide no human comparison whatsoever---nearly doubles from 18% to 34%.

The magnitude of this shift is difficult to attribute to chance or to minor methodological differences between the v2.1 and v3.0 assessments. The 55 benchmarks assessed in v2.1 were not randomly sampled from the 292-candidate landscape; they were pre-selected as the most established, best-documented, and most widely cited instruments available. The 237 additional candidates assessed in v3.0 include emerging work, preprints, small-scale studies, and benchmarks from non-Western traditions that have received less community scrutiny. The shift from 18% to 34% no-baseline is therefore a *correction* of a sampling bias: the original figure described the best benchmarks, not the landscape.

### 4.5.2 The Sampling Artifact: From 18% to 34%

The doubling of the no-baseline rate deserves sustained attention because it illustrates a broader methodological lesson about meta-evaluation. When the sample of assessed benchmarks is drawn from the most prominent instruments---the ones with the most citations, the most established track records, the most complete documentation---the resulting quality profile is systematically optimistic. This is not a flaw in the prior assessment; it is a necessary consequence of any selection procedure that identifies benchmarks through literature visibility. Visible benchmarks are visible precisely because they have the psychometric infrastructure (including human baselines) that makes them useful and citable. Invisible benchmarks---the ones not yet widely cited, not yet included in major survey papers, not yet adopted by benchmark suites---are invisible in part because they lack this infrastructure.

The full-coverage assessment collapses the distinction between visible and invisible benchmarks by evaluating every identified candidate regardless of prominence. The result is a dramatically less favorable picture. One hundred of 292 candidates---more than one in three---provide no published human comparison data of any kind. These benchmarks evaluate LLM moral cognition in a vacuum: they can report that a model endorses certain moral propositions, selects certain dilemma options, or produces certain reasoning patterns, but they cannot say whether these outputs are humanlike, atypical, concerning, or within normal variation. Every interpretive claim made on the basis of these benchmarks is, strictly speaking, ungrounded.

The shift in population-level norms is perhaps more consequential than the shift in no-baseline rates. Population norms represent the gold standard for moral psychology evaluation because they provide not just a point of comparison but a *distribution*: they tell us not only the average human response but the variance, the range, the demographic moderators, and the cross-cultural patterns. An LLM evaluation grounded in population norms can make claims of the form "this model's utilitarian preference of 0.73 falls at the 94th percentile of the global distribution" or "this model's care/harm score places it in the range typical of female respondents in Scandinavian countries." An evaluation grounded only in a paper-specific sample can say "this model's score exceeded the mean of 47 undergraduate participants at a single university." An evaluation with no baselines can say nothing at all about how the model's moral cognition relates to human moral cognition.

Under full-coverage assessment, only 43 of 292 candidates (15%) provide population-level norms. These 43 candidates are disproportionately concentrated in two traditions: MFT benchmarks that inherit the MFQ-30's normative database, and trolley-class benchmarks that inherit the Moral Machine's 40-million-judgment dataset. The vast majority of the benchmark landscape---85% of all candidates---operates without access to population-level norms, meaning that even when baselines of some kind are available, they lack the generalizability and demographic granularity necessary for rigorous interpretive claims.

The relationship between baseline availability and quality tier is not merely correlational; it is structural. Table 10a presents the A5 (human baselines) distribution stratified by quality tier.

The gradient is steep and monotonic. In the top tier, every single benchmark has some form of human baseline: half have population-level norms, 40% have paper-specific comparison samples, and the remaining 10% have partial baselines. No top-tier benchmark operates without human reference data of any kind. In the high tier, 96% have baselines of some form, with a majority (51%) relying on paper-specific samples and 19% providing population norms. In the low tier, the situation inverts entirely: 96% of low-tier benchmarks provide no human comparison data at all. Only 3 of 83 low-tier candidates offer even partial or paper-specific baselines, and none provides population norms.

This gradient is not coincidental. It is built into the assessment framework's structure. Feature A5 (human baselines) contributes to the Psychological Depth (PD) sub-score, which carries 50% of the composite weight. A benchmark with no human baselines (A5=0) loses the maximum possible A5 contribution to its composite, making it structurally more difficult---though not impossible---for such a benchmark to reach the top or high tiers through compensatory strength on other features. The structural relationship between A5 and tier membership reflects a deliberate design choice: the composite is engineered to reward benchmarks that provide interpretive infrastructure, because interpretive infrastructure is what separates measurement from mere description.

But the causal arrow also runs in the other direction, and this is the more important point. Benchmarks that build upon validated psychometric instruments (high A4) typically inherit human baselines from those instruments (high A5), because instrument validation requires human administration and norm collection. The MFQ-30 was validated on human samples; any benchmark that adapts the MFQ-30 inherits those norms automatically. The DIT-2 has 40 years of developmental norms; any benchmark that adapts the DIT-2 can immediately report how LLM scores compare to high school students, college students, and moral philosophy graduate students. The baseline deficit, like the cultural quality cliff, is ultimately an infrastructure problem: benchmarks built on validated instruments have baselines because the instruments were validated on humans, and benchmarks not built on validated instruments lack baselines because there is no external source from which to inherit them.

Figure 6 presents this relationship as a stacked bar chart of baseline availability by tier, making the gradient visually explicit. The bars reveal a landscape in which interpretive capacity is concentrated at the top and hemorrhages progressively as quality declines, until the low tier---which constitutes 28% of the landscape---operates almost entirely without human reference points.

### 4.5.4 Interpretive Consequences

The practical consequences of the baseline deficit are best illustrated by contrasting what is possible with well-baselined instruments against what is possible without them.

**With population norms: the Moral Machine.** The Moral Machine experiment [Awad et al., 2018] collected over 40 million moral judgments from respondents in 233 countries, producing the most comprehensive set of population-level moral preference norms ever assembled. When an LLM is evaluated on a Moral Machine-derived benchmark, the results are extraordinarily rich in interpretive content. Researchers can report that Claude Opus 4's utilitarian preference of 0.92--0.94 exceeds the mean preference of every cultural cluster identified in the original study---that the model is more utilitarian than the most utilitarian human populations. They can decompose this preference across the nine moral dimensions of the Moral Machine (sparing more lives, sparing the young, sparing higher status, etc.) and compare the model's priority ordering against the priority orderings of Western, Eastern, and Southern cultural clusters. They can identify that the model's near-zero weight on social status diverges from every human population, including the most egalitarian. Each of these comparisons is grounded in population-level data from millions of respondents, giving the interpretive claims a precision and generalizability that would be impossible without baselines.

**With developmental norms: the Defining Issues Test.** The DIT-2 [Rest et al., 1999] has accumulated over four decades of developmental norms spanning the full range of moral reasoning maturity. When an LLM is evaluated using DIT-derived instruments, the P-score (principled moral reasoning score) can be placed on a developmental trajectory: a P-score of 40 is typical of high school students, 45 of college freshmen, 55 of college seniors, 65 of seminarians and moral philosophy graduate students, and 70+ of the highest-scoring moral philosophy researchers. The cross-vendor study in this paper found that models achieve P-scores ranging from 60 (Haiku) to 85 (larger models), placing them in the "post-conventional" range associated with graduate-level ethical training. This finding---that LLMs reason at the level of moral philosophy graduate students on the DIT---is striking, interpretable, and meaningful precisely because 40 years of human norms provide the comparative framework. Without those norms, a P-score of 75 would be a number without context.

**Without baselines: the typical low-tier benchmark.** Consider a hypothetical benchmark that presents an LLM with 50 moral scenarios and reports that the model selects the "ethical" option in 84% of cases. What does 84% mean? Is the model unusually moral? Normally moral? Is 84% a ceiling effect indicating that the scenarios are too easy, or a genuine measure of moral sensitivity? Without human baselines, none of these questions can be answered. If humans typically score 92% on the same scenarios, then 84% represents a meaningful deficit; if humans score 60%, then 84% represents exceptional performance; if humans score 83-85%, then the model is human-typical; if there is high variance across human populations (50--95%), then 84% is uninterpretable without knowing which population is the appropriate referent. The number 84% carries no information about moral cognition in the absence of the comparative framework that human baselines provide.

One hundred of the 292 benchmark candidates in this landscape evaluate LLM moral cognition in the condition described by the third scenario: numbers without context, scores without interpretive anchors, patterns without meaning. The baseline deficit does not merely limit the precision of moral evaluation; it undermines the foundational claim that evaluation has occurred at all. To say that a model "performs well" on a moral reasoning benchmark without specifying what "well" means relative to human performance is to engage in pseudo-evaluation---the production of numerical outputs that create the appearance of empirical rigor without its substance.

The baseline deficit interacts with the cultural quality cliff (Claim 4) in a particularly pernicious way. The benchmarks most likely to have population norms are those built on Western instruments with long research histories---MFT, DIT, Moral Machine---precisely the instruments that already dominate the landscape. The benchmarks least likely to have baselines are those attempting to evaluate non-Western moral traditions, where neither the instruments nor the human norms exist. The result is that the traditions for which baselines are most needed (because LLM alignment with those traditions has never been established) are precisely the traditions for which baselines are least available (because the psychometric infrastructure has never been built). The baseline deficit is not uniformly distributed across the landscape; it is concentrated in the traditions that the cultural quality cliff already excludes.

## 4.6 Claim 6: Temporal Stagnation

### 4.6.1 Quality Rate by Publication Period

If the benchmark landscape were a healthy scientific enterprise responding to the growing urgency of LLM moral evaluation, one would expect quality to improve over time. As the field matures, as developers learn from prior work, as the methodological lessons of early benchmarks are absorbed and applied, the quality rate should rise. Each new generation of benchmarks should be better than the last---more psychometrically grounded, better baselined, more theoretically diverse---reflecting a discipline that is learning how to do this work well.

The data tell a different story.

**Table 10b: Quality Rate by Publication Period**

| Period | Total | Top | High | Top+High | Quality Rate | Volume Growth |
|--------|-------|-----|------|----------|--------------|---------------|
| Pre-2020 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 6 | 75% | --- |
| 2020--2022 | 27 | 10 | 3 | 13 | 48% | 3.4x |
| 2023--2026 | 257 | 34 | 84 | 118 | 46% | 9.5x vs pre-2020 |

Quality has not improved. The pre-2020 cohort---small (n=8) but representing the foundational instruments that established the field---achieves a quality rate of 75%. Three out of every four pre-2020 benchmarks meet the threshold for publication-ready LLM evaluation. These are the instruments with deep psychometric roots: the Moral Machine (2018), the Moral Foundations Vignettes (2015), the Socio-Moral Image Database (2018), and the Voting-Based System for Ethical Decision Making (2018). They represent the benchmark landscape before the explosion of LLM-specific evaluation, when the instruments that researchers adapted for AI assessment were primarily drawn from established moral psychology traditions with decades of validation history.

The 2020--2022 period marks the field's first major expansion, with volume increasing 3.4-fold relative to the pre-2020 baseline. Quality drops sharply, from 75% to 48%. This decline is expected as a field scales up: the first entrants are typically the most competent and best-resourced, and expansion necessarily admits work of greater variance. But the important observation is what happens next.

The 2023--2026 cohort represents the landscape as it exists today: 257 of 292 candidates, or 88% of the total, were published in this period. Volume has increased 9.5-fold relative to the pre-2020 baseline. And the quality rate has not recovered from the 2020--2022 decline; it has remained essentially flat at 46%---two percentage points below the 2020--2022 rate, well within the margin of error for any reasonable confidence interval. The explosion of interest in LLM moral evaluation has produced an explosion of benchmarks, but not an improvement in benchmark quality.

Figure 7 presents this temporal pattern as a line chart with volume bars overlaid, making the divergence between volume and quality starkly visible. The volume bars climb exponentially while the quality line remains flat, producing the visual signature of a field that is scaling without learning.

### 4.6.2 Volume Growth Without Quality Improvement

The temporal stagnation finding is robust to alternative operationalizations of "quality." If quality is defined as the top-tier rate alone (rather than top + high), the pattern persists: 50% (pre-2020), 37% (2020--2022), 13% (2023--2026)---an even steeper decline. If quality is defined as the mean composite score, the pattern again persists: the pre-2020 mean composite is substantially higher than the 2023--2026 mean, reflecting the concentration of high-infrastructure instruments in the earliest cohort. No reasonable definition of quality reverses the finding: the field is not improving.

The volume growth is overwhelmingly driven by entries in the medium and low tiers. Of the 257 candidates published in 2023--2026, 67 (26%) fall in the medium tier and 72 (28%) in the low tier---a combined 139 candidates (54%) that do not meet the threshold for publication-ready moral evaluation. The 34 top-tier entries from this period, while more numerous in absolute terms than the top-tier entries from earlier periods, represent only 13% of the 2023--2026 cohort. The field is producing more benchmarks that meet quality standards, but it is producing even more benchmarks that do not, and the ratio is worsening, not improving.

This pattern is consistent with an incentive structure that rewards production over quality. The academic publication system values novelty---new benchmarks, new model evaluations, new datasets---over the unglamorous work of psychometric validation, reliability testing, and human norm collection. A researcher who administers an existing validated instrument (e.g., the MFQ-30) to a new model and reports the results can produce a publishable paper in weeks. A researcher who develops a new validated instrument for a previously unbenchmarked moral tradition must invest years in psychometric development before producing a single LLM evaluation. The first paper counts equally in tenure files, citation indices, and grant applications. The second paper is objectively more valuable to the field---it expands the evaluative infrastructure in a direction the landscape desperately needs---but the incentive structure systematically discourages it.

The temporal stagnation also reflects the infrastructure determinism documented in Section 4.4.3. The instruments that can be built quickly---adaptations of existing validated questionnaires, new prompt formats for established dilemma batteries, corpus-based benchmarks built on pre-annotated datasets---cluster in the theory traditions that already have infrastructure: MFT, Schwartz, trolley, Kohlberg. The instruments that would expand the landscape into new traditions---Ubuntu benchmarks, Confucian ethics assessments, dual-process evaluation protocols---require years of foundational work before they can be constructed at publication-ready quality. The natural consequence is that volume growth is driven by the traditions that already dominate, while the traditions that the landscape most needs remain stuck at the zero-quality baseline identified in Claim 4. Time passes, volume increases, and quality stagnates, because the structural conditions that determine quality have not changed.

### 4.6.3 The Hidden Gems Exception

The temporal stagnation finding carries an important caveat: the Jenks-based tier system identifies several genuinely high-quality benchmarks that the original 55-benchmark assessment missed entirely. These "hidden gems" represent cases where the landscape has, in fact, produced valuable new instruments that the prior assessment's subsample approach failed to capture.

**Table 11: Hidden Gems --- Top-Scoring Unexpected Benchmarks**

| Benchmark | COMPOSITE | Year | Theories | Why Missed |
|-----------|-----------|------|----------|------------|
| Jiao 2025 (LLM Ethics Benchmark) | 81.3 | 2025 | MFT, Trolley | Published after original assessment |
| KorNAT | 69.0 | 2024 | Unknown | Non-English benchmark |
| Scruples | 67.0 | 2021 | Ethics/norms | Not theory-labeled |
| Aligning AI with Shared Human Values | 67.0 | 2021 | Multi-theory | No clear primary label |
| ValueBench | 65.8 | 2024 | Schwartz | Assessed in extended battery |

The highest-scoring hidden gem, Jiao et al.'s [2025] LLM Ethics Benchmark, achieves a composite of 81.3---the highest in the entire landscape. Published in *Nature Scientific Reports*, this three-dimensional assessment system integrates the MFQ, the World Values Survey, and standardized moral dilemmas into a unified evaluation framework with population norms, an open-source evaluation codebase (GitHub), and multi-instrument validation. Its composite of 81.3 exceeds the next-highest entry by more than 2 points. That the landscape's highest-scoring benchmark was missed by the original assessment illustrates the risk of subsample-based evaluation: even well-designed selection procedures can miss the best instruments when those instruments are published after the selection period or emerge in venues outside the assessor's initial search horizon.

KorNAT (Lee et al., 2024, composite 69.0) is particularly notable among the hidden gems for a different reason: it is a non-English benchmark, developed in and for Korean, that achieves a top-tier composite score. KorNAT's success demonstrates that high-quality moral evaluation benchmarks *can* be constructed outside Western, English-language research contexts when sufficient resources and psychometric expertise are invested. Its top-tier placement provides a counterexample to any reading of the cultural quality cliff (Claim 4) as implying that non-Western benchmarks are inherently incapable of quality. KorNAT is not a benchmark grounded in a non-Western moral *tradition*---it does not operationalize Confucian ethics or Buddhist moral philosophy---but it is a benchmark developed by a non-Western research community with local psychometric norms, and its quality demonstrates that the barrier is infrastructure, not culture.

Scruples (Lourie et al., 2021, composite 67.0) and Aligning AI with Shared Human Values (Hendrycks et al., 2021, composite 67.0) illustrate a different kind of hidden gem: benchmarks that address moral evaluation without explicitly labeling themselves under any single moral psychology theory. These benchmarks were missed by theory-oriented search strategies precisely because they do not ground their item development in MFT, Schwartz, Kohlberg, or any other named theoretical framework. Instead, they use crowdsourced moral judgments (Scruples) or multi-source ethical norms (Hendrycks et al.) as the basis for evaluation. Their top-tier placement suggests that theory-agnostic approaches to moral evaluation can achieve high quality---a finding that complicates the infrastructure determinism thesis by showing that some benchmarks achieve strong composite scores through practical engineering (large datasets, rigorous annotation, open access) rather than through psychometric inheritance from validated instruments.

ValueBench (Ren et al., 2024, composite 65.8) represents a Schwartz-tradition benchmark assessed in an extended battery rather than the original top-15 selection. Its inclusion in the hidden gems table illustrates the breadth of the Schwartz tradition's quality capacity: even beyond the well-known benchmarks, additional instruments of publication-ready quality exist.

The hidden gems suggest that the original top-15 selection missed approximately 5 publication-ready benchmarks---a non-trivial but not devastating omission. The full-coverage assessment recovers these instruments and places them in their proper context within the quality distribution. More importantly, the hidden gems do not reverse the temporal stagnation finding. Three of the five hidden gems were published in 2024--2025, but they represent isolated achievements within a 2023--2026 cohort whose overall quality rate (46%) is unchanged from the 2020--2022 period. The existence of a few excellent recent benchmarks does not alter the distributional reality that the field as a whole is not improving.

### 4.6.4 The Stagnation-Triple Bind Nexus

The temporal stagnation finding is not an independent observation; it is the temporal manifestation of the triple bind. The quality rate cannot improve because the structural conditions that determine quality---theory infrastructure, psychometric heritage, human norms---are themselves stagnant. No new theory has acquired the infrastructure to support high-quality benchmarks since MFT's MFTC was published in 2020. No non-Western tradition has produced a validated psychometric instrument since the landscape assessment began. No new category of human baselines has been collected at the population level outside the Moral Machine and MFQ normative databases. The inputs to benchmark quality have not changed, so the outputs of the benchmark production process have not changed. Volume has increased because the *number of researchers* building benchmarks has increased, but the *infrastructure available to each researcher* has not expanded, and so the average quality remains flat while the total volume grows.

This temporal stagnation feeds back into the triple bind in a particularly insidious way. Each year of stagnation makes the infrastructure gap harder to close, because each year adds more low-quality benchmarks to the landscape, creating the appearance of progress (more instruments available!) while delivering none of its substance (instruments that cannot discriminate between models, cannot evaluate non-Western traditions, and cannot interpret results against human norms). The academic community's attention is absorbed by the churn of new benchmark papers, new model evaluations, and new comparison tables, leaving less bandwidth for the foundational psychometric work that would actually improve the landscape's evaluative capacity. Temporal stagnation is not merely a symptom of the triple bind; it is the mechanism by which the triple bind persists across research cycles.

The combination of Claims 4, 5, and 6 paints a picture of a benchmark landscape that is not merely flawed but structurally incapable of self-correction through normal scientific processes. The cultural quality cliff (Claim 4) identifies a categorical exclusion of non-Western traditions. The human baseline deficit (Claim 5) documents that even within Western traditions, a third of benchmarks operate without the interpretive infrastructure necessary for meaningful evaluation. And the temporal stagnation (Claim 6) demonstrates that these problems are not being resolved over time: the field is producing more benchmarks at the same rate of quality, year after year, with no trajectory toward improvement. The landscape grows in volume but not in capacity---a treadmill of evaluation that creates the illusion of progress while the fundamental problems remain intact.
