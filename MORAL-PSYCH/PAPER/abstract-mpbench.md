# Abstract

**Background.** As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in contexts requiring moral sensitivity, hundreds of papers now claim to measure "moral reasoning" in these systems. Yet it remains unclear whether existing benchmarks genuinely engage the constructs established by moral psychology, or merely probe surface-level moral vocabulary. No systematic meta-evaluation has examined how well the landscape of LLM moral benchmarks covers the major theories of moral cognition.

**Method.** We conducted a multi-stage meta-evaluation of moral psychology benchmarks for LLM assessment. From a curated vault of 2,099 AI ethics papers, we extracted 222 benchmark candidates using a five-signal detection pipeline (keyword scoring, benchmark/evaluation flags, data availability, keyword density, and known benchmark names). A twelve-perspective virtual expert council assessed 55 candidates across a novel 162-feature extended (28-feature core) characterization framework organized into three tiers: Psychological Depth (50% weight), Practical Ease (30%), and Citation Impact (20%). We applied composite scoring with hard gates, theory caps, and diversity constraints to select a top-15 benchmark set. We also expanded the vault's keyword scorer, adding patterns for underrepresented theories, and conducted implementation trials on selected benchmarks.

**Results.** We identified five critical findings. First, Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) dominates the landscape, accounting for 101 of 222 candidates after scorer expansion; no other theory exceeds 69. Second, construct validity is weak: the median A6 score across 55 assessed benchmarks is 3 out of 5, with only 6 benchmarks (11%) achieving multi-study validation. Third, three of eight major moral psychology theories---Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Dyadic Morality---have zero dedicated LLM benchmarks, though our expanded keyword scorer surfaced 9, 18, and 7 papers respectively that partially engage these theories. Fourth, 18% of assessed benchmarks lack any published human comparison data. Fifth, format mismatch is pervasive: Likert scales, ranking tasks, and behavioral measures designed for human participants require substantial adaptation for LLM administration.

**Conclusions.** The current LLM moral evaluation landscape suffers from severe theoretical imbalance, weak psychometric grounding, and missing human baselines. We release our 162-feature extended (28-feature core) characterization framework, expanded keyword scorer, and implementation trial protocols as resources for benchmark developers. We characterize this work as a descriptive mapping with exploratory computational analyses, noting important methodological constraints including the self-administration confound inherent in LLM self-evaluation. We call for dedicated benchmarks targeting Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Dyadic Morality to achieve adequate coverage of moral psychological constructs in LLM evaluation.
