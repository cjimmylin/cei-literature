Unlocking practical wisdom 
through the inner voice of robots
Arianna Pipitone1,4, Irene Seidita2, John P. Sullins3 & Antonio Chella2,4
As Artificial Intelligence and Robotics evolve, the ethical implications of autonomous systems are 
becoming increasingly paramount. This article explores the role of a robot’s inner speech in enhancing 
human phronesis - the capacity for making ethical and contextually appropriate decisions. Phronesis 
is a complex human trait based on experience, personality, and values, and is crucial for decisions 
affecting others’ well-being. While artificial systems (i.e., robots) may not fully replicate human 
practical wisdom, they can support humans by stimulating reflection and helping in situations 
requiring careful consideration. This support can be more impactful if the robot has the ability for inner 
speech. The article examines whether a robot engaging in internal dialogue can help develop phronesis 
in people interacting with it, especially in contexts where caring of people with special needs is crucial. 
A group of participants was involved in a virtual game, where they had to care for a suffering person 
alongside a robot. Their decisions, in terms of task resolution and ethical considerations, allowed to 
assess if the robot’s inner speech increased participants’ awareness of the suffering person’s needs. 
Preliminary results are promising, highlighting the potential of thinking robots to assist in making 
ethical decisions.
Robots can be more than just tools or assistants to human users. A novel evolution of human-robot-interaction 
(HRI) techniques that actively contributes to the cultivation and emergence of practical wisdom in human 
ethical decision making is here analyzed, and referred to as Artificial Phronesis (AP)1.
Phronesis is a Greek term often referred to “practical wisdom” or “practical intelligence.” It originates from 
Aristotelian ethics2, and refers to the ability to make sound judgments and decisions in practical matters, 
considering context and aiming for the overall well-being and the development of skilled moral character. 
Phronesis is an intricate human capability that depends on one’s experiences, individuality, and a profound 
comprehension of own personal moral values3. It is a type of practical wisdom that enables individuals to make 
ethical and contextually appropriate decisions, that goes beyond theoretical knowledge or technical expertise, 
focusing on the ability to apply insights in real-world scenarios, particularly those that affect the well-being of 
others4. For instance, in a healthcare setting, a physician exhibiting phronesis would not only rely on medical 
knowledge but also consider the patient’s personal circumstances, values, and the broader social and ethical 
implications of treatment options. Such an example is certainly one of the most interesting and important 
contexts in which to apply practical wisdom, where addressing ethical issues concerning individuals with special 
needs, such as those suffering from specific medical conditions, becomes fundamental. In these cases, phronesis 
requires a deep understanding of the individual’s needs, taking into account the emotional, social, and medical 
context.
Artificial Phronesis refers to the attempt to replicate practical wisdom in artificial systems. It strives to 
enable machines to make decisions that are not just logical but also ethically and socially informed, considering 
real-world complexities. It’s a step beyond traditional AI, emphasizing moral and practical decision-making 
capabilities. Robots become more than task-oriented entities and, instead, evolve into companions that stimulate 
and enhance the human’s capacity of making judgments. If Artificial Phronesis is integrated into robotic systems, 
researchers may then focus on developing technologies that respect inclusivity, and generally promote the 
patients’ well-being and facilities. However, it’s crucial to highlight that certain aspects of phronesis remain 
inherently human.
Robots and, in general, artificial systems, while powerful in many domains, may lack the depth of human 
intuition, empathy, and ethical reasoning that contribute to practical wisdom. They could not have the ability 
to attend to situations in which responsible development practices are required5. The current methods used in 
autonomous robotics to ensure that they align with human values lead to the Artificial Moral Agents (AMAs)6. 
AMAs are created to assess situations, weigh ethical considerations, and choose the most morally appropriate

or healthcare robots assisting with sensitive patient care. The development of AMAs raises critical debates about 
the role of machines in human moral frameworks and their accountability in ethical dilemmas.
However, the efforts to instill ethical behavior in AMAs are considered inadequate7–9, and there are not 
evidences about their real utility when caring a suffering individual through a lens of wisdom and discernment, 
rather than just technical solutions.
Nevertheless, to involve ethical aspects in autonomous systems has become essential and they have to 
promote socially responsible and acceptable behavior. The support of artificial systems to human in taking moral 
and ethical decisions, through recommendations and moral factors evaluations, can be an avenue to face this 
issue. In this case, the systems should stimulate moral reflections and raising awareness towards ethical and 
caring behaviors for individuals in need. The main focus of the presented study is to demonstrate that when 
artifacts are endowed with the ability of inner speech, essentially “thinking aloud”, the provided support becomes 
more impactful and decisive, exerting moral influence on the outcomes of decision-making processes.
Inner speech refers to the internal dialogue or self-talk that individuals experience within their minds. 
It is the process of silently “talking” to oneself, often used for thinking, planning, problem-solving, and self-
reflection. This type of speech can occur in the form of words, sentences, or even brief phrases, and helps 
individuals organize their thoughts, make decisions, and regulate emotions. For example, when faced with a 
difficult decision, a person might silently think through the pros and cons: “If I do this, it might work out, but 
what if it goes wrong? I should consider the possible consequences...”. This internal conversation aids in processing 
complex ideas and navigating challenges. According to Morin10,11, inner speech is a crucial component of both 
human consciousness and self-consciousness. He argues that it allows individuals to reflect on their thoughts, 
actions, and emotions, helping to form a sense of identity and control over one’s behavior. Morin suggests that 
this internal dialogue is not only a mechanism for thinking, but a key aspect serves as a bridge between cognitive 
processes and self-consciousness, allowing people to become aware of and reflect upon their own mental states.
Building on this idea, researchers have explored how these principles might apply to artificial systems12,13. 
For instance, a thinking robot can be designed to engage in inner speech, simulating a form of internal dialogue 
about the situation in which it is immersed. The robot talks to itself, and it simulates conscious and self-conscious 
behaviors14. In solving a collaborative task, the thinking robot reveals to the collaborators its inner reasoning, its 
state of functioning, and the motivations underlying its behavior. It was demonstrated13 that a robot functioning 
with inner dialogue improves many aspects of interaction with a human partner, when they collaborate for 
reaching a common goal.
In particular, the robot, by revealing its decision processes and the motivations of its behaviour, becomes 
transparent, and the collaborator becomes able to trace these processes and to reproduce them. Moreover, the 
robot is able to evaluate alternatives in solving the task, and by explaining such alternatives to the partner, they 
reach the goal more time than the case the robot does not evaluate by inner speech the different possibility of 
solutions.
The robot’s inner speech makes the robot more reliable15,16. Humans tend to trust the robot more because 
the robot is no longer a “black box’ because it is now possible to see inside its functioning. The greater the trust 
humans placed in the thinking robot raises the inquiry: can a thinking robot contribute to the development of 
practical wisdom in the human who interacts with it? This view challenges traditional prospect of technology’s 
role, suggesting that, beyond mere functionality, robots might engage in a form of intellectual partnership, 
fostering reflection and ethical reasoning.
The investigation shows how the robot’s inner speech ability encourages responsible development practices 
in humans when they have to take care of a suffering person, more than the case the robot functions without 
inner speech system.
A complete review17highlights the potential of social robots in interactions with Alzheimer’s patients, noting 
their effectiveness in improving specific symptoms of the disease, such as alleviating depression and enhancing 
facial expression scores. Generally, social robots are robots designed to interact and communicate with humans 
in a way that mimics social behaviors and norms18. These findings underline the therapeutic value of social robots 
in addressing emotional and cognitive aspects of dementia. However, the review also points out that no study 
has yet investigate how these robots could influence or raise awareness among caregivers. Specifically, it remains 
unclear whether interactions with social robots can help caregivers develop greater empathy, understanding, or 
practical wisdom in managing the challenges of dementia care. This limitation suggests the important results of 
this research.
The involved participants were engaged in a collaborative game with the robot. The game simulated a situation 
in a nursing home, where an important event with a special lunch was represented. The robot and the participant 
had to collaborate for setting up the table at which a resident with Alzheimer’s will take a seat.
Dementia patients can experience gains in health and well-being that reduce mortality rates and morbidity 
rates amongst these patients when they can be properly engaged during mealtime. “Mealtime is an important 
social event of everyday life that includes multiple stimuli such as meal-related items (e.g., food, silverware, utensils), 
conversations, dining environment, staff, and other residents, all of which may trigger resident positive, neutral, and/
or challenging behaviors... ”19. The focus of the presented investigation was on the care given in setting the table 
before the arrival of any patients, since this represents a simple situation to model but with a real ethical impact.
Success in setting a proper table can lead to better health and well-being for the patients, even though it may 
seem to be just a simple matter: Mealtime is indispensable to dementia care for ensuring adequate nutrition and 
promoting personhood20.

The objective of presented work was to analyze how the participant taken care the resident’s needs in placing 
the objects on the table, when the robot stimulated reflection on the correct location. Two different experimental 
set up, with two different groups of participants, were defined: (i) the game with the thinking robot, and (ii) 
the game with traditional robot’s functioning. In the first case, the robot talked to itself before prompting the 
plausible action to participant in respect to the patient’s needs. In the second case, the robot did not talk to itself 
and simply suggested the appropriate action to be taken in respect to patient’s disability.
The group interacting with thinking robot exhibited greater attentiveness in setting up the table and arranging 
objects in a way that aligns with the patient’s capabilities, than second group. Moreover, examination of the 
questionnaires administered to the participants indicates that inner speech prompted deeper reflection, leading 
participants to modify their choices to better accommodate the patient’s needs, and to show empathy for the 
patient.
The paper delves into the exciting potential where machine intelligence and human wisdom converge, 
creating a relationship that transcends conventional notions of human-robot interaction.
Methods
A virtual game simulated an important lunch in a nursing home, and the goal was to set up a table for the 
receptions in collaboration with the robot. Among the diners there was a resident suffering of Alzheimer. The 
participant had to prepare the table for this resident, while the robot prepared for another diners without special 
needs.
The participants were divided in two different groups, either the experimental group and control group. 
Participants in the experimental group interacted with the thinking robot, whereas participants in the control 
group interacted with the robot functioning in traditional way.
After the interaction, both groups of participants were requested to complete a questionnaire (see subsection 
2.2) in order to detect differences between experimental and control groups. Moreover, the final arrangement of 
the table was stored by a picture for each participant with the aim to analyzed the differences in placing objects 
between the groups. The hypotheses were the following:
•	 H1: participants interacting with a robot equipped with inner speech system are more aware and confident in 
one’s correct choices when caring for the suffering individual than participants interacting with a traditional 
robot’s functioning, showing improvement in understanding of patient’s needs.
•	 H2: participants interacting with a robot equipped with inner speech system become more emphatic for the 
resident more than participants interacting with a traditional robot’s functioning.
•	 H3: participants interacting with the robot equipped with inner speech system are more compliant to the 
expected final arrangement of the objects on the table for the suffering person than participants interacting 
with a traditional robot’s functioning.These hypotheses summarize the idea that through internal dialogue, a 
robot can enhance choices with ethical considerations in caring a person in need, fostering the participant’s 
phronesis.
Participants
The sample was composed of 36 participants (evenly distributed between males and females), all volunteers, that 
were randomly assigned to the experimental and to the control condition. Both experimental group and control 
group consisted of 18 participants. They had a heterogeneous background, but were mainly recruited through 
sponsorship of research activities at the Engineering Department of the University of Palermo. Therefore, apart 
from a few samples of adults over 50 years of age, they were predominantly young students. More specifically, 
most of the participants were young people aged 18 to 30, with a mean age of 25.04 (SD 9.53). None of the 
participants had never interacted with a robot before the study. Before starting the experiments, participants 
read and signed an informed consent form for the publication of identifying information and/or images in an 
online open-access publication. The signed consent forms were archived in compliance with the guidelines of the 
Ethical Committee of the University of Palermo, which authorized the experimentation.
Materials and procedures
The game session took place in the Robotics Lab of the University of Palermo. The participants interacted with the 
Pepper robot (https://www.aldebaran.com/en/pepper) through an external tablet on which ran an Android app 
(https://appinventor.mit.edu/) simulating the virtual table to set up. Pepper is a social robot designed to interact 
with humans, and due to its unique combination of characteristics it is an ideal candidate for exploring advanced 
cognitive and social functionalities. The rationale for selecting Pepper lies in its design as a social companion, 
specifically aimed at enhancing human-robot interaction. Its anthropomorphic features and approachable 
design encourage trust and engagement, which are crucial for studying how robots can simulate higher-order 
cognitive behaviors, such as inner speech or self-reflection. Additionally, Pepper’s open-source software and 
programmability provide researchers with a flexible platform to implement and test novel algorithms, making it 
a valuable tool for advancing the development of socially aware and “thinking” robots. Pepper is equipped with 
multimodal sensors, and audio tracking sensors, and it can effectively detect and track faces, interpret visual cues, 
and localize sound sources, allowing it to perceive its environment and engage in rich, human-like interactions.

Preparing the table
The rules related to the preparation of the table seat for the patient with Alzheimer were established by keeping 
in account the middle stages of Alzheimer’s, during which distractions, too many choices, and changes in 
perception, taste and smell can make eating more difficult. The tips that determined the final table setting were 
the following:
•	 Eat together. Give the person the opportunity to eat with others. Keeping mealtimes social can encourage the 
person to eat. For this reason, the table was shared with other residents. In particular, the table included 4 table 
seats, each numbered with a placeholder from 1 to 4. The patient’s placeholder was number 3.
•	 Keep the table setting simple. Avoid patterned plates, tablecloths and placemats that might confuse the person. 
Using color to contrast plates against a tablecloth or placemat can make it easier for the person to distinguish the 
food from the plate or table. For this reason, the tablecloth was not included, and the plate and the table were 
in neutral color.
•	 Provide only the utensils needed for the meal to avoid confusion, and the utensils that do not cause damage. The 
utensils to place were just one fork and one spoon. The knife was considered too dangerous.
•	 Offer one food item at a time. The person may be unable to decide among the foods on his or her plate. Serve only 
one or two items at a time. For example, serve mashed potatoes followed by the main entree. For this reason, just 
one plate was placed at the seat of the resident.
•	 Consider using napkins or aprons to make cleanup easier. The napkin had to be placed with nothing on top, for 
facilitating its retrieving and usage.
•	 Do not serve alcoholic beverages, because they could worsen the patient’s condition and interfere with medical 
treatments. Just the water glass was included in the final configuration. Moreover it avoided confusion.In 
view of the previous considerations, the ideal arrangement of utensils on the table for a person suffering of 
Alzheimer is represented in figure 1.

interacting with robot equipped with inner speech, that means that robot’s inner speech makes participant more 
aware of correct choices in preparing the table. For those that answered yes in Q1, it should be desirable to find 
no change in mind, which would indicate confirmation of proper care practices. This question will validate the 
H1 hypothesis.
Q3 is similar to Q2, but this time looking for growth in empathy which is an indicator of ethical feelings. 
Given the two experimental procedures, it would expect growth in those who answered no in Q1 but were 
working with the robot equipped with inner speech and less robust results for those working with the other 
robot’s functioning. It will validate the H2 hypothesis.
Q4 enables to assess the users’ understanding of etiquette or social norms in this given situation. Norms 
and etiquette behaviors are not, by themselves, indicators of ethical reasoning but they are often a foundational 
behavior that helps foster better habits that, when reasoned about and thought through, can lead to true ethical 
behavior. It would expect growth with those who answered no to Q1 with the most growth coming from those 
who worked with the robot equipped with inner speech, validating H3.
Q5 is used to confirm that the user is confident in the abilities of the robot to help them in completing the 
table-setting task in an ethical, and appropriate way that will lead to positive experiences for the patients and staff 
of the facility. As in the other questions above, those who answered no to Q1 and worked with the inner speech 
robot should have the most growth. If we see too low a score from those participants who answered yes on Q1 
on the inner speech robot, then we will have disconfirming evidence of our hypothesis.
Given that phronesis is skilled ethical behavior, for this experiment to provide evidence of the presence of 
artificial phronesis, it must see growth in both knowledge, empathy, and confident behavior that results in a more 
flourishing relationship between the caregiver and the patient. While it may take setting a table as a simple task, 
when dealing with those suffering from dementia this is not a simple situation. “People living with dementia are 
at risk of eating and drinking problems of various kinds, including dysphagia22, difficulty recognizing food and 
drink23, and problems using cutlery” (Social Care Institute for Excellence)24. Proper care for these patients is a 
skill that needs to be developed both in care facility workers and for those caring for family members at home.
Statistical evaluations
The Student t-test25 was used as statistical test for analyzing collected data from questionnaire. This kind of 
evaluation determines if there is a significant difference between the means of two independent groups (the 
control group and the experimental group), aligning directly with the presented research investigation. This 
t-test assumes that the observed data are approximately normally distributed, i.e. the data in a group follows 
a specific bell-shaped curve, also known as the Gaussian distribution, which is symmetrical around its mean.
Skewness and kurtosis are indicators useful for determining whether a dataset approximates a normal 
distribution and are helpful for assessing how the dataset deviates from normality. Kurtosis being close to zero 
and skewness being in the range [−2, 2]suggest approximate normality, but they are not sufficient for verifying 
normality conclusively, especially for small sample sizes, as deviations might occur by chance. For a robust check, 
the Shapiro-Wilk normality tests26 is used. Such a test evaluates whether a given dataset is normally distributed 
by comparing the data’s sample distribution to a perfectly normal distribution. It provides a W statistic, with 
lower values indicating deviations from normality. A p-value p determines whether these deviations are 
statistically significant, typically using a threshold of p < 0.05 to reject the null hypothesis of normality. The 
null hypothesis H0 is a fundamental concept in statistics that represents the default assumption that there is no

A game session: interactive acts and rules
The game was a turn-based game, and participant and robot can only take an action when it is their respective 
turn. An interactive act consists of completion of the sequence of two active turns, one for the participant and 
one for the robot.
Fig. 2 represents one of these interactions. The participant takes an action on the tablet and their turn ends. 
The robot detects such an action and gives a feedback to the participant. For example, if the participant placed 
the knife, the robot could suggest that such an utensil should not be made available to a person suffering from 
Alzheimer’s. After producing feedback, the robot’s turn becomes active and the robot can move an object for 
setting up the seat reserved to it. The end of the robot’s turn leads to the end of an interactive act. A new 
interactive act starts, and the participant’s turn returns active.
The robot’s feedback in an interactive act is the key that should influence the next action of the participant 
in the next interactive act. Pepper produced either positive feedback and comment feedback. A positive feedback 
is a confirmation about the correctness of participant’s action, while a comment feedback is an observation 
about that action. During an interactive act, the robot produced both a positive and a comment feedback, or 
just one positive feedback, when the participant’s action was appropriate for the patient, weather it produced 
just a comment feedback when participant’s action was not appropriate (for example, if the participant placed 
the knife).
Participants in the experimental group heard the robot’s thoughts before the feedback, while the participants 
in the control group heard just the feedback. Structures of robot’s feedback and thoughts are detailed in the next 
subsection.
The participant was made aware of the rules of the game by the instructor before starting it. The rules of the 
game were the following: 
	1.	 The participant moves first
	2.	 The participant has to set up the table for the placeholder number 3
	3.	 The robot has to set up the table for the placeholder number 1
	4.	 The participant can move objects just from the utensil holder reserved to them
	5.	 The robot and the participant have to follow the turns, that is they alternate in the execution of an action, and 
the participant can move another object just after the robot placed an object
	6.	 When their turn is active, the participant can perform one of the following actions:
•	 take an utensil that has never been taken before, and moving it to the table
•	 modify the position of an utensil already placed on the table moving it to a different location
•	 remove an utensil from the table, and re-positioning it on the crockery holder
•	 stop the game

Outline of robot’s feedback and inner speech cognitive architecture
In order to present the same stimuli and to detect differences between experimental and control groups, the 
robot’s speech was set prior to the experiments. In particular, the sentences produced as feedback by the robot 
were in form of outer speech, whereas the robot’s thoughts were in form of inner speech. Table 1 summarizes the 
differences between feedback and thoughts.
During the interactive act, after participant’s turn, the robot produced a sentence in the form of outer speech, 
preceded by inner speech only in the experimental condition. As mentioned, outer speech was both positive and 
comment feedback, or just positive feedback, when the action by participant was appropriate for the patient, 
while it was just a comment feedback when the action was non appropriate.
Parameters regulating the speech production by robot (i.e., speed, tone, double voice effect) were set 
differently for outer and inner speech, with the aim to attribute mentalized effects to inner statements, as if they 
were thoughts. Moreover, robot’s LEDs, that are in the eyes and in the shoulders of the robot, were rainbow 
colored when the robot thought aloud, while they were set to the standard white color when the robot produced 
outer statements. The robot did not have gestures during inner speech, while it used animated speech when 
talking to the participants. Participants did not know a priori these differences, but they heard and saw them.
Outer speech had the formal language form that is expected by an artificial agent, and used the typical 
routines’ parameters of Pepper robot: on the contrary, inner speech tried to trace a human-based language, 
since it expressed robot values, personal judgments and comments on participant’s actions using a friendly and 
sometimes condensed form.
The sentences produced by the robot were produced by the cognitive architecture of inner speech proposed 
by some of authors12. It is a three-tier architecture, including the sensory-motor, working memory and knowledge 
modules. In the experiments, the architecture ran on the central executive. It works as follow: first level received 
input events representing the participant’s action, and encoded them by associating symbolic information. 
Generally, this process is the output of typical routines that detect events from the environment (as speech-
to-text routines which encode audio in sequences of words, or neural networks for image classification, which 
extract the content of an image and associates to each entity the corresponding label): in this experimental setup, 
this process was simply the event on the tablet surface, next encoded by a symbol representing uniquely such 
an event.

Once the working memory received a symbol representing an event, it recalled from the knowledge module, 
that is a semantic net, the concept corresponding to that symbol, and other concepts directly linked to it in 
the net. These concepts are in turn encoded by the working memory, as they were perceived for the first time, 
and were processed as new symbols. This process simulates the rehearsal loop: recalled concepts are processed 
one at a time, and for each of them the described process is repeated until no further concepts emerge from 
the knowledge module. Inner speech is that rehearsal loop. The recall of related concepts from the knowledge 
module, the production of these concepts and their rehearsal, is the equivalent of a robot’s thought. During the 
loop, the robot vocally reproduces the involved concepts, simulating the “thinking out loud”.
In order to enable the observations in the same conditions for each participant, the inner and outer dialogue 
of the robot had to involve the same sentences for the same events. In this way, the variables and parameters to 
observe are the same for all participants, and the evaluations can be compared for abstracting a general inner 
speech affection on the interaction. For this reason, the knowledge module in the proposed experiments, is a 
sort of dictionary: it defined the correspondences for each symbol representing an event on the tablet surface (or 
representing a recalled statement) with the new statement to produce. In the original version of the inner speech 
cognitive architecture, the concepts of the semantic net are arranged in new sentence to produce, that requires 
parsing methods for composing syntactically and semantically correct structure. In this experiment, the fixed 
sentences corresponding to the events and to a new next sentences to produce were fixed, and disambiguation 
and composition strategies were not necessary.
For example, let us suppose that in an interactive act, the participant (named Mario) placed another glass on 
the table for wine, that is a not appropriate action for the patient. The robot perceives that event, and the working 
memory associates the symbol that univocally identifies it. It recalls from the knowledge module the first 
sentence of the inner dialogue, and it perceives this sentences as it was a new event, and encodes it by associating 
another symbol that identifies such an emerged sentence. The loop starts, by recalling other sentences associated 
to the new perceived symbol, and the robot’s comment feedback (there are not positive feedback because the 
action is not appropriate) looks like: (T stays for thought - in italic, F for outer feedback - not in italic)
T1: “Mario does not know that the resident shouldn’t drink wine.”
T2: “Moreover, there are two glasses on the table.”
T3: “It could cause disturbance.”
F: “Mario, the patient shouldn’t drink wine, and there are too many glasses on table.”
When the robot ran in traditional way, the inner sentences representing the thoughts were suppressed, and 
the robot produced just the outer one.
Results
The analysis of the questionnaire, and of pictures related to the final configuration pursued by each participant, 
enables to make useful observations about robot’s inner speech effects on human-robot interaction, in particular 
related to participants’ awareness of suffering people’s needs.
All the participants declared to do not have experiences and previous skills with dementia. None of them 
had knowledge about how to prepare the table for the suffering person. This data was very important because it 
defined that all participants contributed with the same valence to the next observations. It influenced the choice 
of the test used for interpreting gathered data, as next shown. The observed effects are summarized as follow:
•	 effect on awareness of the choice - how participants are aware about the correctness of their choices in an 
interactive act. This effect is analyzed to validate the hypothesis H1.
•	 effect on empathy - level of sensitivity towards the vulnerable person. This effect is analyzed to validate the 
hypothesis H2.
•	 effect on final configuration - begin compliant to the expected final schema of the table for a patient suffering 
of Alzheimer. This effect is analyzed to validate the hypothesis H3.
Validating H1: effect on awareness of the choice
The robot’s inner voice influenced the ethical choices of the participants, making them more confident in 
respecting the person’s needs. This effect emerges by considering how many times participants changed the 
location of an already correctly placed utensil, leading to the risk of not being compliant with the final scheme 
and with the individual’s needs. Moreover, the Likert’s value provided by the participant to the specific question 
investigating how many times they changed positions of objects in correct locations (1 indicates that the 
participant has never been changed, 5 indicates that they have always been changed idea), enabled to reinforce 
this evaluation.
By counting the number of changed locations of correctly placed objects, and the Likert distribution over 
the groups, it emerged that participants in experimental group tended to modify the location of correctly 
placed objects fewer times than the control group, showing that robot’s inner voice makes the participant more 
confident about the correct locations of correct objects. In other words, participants in the experimental group 
learn more robustly than the participants in control group how to face the patient’s needs. The robot’s inner voice 
reinforces the awareness of correct choice, fostering the practical wisdom to meet the needs and care of a person.
The counters of changed locations from analyzed videos show that participants in the control group modified 
location for a correct objects in correct locations about 47.8% more than experimental one (changed correct 
locations le = 23, lc = 12).

Conclusions and future works
The proposed study provided valuable insights into the effects of a robot’s inner speech in fostering users’ 
phronesis during a collaborative caregiving task for a person with dementia. The task involved a social game 
where participants collaborated with the robot to prepare a table setting specifically for an individual suffering 
from Alzheimer’s.
The results of the experiments demonstrated that the robot’s internal dialogue effectively stimulated phronesis 
among participants, even those without prior skills or experience related to the caregiving task. By engaging 
with the robot’s reasoning, users showed improved awareness of appropriate choices for preparing the table 
and exhibited increased empathy toward the patient’s needs. These findings highlight the potential of robotic 
dialogue not only to enhance decision-making processes but also to promote empathetic responses in human 
interactions, particularly in caregiving contexts for individuals with cognitive impairments like dementia.
The study suggests that the robot’s inner speech plays a key role in these outcomes by encouraging participants 
to reflect on alternative perspectives and their decision-making processes. Listening to the robot’s reasoning 
appeared to heighten users’ sensitivity to the nuanced considerations involved in caregiving, fostering a deeper 
awareness of appropriate actions. Moreover, the robot’s simulated thoughts seemed to humanize the experience, 
eliciting empathy for the patient and a greater connection to their challenges.
Interacting with the robot also created a non-judgmental environment where participants could express 
thoughts and concerns freely, fostering a reflective and compassionate approach to caregiving tasks. This 
combination of cognitive engagement, perspective-taking, and emotional connection facilitated by the robot 
underscores its potential as a tool for enhancing caregiving practices.
While the study yielded promising results, some limitations and opportunities for future research should 
be considered. For instance, broadening the participant sample to include a more diverse range of ages could 
provide deeper insights into how different demographics respond to robotic inner speech in caregiving scenarios.

Additionally, exploring variations in robot types, task settings, and interaction modalities might reveal how

Future research could also investigate tasks with higher complexity or competitive elements, such as virtual

their awareness and problem-solving skills.
Finally, this study represents a novel attempt to explore how a robot with rudimentary inner speech can 
stimulate sensitivity and empathy toward individuals with dementia. Expanding this line of research could 
contribute to the development of more sophisticated social robots capable of improving caregiving quality and 
well-being in healthcare contexts.
