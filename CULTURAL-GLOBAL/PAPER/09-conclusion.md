# 9. Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that AI values alignment research suffers from a pronounced WEIRD bias, mirroring the pattern that Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010) identified in psychology more than fifteen years ago. Across 2,329 papers and 34 normative dimensions, we find that the academic literature informing AI governance frameworks is produced overwhelmingly by researchers at institutions in North America (39.5%) and Western Europe (20.6%), with a mean WEIRD score of 64.5/100 and nearly 60% of papers classified as High-WEIRD.

The consequences are both epistemic and normative. Epistemically, the field's understanding of how AI systems interact with human values is informed by a narrow set of philosophical traditions---principally utilitarian and deontological ethics---while non-Western normative frameworks that inform the moral reasoning of the global majority remain marginal. Only 6.9% of papers (161 of 2,329) engage any non-Western normative framework at the detection threshold, and precision-adjusted estimates suggest genuine philosophical engagement in approximately 95 papers (4.1%). Buen vivir (8 raw detections, adj. ~1), Dharmic ethics (18 raw, adj. ~4), and Buddhist ethics (22 raw, adj. ~7) are nearly invisible.

Normatively, the bias undermines the legitimacy of AI governance frameworks deployed globally. When "human values" alignment is operationalized through frameworks developed by and for WEIRD populations, the resulting systems embed particular cultural assumptions as universal standards. This is not merely an academic concern: it has material consequences for the 85% of the world's population whose values may be inadequately represented.

At the same time, our findings reveal reasons for cautious optimism. Distinct regional ethical fingerprints demonstrate that non-WEIRD normative traditions *are* being articulated in the AI ethics literature, even if at low volumes: Ubuntu philosophy in Sub-Saharan Africa (mean score 24.6), Confucian ethics in East Asia (3.5), Islamic ethics in the MENA region (3.4), and Indigenous relational ethics in Oceania (12.7) and Latin America (6.0). Non-Western framework engagement has grown from 3.7% (2019) to 8.2% (2026), and regional diversity in the author pool has expanded from 7 regions to 11 over the same period.

The path forward requires action on multiple fronts:

1. **Targeted expansion** of AI ethics research capacity in the most underrepresented regions---Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, South Asia---through dedicated funding, institutional partnerships, and multilingual publication support.

2. **Substantive philosophical work** to translate non-Western normative frameworks into concrete AI design principles, going beyond surface-level citation to genuine conceptual engagement with Ubuntu communalism, Confucian role ethics, Islamic jurisprudential reasoning, Buddhist compassion-centered ethics, Dharmic duty frameworks, Indigenous relational ontologies, and *buen vivir* cosmovisions.

3. **Structural reform** of AI governance processes to ensure that normative framework diversity is not merely acknowledged but institutionalized---through pluralistic value elicitation, culturally adapted deliberation mechanisms, and community-level governance authority.

4. **Continued empirical monitoring** of the field's normative diversity, tracking the metrics established in this study to measure progress toward genuine pluralism in AI values alignment research.

The WEIRD problem in psychology catalyzed a decade of reform in research practices, sampling strategies, and theoretical frameworks. AI ethics is at a similar inflection point. The question is whether the field will undertake the difficult work of genuine pluralism---engaging non-Western moral traditions not as exotic supplements but as coequal sources of normative authority---or whether it will continue to export a particular cultural perspective as a universal standard. The stakes, given the global reach of AI systems, could not be higher.

## Future Work

Several extensions of this analysis are planned or underway:

1. **Expanded corpus.** A targeted literature search across 12+ databases aims to add 200+ papers from underrepresented regions, with particular focus on non-English-language scholarship.

2. **Positionality analysis.** A deeper examination of the "who studies whom" question, cross-tabulating author region with study population region to quantify endogenous vs. exogenous research on each normative tradition.

3. **Qualitative depth.** Close reading of the highest-scoring papers in each non-Western framework to assess whether quantitative keyword-based detection corresponds to substantive philosophical engagement.

4. **Policy impact tracing.** Mapping the citation pathways through which AI ethics scholarship influences governance documents (e.g., EU AI Act, UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics, African Union AI Strategy) to assess whether normative diversity in the literature translates into normative diversity in policy.

5. **Dashboard and visualization.** An interactive dashboard allowing researchers to explore regional ethical fingerprints, WEIRD score distributions, and framework engagement patterns across the full corpus.
