## Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) is one of the most spoken-about topics in the media, academia, government and other platforms. One of the aspects that is often discussed is the ethical implications of AI and approaches to mitigate the risks. Artificial intelligence has an undeniable impact on industries as well as socio-economic structures; however, this article focusses on the impact of AI on three concerns mainly, humanity, spirituality and the environment. This article is an interdisciplinary study of African theological ethics and the philosophy of technology. It discusses the theological implications (doctrinal issues) of emerging technologies, particularly AI. It discusses technology as power which has impacted Africa since the first industrial revolution and emphasises the importance of African ethics in the context of AI in Africa. This article critically discusses ubuntu ethics and its critique. It focuses on AI and its impact on humanity, spirituality and the environment, and proposes a holistic ubuntu AI ethics approach in South Africa. Intradisciplinary and/or interdisciplinary implications: This article is an interdisciplinary study of African theological ethics and the philosophy of technology. Ubuntu ethics in this article derives from African Theology and African Philosophy. Ubuntu AI ethics is important for various disciplines such as theology, law, social sciences, computer sciences and information technology (especially designers and developers).

## Detailed Summary

This article proposes a holistic ubuntu artificial intelligence ethics framework for South Africa, examining AI's impact through three interconnected dimensions: humanity, spirituality, and the environment. The author, Katleho Mokoena from the University of Pretoria, approaches AI ethics as an interdisciplinary study bridging African theological ethics and philosophy of technology. The paper argues that current AI ethics discourse is dominated by Western epistemologies and that African contexts require distinctly African ethical frameworks rooted in indigenous values.

The article situates AI within the historical context of technology as power in Africa, tracing how previous industrial revolutions entrenched Western hegemony through the transatlantic slave trade, colonization, and globalization. The author draws on concepts of "data colonialism" and "algorithmic coloniality" to argue that AI developed in the Global North perpetuates these power dynamics through biased algorithms, facial recognition systems that disproportionately target people of color, and AI-driven resource allocation that may discriminate against African contexts. This historical awareness frames ubuntu ethics not merely as cultural preference but as a necessary counterbalance to technological colonialism.

The proposed holistic ubuntu ethics approach has three components: (1) Humanity - emphasizing relational identity and human dignity rooted in the maxim "I am because we are"; (2) Spirituality - incorporating relationships between the Supreme Being, the living, and ancestors (the living dead); (3) Environment - recognizing humans as part of (not masters over) the land, sea, sky, nature, and animals. Importantly, the author argues ubuntu is not anthropocentric despite its focus on humaneness - it recognizes the ontological interconnection between human life and ecological systems. The paper engages with critiques of ubuntu (as indigenous, utopian, vague) while maintaining that ubuntu is a dynamic process of becoming more humane rather than a fixed doctrine.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs interdisciplinary conceptual analysis combining African theological ethics with philosophy of technology. The author draws on African philosophical literature (Ramose, LenkaBula, Metz), post-colonial technology studies (Benjamin, Mohamed et al., Zembylas), and Christian systematic theology to construct the ubuntu AI ethics framework. South Africa's Presidential Commission on the Fourth Industrial Revolution (PC4IR) recommendations serve as a policy anchor. The analysis examines how existing AI ethics approaches (predominantly Western) fail to address distinctly African concerns and proposes ubuntu as a contextually appropriate alternative.

## Results Summary

The analysis identifies several key findings: (1) Current global AI ethics frameworks exclude African perspectives and indigenous knowledge systems; (2) Radical human enhancement poses challenges to Christian doctrines of salvation, humanity (imago Dei), and eschatology; (3) AI risks perpetuating colonial power dynamics through data extraction, biased algorithms, and imposed Western epistemologies; (4) Ubuntu offers a relational ethics that extends beyond individual rights to community interconnectedness; (5) Ubuntu must be understood as non-anthropocentric, recognizing the inseparability of human and ecological life; (6) South Africa's PC4IR mentions ethics but lacks epistemological grounding that ubuntu can provide.

## Conclusions

The paper concludes that a holistic ubuntu AI ethics approach is essential for mitigating AI risks in South African and broader African contexts. This framework must address humanity (relational dignity), spirituality (including traditional African spiritual worldviews), and environment (ecological interconnection) as integrated rather than separate concerns. The author calls for AI designers, developers, and policymakers to engage with ubuntu ethics, arguing that technical solutions alone cannot address the fundamentally relational and contextual nature of AI's ethical challenges in Africa. Ubuntu's emphasis on process over achievement ("becoming more humane") provides a dynamic framework adaptable to rapidly evolving AI technologies.
