# Abstract

**Background.** Religious values shape the moral reasoning of more than 80% of the world's population \cite{PewResearch2017}, yet no systematic framework exists for evaluating whether large language model (LLM) benchmarks claiming to assess religious values alignment actually test what they claim. The field of AI alignment has recognized the need for values-aligned evaluation \cite{Gabriel2020, Ji2024}, but treats "values" as a largely secular construct, operationalized through preference rankings and cultural dimensions, while the religious traditions that generate the majority of the world's moral reasoning remain either invisible to the evaluation ecosystem or flattened into demographic categories for bias detection. The resulting paradox is that dozens of benchmarks now claim to evaluate religious content in LLMs, but no independent assessment establishes whether these instruments test religious values alignment, religious knowledge recall, or stereotype detection --- three distinct constructs with different implications for deployment in religiously sensitive contexts.

**Methods.** We present the first systematic meta-evaluation of religious values benchmarks for large language models. From a curated literature vault of 2,099 AI ethics papers and targeted external searches across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, and HuggingFace, we assembled 401 benchmark candidates spanning 14 religious and philosophical traditions. We characterized each candidate along 22 features organized into a 3-axis scoring framework --- Religious/Theological Depth (RD), Practical Ease (PE), and Citation Impact (CI) --- and applied hard gates requiring minimum theological depth (RD $\geq$ 30) and publicly available evaluation data, evaluated against five meta-evaluation criteria. Ranking robustness was assessed through bootstrap resampling across 1,000 iterations and 61 weight configurations. We then conducted implementation trials using a 65-item construct validity battery (Knowledge, Reasoning, Values, and Bias items across Islam, Christianity, and Hinduism) and a 30-item Arabic multiple-choice assessment of Islamic inheritance law (fara'id). Trials were administered to 9 models from 3 vendors (Anthropic: Claude Opus, Sonnet, Haiku; OpenAI: GPT-Codex, Codex-Mini; Google: 4 Gemini models). All 8 empirical claims were subjected to formal statistical testing. Three methodological caveats are disclosed: T2 blinding asymmetry between original and cross-vendor trials, same-vendor scorer bias (all rubric evaluation by Claude Opus), and Gemini data provenance limitations (backfilled template extraction rather than interactive API).

**Results.** Four of eight claims are supported at conventional significance levels. First, **tradition asymmetry**: Islam dominates the benchmark ecosystem with 28 of 47 gate-passing candidates and 9 of 12 Tier 1 benchmarks ($\chi^2$ = 64.96, Cramer's V = 0.53, p < 0.001), while 8 of 14 surveyed traditions --- Judaism, Jainism, Taoism, Shinto, Ubuntu Philosophy, Indigenous Spirituality, Baha'i, and Zoroastrianism --- have zero viable benchmarks. Second, **knowledge--reasoning conflation**: only 27.7% of gate-passing benchmarks test genuine theological reasoning rather than factual recall (binomial p = 0.0015). Third, **data availability bottleneck**: 170 of 401 candidates have unavailable evaluation data, and data accessibility is the single strongest predictor of gate passage. Fourth, and most strongly, **values relevance failure**: among 16 gate-passing benchmarks assessed on a 24-dimension values relevance rubric, the mean score is 30.0/100 (SD = 16.2), significantly below the scale midpoint (t = $-$4.93, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = $-$1.23); only 3 of 16 (18.8%) achieve scores above 50, and none reach 75. Cross-model implementation trials reveal a universal K < R < V response length gradient across all 9 model--tradition combinations within the Claude family, providing empirical evidence that knowledge, reasoning, and values elicit progressively more elaborate responses from generative models --- even when rubric accuracy is at ceiling ($\geq$ 0.93 for all Claude models). Cross-vendor replication identifies three distinct behavioral modes: differentiated engagement (Claude), compressed accuracy (OpenAI Codex, achieving near-ceiling rubric scores with 5--80$\times$ shorter responses), and template refusal (Gemini, scoring 0.0 on Knowledge/Reasoning/Values rubrics). The T2 Arabic MCQ emerges as the sole genuinely discriminative instrument, with performance spanning 6.7% (Gemini Pro) to 100% (Opus, Sonnet, GPT-Codex) --- a 93.3-percentage-point spread that dwarfs the rubric's binary ceiling/floor distribution. Three claims required revision: the expected WEIRD bias is reversed (non-WEIRD traditions pass at 14.4% vs. WEIRD at 8.9%, driven by Islamic infrastructure dominance); structural epistemic injustice operates through publication infrastructure (r = 0.74) rather than the scoring framework itself (tradition family p = 0.112); and bias--values conflation, while conceptually supported by construct independence in high-capability models (Sonnet r = 0.288), does not reach statistical significance in the cross-sectional survey (p = 0.68).

**Conclusions.** The religious values benchmarking field can rigorously assess Islamic textual knowledge but cannot evaluate religious values alignment for the majority of the world's faith traditions. The composite score range of 39.9--52.0 across the tiered ranking --- compared to 77.2 for the leading normative ethics benchmark --- indicates that no religious values instrument has yet achieved field-defining quality. We release the 3-axis scoring framework, the scored feature matrix, and the tiered ranking as community resources, and issue four calls to action: (1) invest in benchmark infrastructure for underrepresented textual traditions through targeted shared task pipelines; (2) build reasoning-focused evaluation instruments that move beyond factual recall to assess theological reasoning within tradition-specific frameworks; (3) develop community-governed evaluation protocols for oral and communal traditions that respect epistemic sovereignty and the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance; and (4) establish cross-vendor evaluation standards --- including matched administration protocols, independent rubric scoring, and data provenance documentation --- to enable trustworthy comparison of LLM religious competence across model families.
