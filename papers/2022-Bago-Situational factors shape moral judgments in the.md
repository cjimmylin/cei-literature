## Abstract

Much research on moral judgment is centered on moral dilemmas in which deontological perspectives (i.e., emphasizing rules, individual rights and duties) are in conflict with utilitarian judgements (i.e., following the greater good defined through consequences). A central finding of this field Greene et al. showed that psychological and situational factors (e.g., the intent of the agent, or physical contact between the agent and the victim) play an important role in people's use of deontological versus utilitarian considerations when making moral decisions. As their study was conducted with US samples, our knowledge is limited concerning the universality of this effect, in general, and the impact of culture on the situational and psychological factors of moral judgments, in particular. Here, we empirically test the universality of deontological and utilitarian judgments by replicating Greene et al.'s experiments on a large (N = X,XXX) and diverse (WEIRD and non-WEIRD) sample across the world to explore the influence of culture on moral judgment. The relevance of this exploration to a broad range of policy-making problems is discussed.

## Detailed Summary

This large-scale cross-cultural study replicated Greene et al.'s seminal experiments on moral dilemma judgments (the trolley problem) across 45 countries with 27,502 participants. The research tested whether the effects of personal force and intention on moral judgments are culturally universal. Moral dilemmas were presented as conflicts between deontological perspectives (emphasizing rules and individual rights) and utilitarian perspectives (maximizing overall good), with participants rating the moral acceptability of sacrificing one person to save multiple lives.

The study found strong evidence that the personal force effect (judging actions as less morally acceptable when physical force must be applied) is culturally universal across Western, Eastern, and Southern cultural clusters. However, the interaction between personal force and intention showed more mixed results: while the Western cluster fully replicated Greene's findings, the evidence was inconclusive for the Eastern cluster and context-dependent for the Southern cluster. Contrary to expectations, no strong association was found between collectivism/individualism and moral dilemma judgments.

The research highlights important methodological considerations, including high exclusion rates (81%) due to strict criteria and the surprising finding that participants familiar with trolley problems showed larger, not smaller, effect sizes. The resulting database provides opportunities for future research on cultural moderators of moral judgment.

## Methods Summary

The study employed a cross-cultural replication design through the Psychological Science Accelerator network. Data were collected from 27,502 participants across 45 countries, with participants completing moral dilemmas in their native language. The design included trolley and speedboat dilemma variants, each with manipulations of personal force (whether physical contact was required) and intention (whether harm was a means or side-effect). Participants provided both binary choices and 9-point Likert scale ratings of moral acceptability. Additional measures included the Oxford Utilitarianism Scale, Individualism-Collectivism Scale, and demographic variables. Bayesian analyses with pre-registered priors were used to test hypotheses, with robustness regions computed to assess sensitivity to prior selection.

## Results Summary

- Personal Force Effect: Strong evidence (BF > 10) found in all three cultural clusters (Western, Eastern, Southern), supporting cultural universality
- Interaction Effect: Strong evidence in Western cluster; inconclusive in Eastern and Southern clusters
- Collectivism: No strong evidence for the hypothesized three-way interaction with collectivism/individualism
- Exploratory Findings: Without exclusion criteria, some moderate evidence emerged for collectivism effects
- Effect Sizes: Smaller on speedboat vs. trolley dilemmas; larger when including familiar participants

## Conclusions

The study provides evidence that the personal force effect on moral judgments is likely culturally universal, suggesting basic cognitive or emotional processes that are independent of culture. The interaction between personal force and intention was robustly replicated in Western samples but showed mixed results in other cultural clusters, potentially due to sample size limitations or genuine cultural variation. The lack of strong association with collectivism/individualism challenges previous theoretical assumptions. The findings have implications for understanding the psychological underpinnings of moral judgments and their application to policy decisions, including autonomous vehicle ethics.

## Data Description

This is a large-scale cross-cultural psychology study replicating Greene et al.'s trolley problem experiments across 45 countries with participants from all inhabited continents. It empirically tests the universality of the effects of personal force and intention on moral dilemma judgments (deontological vs. utilitarian). The study found that personal force effects are culturally universal. No LLMs were tested; this is a human participant study.
