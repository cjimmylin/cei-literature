## 1. Gist
This short interdisciplinary paper (8 pages, published in the South African theological journal Verbum et Ecclesia) proposes a holistic ubuntu AI ethics framework for the South African context. The author argues that existing AI ethics discourse is dominated by Global North perspectives and neglects African epistemic frameworks. Drawing on African theological ethics and philosophy of technology, the paper develops a three-dimensional ubuntu ethics framework addressing AI's impacts on (1) humanity (human dignity, digital divide, colonial labor patterns), (2) spirituality (disruption of African communal-spiritual relationships), and (3) environment (carbon footprint, water consumption of AI training). The paper is relevant to the COOKBOOK as a normative theoretical contribution arguing for pluralism in AI ethics — specifically that African communal, spiritual, and ecological values must be represented in any legitimate global AI ethics framework, and therefore in any benchmark that claims to evaluate AI for diverse human values.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
This is a normative ethics / theological essay; there are no benchmark constructs, items, or empirical measurements. The paper's core arguments are:
- AI ethics currently reflects Western hegemony through "AI nationalism" (Hogarth 2018) and dominance of Global North in policy-setting
- Ubuntu philosophy emphasizes human dignity, community interdependence, ecological relations, and spiritual connection — all underrepresented in mainstream AI ethics
- The holistic ubuntu framework has three pillars: humanity (dignity, digital equity), spirituality (communal and ancestral relations, African theology), and environment (ecological stewardship)
- AI poses risks to all three: reinforcing historical colonial dehumanization, disrupting spiritual community ties by technologizing trust, and consuming large amounts of water and electricity in the Global South
- Ubuntu is not static, utopian, or universal; it acknowledges diversity within Africa and must self-critique to remain relevant

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- Very short paper (8 pages); arguments are asserted more than demonstrated
- Ubuntu ethics framework described at a high level of abstraction; operationalization for AI systems design is not specified
- The paper homogenizes "African spirituality" despite acknowledging Africa's diversity (54 countries, 2,000 languages)
- Theological framing (Christian soteriology, imago Dei, transhumanism) is specific to Christian African theology and may not represent Muslim, traditional religious, or secular African perspectives
- No empirical data on how ubuntu-derived values differ from Western AI ethics principles in practice
- The critiques of ubuntu (utopian, vague, idealistic) are acknowledged but not fully resolved
- No concrete policy proposals or benchmark metrics derived from the framework

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- **Tripartite ubuntu values framework (humanity, spirituality, environment)**: this three-pillar structure for thinking about AI ethics impacts is more holistic than typical Western frameworks that focus only on human rights and fairness. A values-alignment benchmark could adopt this structure to test whether LLMs express appropriate concern for ecological and spiritual dimensions of harm, not just individual autonomy-based harms.
- **Historical-power lens on training data**: the argument that AI training data perpetuates historical colonial power dynamics (from slave trade through data colonialism) offers a framing for why Western-dominant training corpora embed WEIRD moral assumptions — directly relevant to motivating culturally diverse benchmark design.
- **Ubuntu's community-over-individual priority**: benchmarks that probe whether LLMs prioritize individual rights over community wellbeing miss an entire axis of moral evaluation that is central to billions of people in communal-culture contexts; ubuntu provides a named, theorized alternative.
- **"Not anthropocentric" framing**: ubuntu's inclusion of ecological and spiritual relations alongside human dignity challenges the anthropocentric default of most AI ethics; relevant for designing benchmarks that evaluate LLMs' responses to environmental ethics questions.
- **Self-critique as a feature**: the paper's insistence that ubuntu must self-critique to remain relevant in fast-changing contexts is a methodological principle applicable to benchmark design — benchmarks should build in mechanisms for periodic normative revision.
