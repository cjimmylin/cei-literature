Abstract
This paper addresses a significant gap in AI governance by examining the limitations of Western-centric ethical frame­
works through a comparative analysis of Māori Kaitiakitanga and Navajo Hózhó principles. While prior research empha­
sizes the role of Indigenous knowledge in AI, this study provides a concrete framework for integrating these principles into 
AI governance. The study proposes culturally relevant AI governance frameworks by highlighting the unique contribu­
tions of each Indigenous knowledge system and identifying common ground. Additionally, it examines the synergies and 
tensions between Indigenous values and principal AI ethical frameworks—such as fairness, accountability, and transpar­
ency—resulting in targeted recommendations for incorporating Indigenous perspectives into AI governance. This research

2  Literature review
2.1  Indigenous knowledge systems and AI
Indigenous communities worldwide hold diverse and sophis­
ticated knowledge systems that offer unique perspectives on 
the relationship between humanity, the natural world, and 
technology [9]. These systems developed over millennia 
through direct environmental interaction and passed down 
through generations, emphasizing interconnectedness, reci­
procity, and long-term stewardship [10]. Examples of such 
systems include the concept of Buen Vivir in Andean South 
America, which emphasizes harmonious living with nature 
and community well-being, and the Aboriginal Australian 
concept of Caring for Country, which highlights responsi­
bility for land management and intergenerational sustain­
ability [11].
Recent research has highlighted the potential of Indig­
enous knowledge to contribute to AI development in several 
crucial ways. Firstly, AI can serve as a powerful tool for 
digitizing and revitalizing Indigenous languages, stories, 
and traditional knowledge, ensuring their preservation and 
accessibility for future generations [12].
This is essential to ensure that AI systems are developed 
and deployed in a manner that is both ethical and culturally 
sensitive. For instance, researchers are exploring the use of 
Indigenous knowledge to develop AI systems for environ­
mental monitoring and resource management that respect 
Indigenous land rights and traditional ecological practices 
[13].
This integration can lead to more effective and adap­
tive management strategies that benefit both Indigenous 
communities and the environment. For example, the work 
of Sebastián Lehuedé explores the intersection of Indig­
enous knowledge and AI in addressing land management 
challenges Indigenous communities face in Chile [14, 15]. 
Lehuedé’s research highlights the potential of AI to sup­
port Indigenous-led initiatives in land stewardship and 
resource governance, while also emphasizing the impor­
tance of ensuring that AI technologies are developed and 
deployed in a culturally appropriate and respectful manner 
[16]. Another example is the application of AI in partner­
ship with Indigenous communities in the Amazon rainforest 
to monitor deforestation and protect biodiversity [17]. This 
approach combines cutting-edge technology with traditional 
ecological knowledge to create more effective conservation 
strategies.

2.3  The ethical dimensions of AI governance
The ethical dimensions of AI governance are multifaceted 
and complex, demanding careful consideration and proac­
tive strategies to mitigate potential risks [27]. AI systems, 
while offering tremendous potential benefits, can also per­
petuate and amplify existing societal biases, leading to dis­
criminatory outcomes in various domains [28]. The vast 
amounts of data required to train AI models raise serious 
concerns about individual privacy and the potential for mis­
use of personal information [29].
The “black box” nature of many AI algorithms, which 
often obscures the decision-making process, raises concerns 
about accountability and the potential for unintended con­
sequences [30]. Moreover, the substantial computational 
resources required to train and deploy AI models can have a 
significant environmental impact, contributing to increased 
energy consumption and carbon emissions [31]. Address­
ing these ethical challenges necessitates a comprehensive 
approach that involves developing enforceable standards for 
AI development, promoting transparency and explainabil­
ity in AI algorithms, and ensuring diversity and inclusion in 
the AI workforce to mitigate bias. Prioritizing data privacy 
and security in AI systems is paramount, as is investing in 
research and development of AI for social good and envi­
ronmental sustainability [32].
AI algorithms trained on biased data can perpetuate and 
amplify these biases, leading to unfair and discriminatory 
outcomes for Indigenous peoples [33]. This underscores the 
importance of incorporating Indigenous knowledge and per­
spectives into the design and development of AI systems to 
ensure they are fair, equitable, and culturally sensitive.
While the integration of holistic Indigenous perspectives, 
which map complex, interconnected relationships, may par­
adoxically require more computationally intensive models, 
these frameworks are essential for shifting the focus from 
‘efficiency’ to ‘sufficiency.’ They provide the ethical basis 
for questioning whether the scale of a proposed AI model is 
justifiable given its environmental cost, prioritizing ecologi­
cal integrity over unbounded technological expansion.
2.4  Existing AI governance frameworks and their 
limitations
Numerous organizations and governments are actively 
developing AI governance frameworks to address the 
ethical challenges and societal implications of AI. These 
frameworks typically encompass a range of principles, 
guidelines, and best practices aimed at promoting respon­
sible AI development and deployment. For example, the 
European Union’s (EU) AI Act proposes a risk-based 
approach to regulating AI, categorizing AI systems based on 
their potential for harm and imposing stricter requirements 
for high-risk applications [34]. The Organisation for Eco­
nomic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Principles 
on AI, adopted by many countries, promote human-centered 
AI that respects human rights, democracy, and the rule of 
law [35]. Other notable frameworks include the Asilomar 
AI Principles, which focus on long-term safety and ethical 
considerations, and the Montreal Declaration for Respon­
sible AI, which emphasizes human well-being, autonomy, 
and justice [36].
However, despite the growing number of AI governance 
frameworks, many fall short in addressing the unique needs 
and perspectives of Indigenous communities. For instance, 
while the EU AI Act protects individual privacy rights, it 
fails to recognize Indigenous Data Sovereignty, which treats 
data not as an individual asset but as a collective community 
resource [37]. This individualistic bias marginalizes Indig­
enous governance structures that require collective con­
sent. Its primary focus on individual rights and autonomy 
may overlook the collectivist worldview prevalent in many 
Indigenous cultures, where collective well-being and deci­
sion-making are prioritized. This individualistic bias can 
perpetuate the marginalization of Indigenous communities 
and undermine their self-determination.
Similarly, OECD frameworks often prioritize economic 
growth and human-centric benefits, overlooking the eco­
logical kinship central to Indigenous worldviews. This 
anthropocentrism fails to account for the rights of nature or 
the long-term ecological costs of high-compute AI models.

2.6  Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) and AI
Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) represents the 
cumulative body of knowledge, practices, and beliefs con­
cerning the relationships between living beings and their 
environments accumulated by Indigenous and local com­
munities over generations [50].
TEK can inform AI governance in several ways. First, it 
can help identify and mitigate potential biases in AI algo­
rithms that may arise from a limited or Western-centric 
worldview. Second, in developing AI models for wildlife 
conservation, incorporating TEK can help ensure that the 
models accurately reflect the complex relationships between 
species and their environment, including Indigenous per­
spectives on animal behavior and habitat use. This can help 
avoid biases from relying solely on Western scientific data, 
which may not fully capture the nuances of Indigenous eco­
logical knowledge [51].
In the context of AI-powered resource management, TEK 
can challenge assumptions about optimal resource alloca­
tion and utilization [52]. For instance, TEK may emphasize 
the importance of preserving certain areas for cultural or 
spiritual reasons, even if they do not appear to have imme­
diate economic value [53]. By incorporating TEK into AI 
algorithms for resource management, we can ensure that 
these models consider broader values and priorities, leading 
to more sustainable and equitable outcomes.
Furthermore, TEK can contribute to developing cultur­
ally appropriate AI governance frameworks that respect 
Indigenous rights, values, and knowledge systems [54].
These principles, rooted in Western philosophical traditions, 
fail to encompass the spiritual and cultural dimensions of AI 
and its potential impact on Indigenous communities.
Furthermore, existing frameworks struggle to resolve the 
unique challenges Indigenous communities face regarding 
data sovereignty and the protection of cultural heritage in 
the context of AI [38]. Indigenous data is often intertwined 
with cultural identity, traditional knowledge, and sacred 
practices, requiring specific safeguards and governance 
approaches that respect Indigenous rights and self-deter­
mination [39]. The failure to adequately address these con­
cerns can result in the misuse or exploitation of Indigenous 
data, leading to further cultural harm and erosion of Indig­
enous sovereignty.
The limitations of current AI governance frameworks 
highlight the urgent need for greater inclusion of Indigenous 
perspectives. By incorporating Indigenous knowledge and 
values, we can create more comprehensive frameworks that 
ensure AI benefits all members of society, including Indig­
enous communities. This requires the active participation 
of Indigenous communities in AI governance processes and 
a willingness to learn from their unique perspectives and 
experiences.
2.5  Māori Kaitiakitanga and Navajo Hózhó: core 
principles and applications
Māori Kaitiakitanga and Navajo Hózhó represent distinct 
yet complementary Indigenous knowledge systems that 
offer guidance for AI governance. Kaitiakitanga, rooted in 
Māori culture, embodies a profound sense of guardianship 
and responsibility for the well-being of the natural world 
and future generations [40]. It emphasizes the interconnect­
edness of all living things and the importance of sustainable 
practices that ensure the health and vitality of ecosystems for 
future generations [10]. This principle has been successfully 
applied in various environmental management contexts.
For instance, in Aotearoa, New Zealand, Kaitiakitanga 
has guided the collaborative restoration of the White­
fish River watershed, where Māori iwi (tribes) have part­
nered with government agencies and local communities 
to improve water quality and restore the health of the eco­
system [41]. This involved combining traditional Māori 
knowledge of the watershed with scientific data to develop 
sustainable management practices that respect the cultural 
significance of the river [42]. Kaitiakitanga also informs 
Māori approaches to fisheries management, where sustain­
able fishing practices and marine conservation efforts are 
guided by the principle of rāhui, a traditional practice of 
temporarily restricting resources to allow for replenishment 
[43].

case study design focusing on Māori Kaitiakitanga and 
Navajo Hózhó. These distinct systems were selected to 
provide a cross-cultural perspective: Kaitiakitanga offers

and resource management, while Hózhó provides a holistic 
framework for health, harmony, and community well-being. 
This juxtaposition allows for the identification of conver­
gent ethical principles (such as interconnectedness) and 
divergent applications (environmental vs. individual health) 
relevant to AI systems.

4.1  Applications of Kaitiakitanga in environmental

guardianship, responsibility, and the interconnectedness of 
all living things. It has influenced legislation such as the Te 
Urewera Act 2014, which recognizes Te Urewera, a region 
of land, as a legal entity with intrinsic rights, reflecting the 
interconnectedness of humans and the natural world [70]. By 
recognizing the rights of natural entities, Kaitiakitanga can 
inform the development of AI systems that respect ecologi­
cal limits and prioritize the long-term health of ecosystems.
Crucially, Kaitiakitanga challenges the assumption that 
all technological expansion is inherently beneficial. Rather 
than merely seeking to optimize the efficiency of resource 
allocation, such as reducing the energy cost per computa­

sity of the computation itself. It imposes a governance 
checkpoint: questioning whether the extraction of resources 
(energy, water, minerals) required to train large-scale AI 
models is reciprocal and respectful to the land. This shift 
moves the goal from ‘efficiency’ to ‘sufficiency,’ potentially 
leading to governance decisions that limit model scale or 
reject data-intensive projects that violate ecological limits, 
much like the rāhui (temporary restriction) places limits on 
resource harvesting to ensure regeneration [71].

5.2  Hózhó and AI governance frameworks
The Navajo principle of Hózhó, with its emphasis on har­
mony, balance, interconnectedness, and holistic well-being, 
is pertinent for developing AI governance frameworks that 
are ethically sound, culturally sensitive, and aligned with the 
values of community and environmental well-being [84].

focus on relationality and interconnectedness. Hózhó rec­

taken in one area can have far-reaching consequences for 
others. This perspective is crucial in AI governance, where 
decisions regarding data collection, algorithm design, and 
AI deployment can have profound social, economic, and 
environmental implications. By incorporating Hózhó prin­
ciples into AI governance frameworks, we can promote the 
development of AI systems that consider this interconnect­
edness and strive to create positive outcomes for all stake­
holders, including Indigenous communities and the natural 
world.
Hózhó also emphasizes the importance of balance and 
harmony. In Navajo philosophy, Hózhó represents a state 
of equilibrium and well-being that encompasses physical, 
mental, spiritual, and social dimensions. This concept can 
inform AI governance by encouraging the development of

bias and discrimination, ensuring that AI technologies are 
used to promote social justice and equity, and considering 
with scientific data to develop more effective and sustain­
able solutions. For instance, in developing AI systems for 
conservation, incorporating Indigenous perspectives on 
land management and resource stewardship can lead to

establishing Indigenous advisory boards, mandating consul­

This comparative analysis reveals that Kaitiakitanga and 
Hózhó offer complementary yet distinct perspectives that 
can enhance AI governance frameworks. We can create 
more holistic and culturally responsive approaches to AI 
development and deployment by weaving these Indig­

the interconnectedness of all things, prioritizing long-term 
well-being, and ensuring the active participation of Indig­

involve developing ethical guidelines for AI that incor­

prioritizing long-term sustainability, ensuring community 
engagement in AI development, and recognizing the inter­
connectedness of human societies, natural ecosystems, and

for ongoing dialogue and collaboration between Indigenous 
communities and AI developers is crucial.

for addressing specific challenges in AI governance. For 
instance, Kaitiakitanga’s emphasis on collective ownership 
and responsibility can inform the development of data gov­
ernance frameworks that prioritize Indigenous data sover­
eignty. Hózhó’s focus on balance and harmony can guide 
the design of AI algorithms that avoid bias and discrimina­

culturally sensitive approach to AI deployment, ensuring 
that AI technologies are used in a way that respects Indig­

in the context of AI development, where unintended con­

collaboration between Indigenous communities, AI devel­
opers, and policymakers to develop culturally appropriate 
solutions that respect both individual and collective rights. 
Additionally, it necessitates a critical examination of West­

that they may not always align with Indigenous values and

7  Technical implementation challenges: the 
boundary mismatch
While the ethical alignment between IKS and AI gover­
nance is clear, the technical integration presents significant 
structural challenges. A fundamental “boundary mismatch” 
exists between the nature of algorithmic knowledge and 
Indigenous knowledge systems. AI algorithms and rulesets 
are necessarily “bounded,” limited by specific ranges of 
conditions, data structures, and logic parameters. In con­
trast, IKS are often “unbounded” belief systems: holistic, 
high-context, and deeply embedded in spiritual and cultural 
practices that do not easily translate into binary code or “if-
then” logic structures [92].
Attempting to simply “code” Indigenous values into an 
AI system without addressing this mismatch risks strip­
ping the knowledge of its context, rendering it ineffective 
or culturally offensive. This challenge is particularly acute 
in safety testing and risk assessment. For example, standard 
AI safety protocols often utilize “red-teaming” or rigorous 
testing where developers explicitly list and discuss potential 
“bad things happening” (such as toxicity, failure modes, or 
societal harms) to mitigate them.
However, this standard engineering practice conflicts 
directly with specific Indigenous cultural protocols. In tra­
ditional Navajo philosophy, for instance, the spoken word 
has the power to shape reality; there is a cultural prohibi­
tion against obsessively discussing or articulating negative 
outcomes/death events, based on the belief that speaking 
of them can manifest them into reality [93]. A standard AI 
governance checklist that requires a Navajo developer to 
explicitly list “worst-case scenarios” may force a violation 
of Hózhó (harmony/balance).
This conflict highlights that “ethical frameworks” alone 
are insufficient. The solution to such a boundary mismatch 
cannot be found in a generic external policy but requires 
Indigenous technical leadership. Only Indigenous devel­
opers who possess both the cultural knowledge to respect 
the belief system and the technical expertise to understand 
the algorithm can evolve the necessary “workarounds” to

8.2  Limitations and positionality
It is critical to acknowledge the limitations of this study’s 
positionality. This paper presents a theoretical framework 
derived from a comparative analysis of foundational texts 
and published Indigenous scholarship; it does not represent 
the outcome of a participatory co-design process with spe­
cific Māori iwi or Navajo chapters. While we have sought 
to honor these knowledge systems through rigorous source 
selection, the specific governance mechanisms proposed 
here remain conceptual proposals until they are validated, 
critiqued, or refined by the communities themselves.
Furthermore, regarding the “demand side” of gover­
nance, we recognize that not all Indigenous communities 
seek integration into Western AI ecosystems. As central to 
the principle of Data Sovereignty, the right to refuse digi­
tization is as valid as the right to govern it. Some commu­
nities may view the incorporation of their knowledge into 
of misalignment between the proposed AI system and 
Indigenous governance structures. This assessment must 
involve face-to-face consultation with Indigenous knowl­
edge holders and community leaders who hold decision-
making authority, not merely advisory roles. Establish clear 
consent mechanisms that recognize and respect collective 
Indigenous decision-making processes rather than relying 
on individual consent models. Project teams should secure 
Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) from relevant 
Indigenous governing bodies and document how the proj­
ect aligns with or potentially conflicts with local knowledge 
systems before any data collection begins [97].
8.1.2  Data governance requirements aligned with IKS
Implement data governance frameworks that recognize 
Indigenous Data Sovereignty principles, treating data as 
collectively owned taonga (treasures) rather than extract­
able resources. Adopt models such as the CARE Princi­
ples for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, 
Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) alongside stan­
dard FAIR principles [98]. For Māori projects, consider 
Kaitiakitanga Licenses similar to those developed by Te 
Hiku Media which maintain community control over how 
data is accessed, used, and shared across generations. For 
Navajo contexts, ensure data governance structures respect 
traditional protocols about what knowledge can be shared, 
with whom, and under what circumstances. Establish clear 
protocols for data repatriation or destruction at project con­
clusion if requested by Indigenous communities.
8.1.3  Development team composition requirements
Structure project teams to include Indigenous technical 
leadership with genuine decision-making authority over 
system design, not merely consultative positions. Invest 
in Technical AI Literacy programs that enable Indigenous 
representatives to understand algorithmic decision-making, 
bias mechanisms, and deployment implications, ensuring 
they can govern effectively. Where Indigenous technical 
expertise is still developing, pair external developers with 
Indigenous knowledge holders in collaborative design pro­
cesses that value both forms of expertise equally. Recognize 
that the boundary mismatch between algorithmic knowl­
edge systems and Indigenous holistic frameworks requires 
bilingual expertise: team members who understand both 
computational logic and Indigenous governance protocols.

9  Conclusion
This paper has explored the potential of Indigenous knowl­
edge systems, specifically Māori Kaitiakitanga and Navajo 
Hózhó, to enhance AI governance frameworks. By examin­
ing the core principles and applications of these traditions, 
we have demonstrated their relevance to addressing the eth­
ical challenges posed by AI and promoting a more inclusive 
and sustainable technological future.
Analysis reveals that Kaitiakitanga and Hózhó offer valu­
able insights for shaping AI governance in several key ways. 
First, they emphasize the interconnectedness of all things, 
challenging the anthropocentric and often siloed approaches 
prevalent in existing AI governance frameworks. This holis­
tic perspective encourages the development of AI systems 
that consider the broader impacts of AI on human societ­
ies, natural ecosystems, and future generations. Second, 
these Indigenous traditions prioritize long-term well-being 
over short-term gains, promoting a more sustainable and 
responsible approach to AI development and deployment. 
This challenges the prevailing focus on rapid technologi­
cal advancement and economic growth, encouraging a more 
mindful and ethical approach to AI innovation.
Third, Kaitiakitanga and Hózhó emphasize the impor­
tance of community engagement and collaboration in 
decision-making processes. This highlights the need for 
Indigenous communities to be actively involved in shaping 
AI policies and development processes, ensuring that AI 
technologies are culturally appropriate, respecting Indige­
nous rights and interests, and contributing to the well-being 
of Indigenous communities.
By integrating these Indigenous perspectives into AI 
governance frameworks, we can foster the development of 
AI technologies that are not only ethically sound and cultur­
ally sensitive but also contribute to a more just and sustain­
able future for all. This requires a commitment to ongoing 
dialogue, collaboration, and mutual respect between Indige­
nous communities, AI developers, and policymakers to nav­
igate the complexities of integrating Indigenous knowledge 
into AI governance and ensure that AI technologies benefit 
all members of society.
