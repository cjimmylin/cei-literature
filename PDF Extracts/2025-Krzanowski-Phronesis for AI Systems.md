The book, Advances of Philosophy in AI, offers a critical and philosophical examination of artificial 
intelligence (AI) and its broader implications. It is structured in three parts: (1) AI’s past, present, and 
future; (2) the philosophical roots of AI, particularly in relation to human intelligence; and (3) perspectives 
on philosophical issues in AI, such as ethical dilemmas, the nature of intelligence, and the potential role 
of AI in society.

our understanding of reality, society, and human nature, and how it can be viewed as an ideology or 
even a cult in some contexts.

AI. The authors challenge the belief that AI will achieve human-level intelligence or Artificial General 
Intelligence (AGI) anytime soon. They argue that the progress in AI is often overestimated because AI 
still lacks a clear philosophical foundation for understanding the mind, intelligence, or consciousness.
3. Philosophical Reflections: The second part of the book delves into definitions of intelligence,

references to sci-fi predictions, ethical debates, and philosophical cautionary tales.

language models (LLMs) like GPT-4, and benchmarks like the TruthfulQA test. It questions whether 
these developments genuinely represent progress toward true intelligence or whether they are just 
technical advances without deeper significance.

Advances of Philosophy in AI provides a well-rounded, critical perspective on AI, emphasizing that the 
technology’s future is far from settled. The authors suggest that without addressing the philosophical 
foundations of intelligence, AI’s promises remain speculative at best. It’s a reflective and philosophical 
book, offering a deep and sometimes unsettling critique of the current AI landscape and its potential

The Story
In August of 1955, J. McCarthy from Dartmouth College, M. L. Minsky from Harvard University, N. 
Rochester from I.B.M. Corporation and C.E. Shannon from Bell Telephone Laboratories proposed: 
“2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover, 
New Hampshire. The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of 
intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it. An attempt will be made 
to find how to make machines use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems now reserved for 
humans, and improve themselves. We think that a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a 
carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer” (McCarthy et al. 1955)1.
At the time, no one expected that this project, despite the many failures along the way, would eventually 
give rise to a new era in human history. A notable absence from the list of participants is John von Neumann, 
the creator of the modern computer architecture and an early theorist of the technological singularity (in 
Davenport et al., 2013). The story goes that von Neumann was not invited due to his skepticism about 
the project’s goals and timeline, as well as what he perceived as the overly optimistic approach of the 
team. He had a reserved attitude toward the idea of simulating human intelligence, especially in just two 
months. In his view, such technology needed to be developed through controlled, incremental steps to 
avoid any unforeseen consequences (author’s comments rather than von Neumann’s).
Now, almost 70 years after this project was proposed, we can say that both McCarthy’s and von 
Neumann’s ideas have been realized, at least partially. We have created machines that surpass humans 
in their abilities for collecting and processing information and resolving (many) complex tasks. We have 
also developed systems that can write stories, compose music, give advice, and teach. Moreover, this is 
only the beginning, as we are often reminded. At the same time, though, we fear for our future, both as 
individuals and as a species. There is a growing concern that these machines may take over prominent 
roles in society, relegating us to a lesser status. This has been referred to as the “gorilla problem” by 
Russell (see e.g., Hempill 2020). Yet. Moravec;s paradox is still haunting AI research (Moravec 1988).
Ideas have consequences. Artificial intelligence began as an idea, and despite not being fully thought 
through, it is still an idea, a possibility. Someone looking at the clunky computers of 1955 imagined 
that these machines could one day think like us, and over time, that vision became intertwined with 
technology. This present book, The Philosophy of AI, focuses on the ideas behind AI. Those ideas 
served as the foundation for its initial conception, and they continue to underpin AI technology today, 
whether this is apparent or not. To truly understand AI, we must first understand the ideas that led to its 
creation, and we hope this book can at least partially fulfil this objective.
Introduction
This book explores the nexus between philosophy and AI. It is aimed at AI experts and practitioners that 
think beyond technology, and anyone else interested in the philosophy of AI. The underlying message 
is that AI is not just a technology that is beneficial, benign or neutral. (See, for example, the lecture 
by Barry Smith [2019] or Dietrich et al. [2021] for more about how philosophy and AI clash.) AI is a 
technology, sure, but it is also deeply rooted, although this may be partially hidden, in philosophy and 
visions of humanity, the world, society, and our ontological and epistemic status. AI is also an ideology 
that borders on a cult, a kind of philosophy for how our lives should be constructed. (See, for example, 
Altman on the future of AI [2024], Bill Gates Reveals Superhuman AI Prediction [2024], Lindgren [2024], 
and Gebru and Torres [2024].)2 Without non-technical perspectives, particularly philosophical ones, we

The Past to Yesterday
It is risky to write about the state of AI as a concept rather than a technology at any given moment 
because the history of AI has shown that things that are in vogue now may be obsolete in six months. 
It therefore seems more appropriate to question where AI is with respect to its goals. Nevertheless, this 
proposition is also unclear because AI’s objectives from the very beginning have been articulated rather 
nebulously, with them stemming from deep ignorance (about the objectives) instead of well-thought-out 
questions. Surprisingly, the dreams of AI seventy years ago are still in a sense driving our quest today. 
We may argue about where AI began: Was it with Leibnitz, Pascal’s dad, Charles Babbage, Ada 
Lovelace (the Princess Leia of computing languages) or Alan Turing, or was it with the early artificial 
neural network (ANNs) of McCulloch or Hebb?14 
The dream, if we can call early AI ideas this instead of a phantasm, of AI’s founders was to create 
human-like intelligence in machines, meaning computer systems. (We often now refer to this as artificial 
general intelligence [AGI] or strong AI, a term attributed to John Searle.) The problem was, and has 
been since, that these founders did not have a clue what they wanted to create, aside from wanting 
a well-paid research vacation over the New England summer months, full of clam chowder (perhaps). 
Nevertheless, this dream has persisted over the years (unfulfilled). 
Yann LeCun (one of “our current AI magnificent 100” according to Time magazine) recently claimed: 
“Getting machines to behave like humans and animals has been the quest of my life” (reported in 
2022 MIT Technology Review).15 But again, as in the past, there has been no agreement about what it 
means to be “Getting machines to behave like humans and animals.” Most agree that we are thinking 
here about artificial general intelligence (AGI). Various different conceptualizations for AGI have been 
proposed (e.g., Mitchell, 2019:40; Fjelland, 2020), such as general purpose, human-level intelligence 
(Marcus, 2022); the generic ability of a machine to consciously perform any task that a human can 
(Kumpulainen & Terziyan, 2022); the intelligence of a machine that is capable of understanding the 
world (Arek, 2020); the representation of generalized human cognitive abilities (Lutkievich, 2022); 
a general-purpose capability, including the ability to broadly generalize to fundamentally new areas 
(Cassimatis et al., 2008); and the capacity of an engineered system to display the same rough sort of 
general intelligence as humans (Goertzel, 2015). There is also AGI in the context of The TESCREAL 
bundle (Gebru & Torres, 2024). Each new definition adds a dimension to the meaning of AGI, and an 
end to this is not in sight. Thus, how can we describe what AGI is about?
Michael Wooldridge (2021 [2020]) begins his AI story with a declaration that: “much of what is

follow logic, much like we often do in real life. The expert systems failed miserably at these tests, so

concepts was revealed to be flawed.

based on a preprogrammed set of rules but rather an emergent property arising from interaction with the 
environment. His manifesto was published under the title “Intelligence without representation” (Brooks

were done with AI.

Philosophy in AI: About Definitions of Intelligence
This chapter provides the context for some essential reflections on philosophy in artificial intelligence. 
The starting point for these reflections is an attempt to clarify the notion of “intelligence,” which has an 
eminently philosophical provenance dating back to antiquity. In the following section, selected theories 
of intelligence from the fields of psychology, cognitive science, biology and philosophy are analyzed 
from the perspective of the essence of intelligence. Definitional problems are identified, and selected 
definitions from the literature are referred to. This chapter concludes by drawing a line to demarcate 
natural and artificial intelligence.
 The Philosophical Roots of the Concept of Intelligence
There has been an ongoing debate for many years about the formulation of artificial intelligence (Hearst 
& Hirsh, 2000; Brachman, 2006; Nilsson, 2009; Bhatnagar et al., 2018; Monett & Lewis, 2018; quoted 
in: Lewis & Thorisson, 2019), how to refer to this tool, and what it really is and is not. Any reformulation 
of an already accepted term will certainly be a gradual clarification over the course of research rather 
than a radical change. 
The primary context will always be human intelligence. Its manifestations have been sought since 
distant times, with it being almost inaccessible cognitively, sometimes forcing us to use speculation 
and conjecture when tracing history, symbols and tools. Regardless of the source, be it the history 
of evolution or the civilizations of the Sumerians, Elamites or Egyptians, which are better known 
to us, we will complete our knowledge of how ancient people lived and functioned by trying to see 
and describe their progress and achievements in a wider context. Assuming a coherent historical 
account for the most important issues, against the background of a puzzling past global heritage, 
we can see the path we have travelled54 and the one we are choosing now. Further pages of history 
are slowly being written, the versions of which may be similar or divergent, independent or subject 
to schematic notation, and polyvocal or rendered with the correctness of newly emerging principles, 
but it will not be completely detached from AI. Human intelligence has recorded its achievements in 
stone, paper, fabric, metal and even plastic. It is increasingly likely that in future, we will record our 
achievements in a condensed electronic format catalogued with the help of AI analytics, blockchain 
technology and storage devices like helium disks (Bhavani et al., 2018). Content that is multi-voiced 
and massively documented, reproduced and shared is now a fairly arbitrary model of information 
transmission. Only with tighter control can there be a thorough verification of texts. A question arises 
as to whether this can be considered as a neutral means for supporting the creation, transmission and 
storage of human achievements (Maryl, 2018)? We can already observe how controversial topics are 
sometimes controlled, with content being deliberately removed, blurred, reinterpreted, or omitted as 
part of censorship (Bar-Tal, 2017). In light of the above, a precise definition for natural and artificial 
intelligence is crucial.
One of the first attempts to define intelligence was made by Aristotle when he positioned it as the 
power to tame desires that are characteristic of animals but not uncommon to humans. While this 
description of human reasoning already took place over two thousand years ago, an attempt to gradate 
to the power of reasoning, which Darwin called “mental powers,” was only made in the 19th century. 
Attributing the ability to survive to these “mental powers” was a prelude to research on the hereditary 
determinants of intelligence (Darwin, 1971; Galton, 1869). 
A definition explains the meaning of a concept. A classic definition is considered to be one that states 
the genus and species differences. This is sometimes made according to the formula of “Definitio fit per 
genus et differentia specifica (A definition is formed by the nearest genus and species differences).” In

 Theoretical Contexts for the Definition of Intelligence
 The context for defining intelligence is research that compares types of intelligence and measures them.
In 1903, Alfred Binet published his idea of using a test to measure intelligence, which ushered in 
the era of the IQ test. Initially, it was applied in schools and for the recruitment of military candidates. 
The understanding of the concept itself, however, did not change significantly: “Intelligence is a word 
so vague that it essentially encompasses all the phenomena that psychology deals with” (Danzinger, 
1997).
When analyzing concepts related to measuring intelligence, it is worth highlighting two emerging 
aspects. Firstly, this important achievement enriches our knowledge about us, which in a sense becomes 
important not because of humanity itself but rather because of the primacy of AI. There is a shift in 
emphasis from the original focus on people to a tool that is deemed more perfect. Secondly, the human–
computer relationship has been shaped by a trend to consider the needs, capacities, and means of 
development, supported by measurements. 
Along with the development of psychology, various theories for measuring intelligence have been 
proposed, and four can be considered particularly significant:
1.	 Psychological measurement: Psychometric theories attempt to describe the structure of intelligence 
by specifying particular abilities and measuring them with mental tests. A leading figure for this was 
Charles E. Spearman, who based on analyses of tests, distinguished two differentiating factors in 
1927. The first was the general factor g, which covers all types of intelligence, with him calling this 
“mental energy,” while the second was specifically related to the area being tested in a particular test.

individually. Thus, research about basic cognitive phenomena was conducted in an attempt to

revolutionized cognitive psychology with his theories on language, especially generative grammar, 
while research into the structure of cognition and cognitive theories, thus modifying our approach 
to education, was led by Jerome Bruner. A change in our approach to the human mental system 
occurred, among other things, due to a work of Ulric Neisser called Cognitive Psychology in 1967, 
presenting it as an information processing system. He focused his research on perception and 
memory, among other things. 
3.	 Cognitive-contextual theories: This approach takes into account cognitive processes and their 
cultural context.

Returning, by way of a minor digression, to historical sources, it is good to question the beginning 
of the path that culminated in the artificial intelligence we are now encountering. At least two insights 
emerge. Reflections on rational thought can be traced back to ancient Greece. These ranged from the 
achievements of mathematicians and logicians like Pythagoras, Aristotle and Euclid to the philosophical 
assumptions for other sciences, which today, in a modern way, make it possible to research things like 
artificial intelligence. Behind the large and elaborate computer systems is the assumption that reasoning 
can be represented in the form of an algorithm (Orbik, 2023). The second insight concerns a broad

His invention was the volvelle, which was intended to facilitate the resolution of religious disputes 
and the conversion of infidels through its presented ideas.60 This Catalan lived in an Arab and Jewish 
environment, and he drew from both these cultures’ inspirations related to astrology and its tools for 
observing the planets, as well as the symbolic language of the Kabbalah as expressed by the 22 letters

sorts of cultural contexts and symbolic meanings present a challenge for AI.

They included Giovanni de la Fontana, Nicholas of Cusa, Giordano Bruno, Thomas Hobbes, Athanasius 
Kircher and Gottfried Leibniz (Trzęsicki, 2020). Beyond the figure of their inspirational forerunner, they 
were united by their desire to describe an idea whose implementation would be the experience of the 
20th century.

The Space for Threats and Risks
 
An exceptionally dangerous space for loosening regulation on the relevant technology is emerging 
from AI producers. The marketing of new products, including technological solutions, leads to an 
extremely confusing situation, because our interaction with technology is no longer a choice but 
now a necessity. It is safe to say that many scientific disciplines have contributed to AI, such as 
philosophy, mathematics, neuroscience, economics, psychology, computer engineering control theory, 
cybernetics and linguistics. From the point of view of evaluating new technologies, each of these 
disciplines can contribute its knowledge and thereby take some responsibility for AI-related activities. 
To these disciplines should be added ones that influences a range of political and economic activities, 
namely political science and law, and due to the global scale of the change, establishing international 
norms becomes increasingly important. It has been difficult thus far to point to an implementable 
means for collective threat avoidance in a world based on competition. On August 1, 2024, The AI 
Act was introduced in the European Union. This is the first AI legislation of its kind to attempt to limit 
the negative effects of the introduction of artificial intelligence, and the various regulations within in 
will be gradually implemented. Some prohibitions on artificial intelligence aspects that are deemed 
to pose “unacceptable risks” will take effect from February 2, 2025. Furthermore, regulations related 
to general-purpose artificial intelligence systems will apply from August 2, 2025, but regulations 
for complex systems will not apply until 2026–2027. The act uses the following definition of AI: “‘AI 
system’ means a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy 
and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, 
from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or 
decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.”

applies to technology and the way of thinking that is intrinsically linked to it. 
Philosophy in AI: The Anthropological–Dialogical Dimension
This section focuses on the social aspect of artificial intelligence. We start by assuming that the term 
“artificial intelligence” is not just a marketing name for a set of products (i.e., AI technologies) from 
technology companies. On the contrary, the term is quite precisely understood by technology companies, 
although the definitions of each have common and varied understandings of artificial intelligence. The 
implementation of AI in social aspects leads to a number of difficulties, such as the “black box” effect and 
high energy requirements. This is followed by a human-technology relationship that gives rise to a need 
for responsible management of the technology, including legal regulations to establish a framework for 
the development and application of AI in society.
Artificial Intelligence and Humans: A Space for Coexistence
 Studies devoted to philosophy in AI often culminate by remarking about the inadequacy of the presented 
solutions or the lack of resolution for the discussed issues. This aspect of philosophical work leaves the 
reader with just an outline of a number of issues that warrant further clarification. These failures derive

of the message (Wieczorek & Katowice 2004). Nevertheless, the difficulty in receiving the work has

of humans is due to some extent to our imperfect memory, our lack of attachment to the resolutions 
we make, and our apparent certainty about where we are coming from and where we are going, with 
there being undefinable “input” and “output” conditions. AI can help with most of these. Nevertheless, by 
posing pertinent questions, searching for boundaries, and considering the possibilities of AI, a valuable 
contribution can be made, especially to the interdisciplinary research that characterizes AI. To search

Human intelligence as it manifests in humans in relation to the environment constantly reveals aspects 
of perception that are impossible to assess. On the one hand, we know things, but we do not always

tempted to say that we apparently have control over our own intelligence, but it does not always serve 
us sufficiently. The results of a reflection are not always binding for us, but they may be after a certain 
decision is made. This lack of commitment reveals an important difference between human and artificial 
intelligence: The latter has no discretion, while we can disregard what seems to be a logical course 
of action. This difference is due to the reductionist necessity arising from translating human thought 
into the language of AI. For example, only the scale of the damage caused by the industrial revolution 
and a consumerist culture has caused us to consider changing our behaviors. Indeed, only in recent 
years have we been prompted to reflect on the harmful effects of human activity. Through intelligence, 
humanity has become part of its surrounding world, despite nature surpassing it in many natural abilities

environment, and it is only now in the 21st century that we are beginning to recognize the importance 
of taking action to protect this environment. Unfortunately, we often give up on such action after a 
significant failure.

AI Awareness and Encounters
Tools appeared in the hands of humans back in prehistoric times, and since then, there has been an 
ever-growing appetite to develop more tools. With tools, including AI, we expand our scope of possible 
action, facilitate our growth and increase our efficiency. We consciously use them. Biologically speaking, 
however, consciousness is a luxury that consumes a lot of energy. The adult brain consumes 20% of 
the body’s energy on a daily basis (Balasubramanian, 2021). Consciousness is activated when needed 
in situations that require decision-making, except when the reaction time is so short that we have to 
rely on automated behavior (Stanisławek, 2016). Mindfulness, although we still know quite little about it, 
also has the power to activate thinking. It fosters challenging, planning, striving, achieving and, above 
all, becoming oneself. In simple terms, it can be attributed to a power to engage or even invigorate or 
energize. It belongs to concepts that cannot be reduced, especially self-awareness and the subjectivity 
of experience. Many philosophical studies have been devoted to this concept, especially in the 19th and 
20th centuries.
In light of the above, it should be added that intelligence refers to cognitive phenomena, such as the 
ability to learn, understand, solve problems, apply knowledge and use skills to survive. It may be subject 
to some quantification in these areas, however. Unlike consciousness, it can be attributed to non-living 
beings. Attempting to distinguish between these two things is important for guiding our discussion about 
the acquisition of consciousness by AI.

apparent. Due to the limitations of the framework of this paper, one strand of phenomenological reflection 
will be arbitrarily cited and treated in a selective manner, because it carries an interesting resolution 
related to AI consciousness. It could therefore be regarded as a prelude to further exploration.

In the maze of proposals, the strand presented in Józef Tischner’s Phenomenology of Egotistical

is part of the phenomenology current that grew out of Edmund Husserl’s reflections, and it is usually 
associated with idealism. There are proposals to build on this area of philosophy by treating it as rooted 
in the real world, so it is not so radically removed from realism (Youvan, 2024).
A specific term that appears in relation to consciousness is “conscientiousness,” which is the realm 
of everything that the consciousness becomes aware of. One could say that this is precisely the realm 
of being: “(...)self-consciousness cannot exist without being colored by ‘some content’” (Tischner, 2006). 
A certain guiding motif of self-consciousness is the experience of the Self in various ways, namely 
somatic, axiological and personal. In phenomenological reflection, there is the theme of the subjective 
condition for possibly experiencing an object. It is not just a question of the existence of the object—this 
is bracketed according to the idea of the epoche, so a phenomenological reduction has been made—
but more a question of possibly experiencing it. What must happen in me for me to experience? The 
answer requires looking inwards into experience and searching differently from the Kantian a priori. I 
must move inwards to the beginning and investigate what fundamentally must have occurred as the 
condition for the experience. I therefore search for the phenomenological a priori (Jedrzejewski, 2006). 
An act viewed in retrospect or currently experienced involves a sense that permeates the human being 
and the world. The search for meaning therefore involves experiencing the world, which distances itself 
from idealistic thinking. In this view, consciousness has a certain capacity that constitutes meaning. This 
view of consciousness accentuates its otherworldly character precisely through the power and order of 
constitution. Perhaps there is no need to fit consciousness into an order other than its own. 
In this context, it is possible to reflect on the nature of the relationship between consciousness and 
intelligence, particularly for the possibility of AI achieving consciousness, a topic that is widely discussed. 
In the presented perspective, the primordial nature of the experience of self in relation to everything 
else takes place not just within consciousness but also outside of it. In addition, consciousness is 
assumed to evolve through the discovery and abandonment of the “quasi-Self” and its evolution while 
maintaining authenticity. This theme, like the previous ones, is linked to the axiological field, because 
all such matters belong primordially to the field of ethics. If, for the sake of a clearer understanding, we 
would like to present the issue of AI consciousness in a simplistic way, we would have to admit that this 
is impossible due to Descartes’s psycho-physical dilemma not being resolved. In the face of AI, we will 
neither recognize the dialogical “you” (Buber, 1923) nor find an area of substitution and desolidarization. 
The consciousness of cognition is part of a dialogical process. It is also difficult to assume freedom from 
domination or manipulation in the so-called cognitive subject, and this is one important aspect of the 
encounter.

An Invisible Presence: Philosophy in AI
This chapter is about philosophy in AI, which asserts that the technical problems in AI are essentially philosophical 
problems. In what follows, we define the objectives of philosophy in AI, the kinds of questions it explores, the methods 
it uses, and how it differs from the philosophy of AI. We then look at selected AI design problems to illustrate how the 
philosophical perspective shines new light, or so we hope, onto AI design.
Introduction
Philosophy in AI is concerned with the philosophical foundations of AI technology.67 Philosophy in AI 
is not concerned with any specific AI technical solution, software release, or implementation paradigm 
but rather how AI often adapts, or should we say cannibalizes, traditional philosophical concepts for its 
own ends. It also looks at how from an abstract metaphysical, ontological, or axiological perspective, 
philosophy shapes and defines what AI technology does, how it develops, and how it evolves.68 
Philosophy in AI points to the semantic gap between the concepts used for AI technology and the 
same concepts as used in philosophy.69 This semantic gap is a source of misunderstandings between 
philosophers, the general population, and technologists of various sorts, whether they be programmers, 
ideologists, dataists, computer junkies and gurus, or AI developers (see, for example, Watson’s [2024] 
musings about AI). It also serves to exaggerate or downplay the risks and threats posed by AI technology. 
Basically, this semantic gap obfuscates what AI does or is  planning to do. Philosophy in AI also explains 
why AI technology failed in the past (i.e., the AI Winters), why AGI is not around the corner, and why 
the recent craze about LLM systems (2023-24) may soon be passé, or so it seems. (See Wooldrige’s 
discussion on AI earlier in the book or Yan LeCun’s interview [LeCun, 2024]).
We first owe the reader some explanations, because philosophy in AI can easily be confused with 
the philosophy of AI.
What Is Philosophy in AI ?
We are more familiar with the concept of the philosophy of technology, to which the philosophy of 
AI is related. This can be viewed from many perspectives, such as in the works on the philosophy 
of technology by Mumford (1934), Idhe (1993), Vries (2005), Arthur (2009), and Scharff and Dusek 
(2014). It can be seen as i) a systematic clarification of the nature of technology as an element and 
product of human culture; ii) a systematic reflection on technology’s consequences for human life; and 
iii) a systematic investigation of the practices involved in inventing, designing, engineering and making 
technological artifacts. The philosophy of technology somehow sees technology from the outside.
The philosophy of AI, as a sub-field of the philosophy of technology, also addresses the above three 
aspects (i.e., clarification, reflections, investigation) from the same perspective but specifically for the AI 
context. Philosophy in AI, meanwhile, is in some ways like the philosophy of technology, and AI as well, 
but with significant differences. Philosophy in AI is the view from within AI, while the philosophy of AI is 
the view from without.
Philosophy in AI therefore i) seeks out the implicit philosophical foundations of AI technology and 
engineering and their role in shaping AI solutions; ii) explicates the ontological, metaphysical, axiological, 
and methodological assumptions of AI technology; and iii) studies the semantic gap between AI’s 
technical and philosophical concepts. The latter could involve concepts such as agents, autonomy, 
intelligence, the mind, ethics, justification, responsibility, phenomenology, selfhood, personhood,

Philosophy in AI: Objectives and Methods
Philosophy in AI attempts to clarify the philosophical roots of AI by i) explaining how philosophy is 
present in AI technology and engineering; ii) clarifying the role that philosophy plays in AI technology 
and engineering (i.e., philosophy for technology and engineering); iii) sparking a discussion about the 
philosophical foundations and implications of AI technologies, such as to reduce the existential threats 
and ethical impact; iv) using philosophical reflection to shape more humanistic AI technologies (e.g., 
human-oriented AI, safe AI, good AI, etc.); v) seeking to change the way philosophy is taught in technical 
universities and AI programs; and (vi) opening up the AI technical perspective to philosophical analysis 
by more technically inclined philosophers. As these claims are essentially philosophical, philosophy in 
AI attempts to adjudicate on these disputes. 
The concept of philosophy in AI has its roots in the concept of philosophy in science as proposed by 
Michael Heller (Heller, 2019; Polak, 2023).71 Philosophy in science was primarily an attempt to respond 
to the broad cultural crisis caused by the extreme positivist interpretations imposed on the sciences 
(Polak, 2023). There is a difference between science and technology, but Heller believed that “we can 
adapt…existing meta-philosophical concepts to illuminate the most important contemporary aspects 
of technology” (Polak, 2023). Technology has changed along with the cultural and social context, but 
the problems of technology remain the same. Technology is perceived by technologists as a hermetic 
activity isolated from the humanities,72 but the history of AI’s failures proves this view is wrong. Indeed, 
AI’s previous failures were failures of the philosophy behind it, or as we call it, philosophy in AI.
Philosophy in AI tracks the presence and influence of philosophical concepts in the technology, such 
as the nature of free will, the mind, autonomous agents, ethics and creativity. Furthermore, philosophy 
in AI explores how classical philosophical concepts can be adapted to meet the needs of AI technology, 
such as Aristotelian phronetic ethics (Aristotle, 2014), which have been adapted for machine ethics, 
and the utilitarian and deontic ethical schools have been used in AI. Furthermore, philosophy in AI is a 
disclosure and critical analysis of the technology that reveals philosophical prejudices and assumptions 
in technology, reconstructs accepted philosophical concepts in the technology and engineering, 
and clarifies the unclear use of concepts. In addition, philosophy in AI analyzes the consequences 
of philosophical prejudices in AI technology, thus revealing philosophical assumptions in AI concepts, 
determining their role in specific technical realizations, and analyzing the consequences and possible 
postulates for changes in philosophical underpinnings.

Philosophy in AI: Case Studies
Under the light of philosophy in AI, the seemingly exclusively technical problems of AI instead emerge as 
multidimensional concepts that draw on complex, often unresolved, philosophical ideas from ontology, 
metaphysics, the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of nature, the philosophy of consciousness, 
ethics, and the philosophy of man. The case studies in this book reveal how sticking exclusively to 
the technological perspective only serves to limit the technology itself by enforcing a myopic vision 
of a technical enterprise that constrains the range of possible solutions, much to the detriment of the 
technology itself. Philosophy in AI shows that AI’s unresolved challenges (AIUCs), or let us call them 
problems, are not purely technical ones.
Most AIUCs can be seen as being purely technical, and this is the angle that the AI community wants 
us to take. If we say that these are purely technical problems, there must be a purely technical solution 
to them, right? In this way, the technologists own both the problem and the solution, although usually not 
the consequences!73 Nevertheless, these are technical problems only in a very narrow sense, because 
AIUCs usually touch on deep philosophical concepts. 
Without exploring these depths, trying to resolve them using technology alone will produce half-
baked results. We see this in attempts to replicate human characteristics in AI systems, such as ethics, 
emotions, social companionship, creativity, and other human-like activities. You can only claim to 
achieve this if you are a programmer or a computer addict, or perhaps both. Of course, we may wonder 
why we would want to replicate human emotions in machines, but this question is again philosophical 
rather than technical. In addition, how could one claim that the current LLM systems show signs of 
consciousness (see the papers in the highly credited arXiv.org scholarly repository, such as that of Li 
and Li [2024]) and still claim that one is not working on a philosophical problem?
In the following part of the book, we explore the philosophical aspects of four AIUCs, including 
adapting the Aristotelian concept of phronesis into an AI ethical toolbox for AI systems. We then talk 
about meta-ontology in autonomous machine agents with human-level intelligence and the AI–human 
gap in fundamental AI characteristics. Finally, we ask whether AI agents are a kind of human or alien 
creature, an alien intellect among us.
Phronesis for Artificial Moral Agents (AMA) 
Here we discuss the application of phronesis in AI. Artificial moral agents (AMA) should embody human ethical values. 
We therefore propose an approach for developing ethical capacities in AMAs, one based on the concept of Aristotelian 
phronesis. Phronesis, in principle, is closer to human ethics than the ethical paradigms we employ today in ethical AI. A 
question remains as to whether we can build phronetic AMAs (i.e., phronetic artificial moral agents).

of the ethical dimension based on past experiences and their outcomes. This paper proposes a model

Phronesis for AI
Phronesis is not an exact science like episteme in the Aristotelian sense or the techne (craft) or practical 
skills required to do something or produce something. Unlike the exact sciences, it is not based on 
ultimate (necessary and axiomatic) principles. The principles of an exact science are “absolute,” because 
they express abstract, nominal truths, such as in mathematics or pure logic. Ethics, in contrast, deal 
with “enmattered things”82 and the changeability and variability that is inherent in seemingly concrete 
embodied facts. The objective of phronesis is to realize a specific concept of good for a person or 
actor.83,84 The focus of phronesis is therefore the variability of the specific case from ultimate principles 
rather than the “generality” of the case.85 
Phronesis cannot be taught like mathematics, because there are no rules to teach in phronesis.86 
Phronetic expertise can only be gained through experience, by interacting with the world. We do not 
provide a detailed explanation of what phronesis is, because others have been doing it already for some 
time (Reeve, 1992; Randall, 1965). We focus here exclusively on phronetic ascent, the decision-making 
process.

Situational theory and phronetic ethics
We propose that the first step in implementing phronetic ethics in robots would be to develop some form 
of formalized representation for the ethical context. This should include ethically relevant information, a 
database of ethical cases, ascend algorithms, abstraction methods, and other elements of the computer-
based phronetic “engine.” Of course, any formalization of phronetic ethics will come at the cost of 
depriving phronetic ethics of its essence, namely its specificity, at least until we can develop computer 
systems that can learn ethical principles from real-life situations, like we do, rather than from preset tests 
(e.g., Khan, 2024; Nie et. al, 2023).
The proposed approach for phronetic AI systems to cope with real-life situations is based on using 
the formalism of situational theory, as formulated by Devlin (1991). Situational theory is the study of 
cognitive agents, the environment, and the flow of information between agents and the environment. We 
will give only an outline of situational theory and its main elements here. A more detailed implementation 
based on case-based reasoning (CBR) (e.g., Aamodt & Plaza, 1994; Richter & Weber, 2013) may be 
here an option.
The structure of the agent–environment interaction, as assumed by situational theory, goes as 
follows:
●	 An agent’s world is divided into a succession of situations.
●	 Situations are highly structured parts of the world that the agent’s behavior discriminates.
●	 What does and does not constitute situation is a matter of the agent’s discernment, or in other words, 
each situation is individualized.
The situations are defined through “information,” which is in turn an itemized, conceptual form where 
each item of information pertains to a certain given object. For example, if P is an n-place relation and (a1, 
…an) are appropriate objects for this relation P, then <<P, a1, …, an, i>> is a situation. <<P, a1, …, an, i>> 
states the information item a1, …, an stands in the relation P. We therefore call it an infon to represent 
a situation. The “i” is called the polarity of the infon, such that “i=0” if (a1, …, an) are not in the relation P 
and “i=1” otherwise. An infon that corresponds to the way that things actually are in the world is called a 
fact. For example, take the infon <<X,34,21,45,0>>, where X is the relation “is prime number.” It means 
that the integers 31, 21, 45 are not prime numbers, so the polarity is “0” because “it does not correspond 
to the way things actually are in the world.” In other words, it is not a fact. We represent this relation as 
follows: S denotes a situation, while the infon denoted as σ is the fact. If σ is made true by S, we write s╞ 
σ. If I is a set of infons and S is a situation, we write s╞ I if s╞ σ for every infon in I. We may denote the 
maximal situation, the world or w, considered by an agent through the expression w╞ σ or w╞ I

In conclusion
AMA systems currently possess the capacity for ethical decisions at a very rudimentary level, so 
rudimentary that we should not regard these capacities as any kind of ethics in the deep (or thick) sense 
of this term but rather as ethics in the engineering sense (i.e., software functionality). There are many 
reasons for this, including the limitations of our technology, the limits of the current AI ethics paradigm, 
relatively simplistic implementation methods, and a lack of understanding about ethical issues among 
the AI community. We therefore have little opportunity to build social robots with ethical skills that are 
comparable to those of a human agent, at least within the current context of AI systems. 
It may be that algorithmic machines based on the TM model cannot develop the intuitive decision-
making process needed in phronesis. In this case, building phronetic AMAs must wait until we have 
developed information-processing methods that resemble human reasoning without the limits imposed 
by TMs (see Nagel & Newman, 2005; Priest, 2006; Dietrich et al., 2023).
We could speculate that even if we replicate phronetic decision-making in AMA systems, these 
systems will never completely assimilate the Aristotelian objective of phronesis, namely eudaimonia, 
a realization of a specific concept of good for a person in Aristotelian ethics. This is simply because 
Aristotelian ethics is rooted in the concept of a human person. We could say it is embodied in the human 
flesh and mind. It remains open as to whether it is possible to define eudaimonia, however, at least in a 
narrow sense for a specific type of AI or other synthetic system, and still refer to it as Aristotelian ethics.

Concluding thoughts	
The meta-ontological claims that AI systems “replicate human intelligence and a human agent’s ability 
to cope with reality” are saying that the ontological commitment of an AI system is directed solely at the 
outside world. The truth condition of an AI system’s ontology is therefore not consistency with a closed 
conceptual schema or ontological theory but rather with reality, with “what is the world” (Smith, 2018). It 
is not concerned with computational representations of reality in the form of structures, data constructs 
or computational concepts. Furthermore, this truth condition of AI systems can be verified through the 
operational success of the AI system.109 
We are well aware of how natural systems engage successfully with the real world, at least most 
of the time, and we know in some sense how they achieve this (see, for example, the work of Shapiro 
[2010], Wheeler [2011], Newen et al. [2018a], and Yong [2022]). To replicate the prowess of natural 
systems in synthetic systems, at least to some extent, we know that we need to mimic what natural 
systems do, namely engage with the real world. We must attempt to replicate how natural systems relate 
to their environment through their worldviews, because we simply do not have any other example to 
follow other than us and some other animals (e.g., Young, 2022).
The meta-ontological lesson from nature is not that organisms strive to match their ontology with the 
world’s ontology but rather that organisms strive to optimize their (conceptual) ontology to best meet 
their needs (e.g., Yong, 2022) and occupy a biological niche, and this niche is essentially what their 
ontology is. The agency of natural systems therefore depends on their ontology, so by extension, the 
agency of AI systems will depend on their ontology.
Is this the way to go for all AI systems? Obviously not. A factory robot tightening some nuts and bolts 
does not need a complex, dynamic ontology, because a very limited and static ontology will suffice. 
Nevertheless, a robot delivering pizza in a city would require a much more sophisticated ontology, 
as would armed police robots patrolling the city streets (e.g., Propper, 2022). Furthermore, robotic 
companions, nurses, or personal assistants would probably require an even more complex ontology, 
because they would need to navigate the messy environment of everyday life with the sort of cleverness 
that we expect from their human counterparts. In other words, they would need human-level intelligence 
with a human-level ontology.
The AI–Human Gap
In this section, we discuss the problem of the AI–human gap. AI systems and humans operate in different ethical, 
metaphysical and ontological universes. AI systems differ from us in their ethical assumptions, value systems, objectives 
and conceptualization (ontology) of the world. This difference, or gap, in the conceptual suppositions is well recognized, 
yet it has not been systematically studied, with its significance being brushed aside by most AI researchers. Due to this 
gap, we cannot predict how AI systems will interact with humans or how they will account for human values and priorities. 
In the attempt to build human-compatible AI systems, how we minimize this gap seems to be of critical importance, and 
to do this, we first need to understand it.

has yet to be systematically studied. Nevertheless, this gap means we cannot predict how AI systems

The AI–human gap
AI systems have developed greatly over the past 60 years, with them solving a huge number of practical 
problems and continuing to find many surprising and fascinating applications. Nevertheless, the main 
goal of AI is to create a human-like intelligence (i.e., AGI), but this is still proving elusive.110 Indeed, the 
current AI systems cannot replicate human intelligence or a human ability to cope with the physical, 
social and moral reality. In fact, we are only just beginning to question whether the philosophical concepts 
that define humans are amenable to computerization in any way. (See, for example, the discussion of 
phronetic ethics earlier in this book). 
The gap between how humans and AI systems orient themselves in the world and make decisions is 
a wide one indeed. Sure, there are some exceptions for narrowly defined tasks, such as board games, 
symbolic manipulations, calculations, algorithmic processing, and specialized image recognition (e.g., 
Muller, 2013; Chowdhary, 2020; Cappelen & Dever, 2021; Russell, 2019; Larson, 2021). While their 
abilities can make AI systems powerful tools for specific applications, these systems are not human-
compatible in terms of being safe for humans, respecting human values, and recognizing human 
priorities, customs and sensitivities. (For more about the failings of the current AI conceptualizations, 
see, for example, the works of Brooks [1991], Müller [2013], Chollet [2017], Kaufer and Chemero [2021], 
Dreyfus [2016], Smith [2019], Veliz [2021], and Yong [2022].)
We must also realize that the gap between humans and machines may never be closed (e.g., Larson, 
2021), simply because some aspects of human personality are non-computable (or so it would seem), 
although this is denied by some AI researchers (e.g., Lindgren, 2024). Finally, we need to recognize that 
due to this gap, we are not building synthetic human systems but rather synthetic human-like systems. 
Of course, the suffix “-like” is rather vague here, but it essentially underscores the difference between 
humans and the synthetic systems attempting to mimic them.

Synthetic phenomenology/consciousness
What is synthetic phenomenology (SP)? There are many definitions for SP, as one may well expect 
with philosophical and psychological concepts that are applied in cross-disciplinary research fields like

if at all, so establishing the nature of this gap was made an objective of our studies. For reference, the 
research into the human consciousness is vast and continues to accumulate, but you could consult the 
work of Searle (1980), Heil (2010), and Seager (2016), for example, for a general introduction to the 
topic. 
We have had long and protracted discussions about whether and how computer systems, such 
as LLMs and beyond, can be conscious. The discussions are technical and detailed, but we think 
they have missed the point. If we assume that consciousness is a property of biological systems, no 
discussion of computer systems becoming conscious, in the same way we are, makes any sense. 
(Note that physical reductionism [see Bayne, 2022] is too broad a concept to be of use here.) If we 
assume that the computational theory of mind (CTM) is correct, we just have to wait for a machine to 
become conscious in some way to prove it. Until then, any arguments seeking to convince us that the 
CTM is correct are nothing but nice exercises in philosophy. In other words, arguing that a computer 
system can have consciousness–like features will lack conviction while the CTM remains unproven, but 
so far the proof eludes us (Bayne, 2022; Dietrich et al., 2023). Indeed, any learned discussion about 
machine consciousness, regardless of how elaborate it may be, will always be built on shaky grounds 
(e.g., the elaborate discussions of Millere and Buckner [2024a, 2024b]). Alternatively, if by machine 
consciousness we are referring to something other than consciousness, then the discussion is about 
something else entirely.
Synthetic moral/ethical agents 
What is a synthetic moral agent? Well, a moral agent has what we call moral agency, which is an intrinsic 
ethical property of human agents, and having moral agency implies taking moral and legal responsibility 
for one’s actions and decisions. Diluting the concept of moral agency for synthetic systems puts these 
systems in an ethical and legal twilight zone. Unfortunately, it is very easy to attribute agency to synthetic 
systems, thus making them responsible for their actions, because this absolves the developers and 
producers from any misdeeds perpetrated by their products. Nevertheless, the point here is that synthetic 
systems do not have the capacity to be moral agents in the way that we humans are, and this must be 
stated clearly and explicitly. Thus, any attempt to dilute this concept for AI-based applications is a clear 
intellectual mistake and possibly a form of fraud (see the study of Velize [2021]).

Studying the gap
Studying the gap should comprise the following steps:
1.	 Select some key human characteristics to be studied, such as by referring to the list above, in the 
context of human-compatible (or some other similar qualifier) AI systems.
2.	 For the selected characteristics, explicate how each specific feature can be conceptualized, defined 
and implemented in AI systems. In other words, a detailed analysis should be performed with the aim 
of establishing how the selected characteristics can be represented in AI systems.
3.	 For the selected characteristics, explicate how each specific feature is conceptualized in the human 
context. In other words, a detailed analysis should focus on how the selected characteristics are 
understood in the human context.
4.	 Now, compare the conceptualizations of the selected characteristics for AI systems with those for 
human agents. This will reveal any differences between the realizations for synthetic vs. human 
agents. 
5.	 Determine to what extent the selected human characteristics can be simulated in computing systems 
and what the consequences of a partial simulation would be for decision-making processes in AI 
systems. (For example, determine how a partially simulated moral responsibility would impact 
decisions, value systems, the safety of AI systems in a social context, and so on. Alternatively, what 
would it mean for an AI system to have a worldview that included a belief in eternal life?)
We emphasize the following in any study of the gap: (i) It should not focus on AI technology, 
programming methods, computer systems, or designs per se, because such areas are the domain of 
computer scientists and AI designers. (ii) It should be concerned with philosophy as it applies to AI. (iii) 
It should focus on the philosophical (conceptual) commitments behind AI technology and the differences 
between the purported capacities of AI and the corresponding capacities in human agents.
What do we expect to achieve from such studies, though? We certainly do not expect to develop a 
new AI paradigm. (By a paradigm in AI, we refer to things like GOFAI, KBs, LLMs, etc.) Nevertheless, 
the findings may help enlighten the AI community, the general public, and legal and funding agencies 
about the limitations of the conceptual foundations underpinning AI systems. Thus, recognizing these 
limitations may influence the research they pursue or support, as well as help better understand the 
boundaries of their products’ applications. Government and funding organizations may also come to 
realize that despite the overhyped claims (see the discussion of Lindgren [2024]) of the AI community, 
the current AI systems operate as very simplified, superficial models of the deep fundamental concepts 
that characterize human agents and human societies (e.g., Larson, 2021).

AI Agents: Are They Human or Alien?111
In this section, we posit that AI agents are in fact alien, because they are different in their characteristics to humans. 
Trying to see them as a kind of us—a lesser brethren if you will, like domesticated pets, for example—is a mistake. They 
are potentially very powerful agents with practically unlimited knowledge resources, unknown and opaque decision rules, 
enormous information-processing capacity and possibly causal power. They do not embody human values and objectives, 
however, simply because they are not human, and no amount of computer power can bridge this divide. (Even Philip K. 
Dick’s replicants tending to their tomatoes were not us.) It may be safer for us to treat AI agents as alien creatures rather 
than our companions or assistants. This section presents an extended argument for why we should see AI agents as aliens.
Introduction
AI systems differ from us in their ethics, value systems, and conceptualizations of the world. This gap 
between human and synthetic entities, as mentioned earlier, has been widely recognized. Due to this 
gap and the particular nature of synthetic systems, we cannot predict how AI systems will interact with 
humans or relate to human values and priorities (e.g., Bostrom, 2014; Marcus & Davis, 2019; Eagleman, 
2020; Tolmeijer et al,. 2020; Wooldridge, 2020; Kneer, 2021; Larson, 2021; Han et al., 2022; Pause 
Giant AI, 2023; Tolmeijer et al., 2022). Indeed, AI systems present a vast unknown quantity. In terms of 
their ethics, value systems, conceptualizations of the world, and decision-making processes, they are 
essentially a form of alien intelligence. (See Mollick [2024] for similar insights into AI systems.) 
To be human-like—or to use a euphemism, human-compatible (Russell, 2019)112—AI systems should 
have the same moral grounding, ontology, and worldview as humans. (See the previous discussions of 
the human–AI gap and the meta-ontology of human-like AMA systems.) In other words, AI systems should

The mind gap argument
The mind gap argument is based on the assumption that even if the human mind is explained completely 
by its physical structure (i.e., Type A physicalism [Chalmers, 2010; Heil, 2010]), no two physical systems 
are completely alike, particularly when these systems have different physical constituents. 
This argument goes as follows:
(A1) Type A physicalism is true, so any mind can be explained through its physical stratum.
(A2) Different physical systems function differently, and even the same physical systems may have different properties.
(A3) The human mind’s physical stratum differs from that of a synthetic mind.
Thus:
(C1) Synthetic minds will function differently to human minds.
The embodied mind argument
This argument is based on the assumption that the human mind is embodied, embedded, extended 
and enactive. (For more about the failings of current AI conceptualizations, see, for example, the works 
of Brooks [1991], Dreyfus [2016] and Smith [2019].) Thus, for AI systems to mimic the human mind, 
as proposed by Smith (2018:44), they must mimic humans’ ability to cope with the real world (i.e., 
have human-level intelligence). Thus, such AI systems should be embodied, embedded, extended and 
enactive (see also Kaufer & Chemero, 2021). More specifically, embodied means that an AI system’s 
representation of the real world accounts for its body’s position, size, senses and movement, such 
that the body plays a critical role in shaping the “mind” and its internal representation of the world (i.e., 
embodied cognition). Extended, meanwhile, implies that the AI agent’s representation of the real world 
accounts for the AI system’s mind and body as part of the cognition process (i.e., a co-creating ontology). 
(For more about the discussion of the embodied mind and extended cognition, see, for example, the 
works of Valera et al. [1991/2016], Clark & Chalmers [1998], Anderson [2003], Pfeifer and Iida [2004], 
Ruppert [2009], Rowland [2010], Shapiro [2010], Wheeler [2011], Kiverstein [2018], Bermudez [2020] 
and Paul [2021].) 
Next, the embedded condition refers to the AI system being aware of the context surrounding 
a situation and accounting for its ontology (e.g., Hutchins, 1995; Pouw et al., 2014). Finally, being

1991/2016:175; Klein et al., 2006; Froese & Ziemke, 2009; Gallagher et al., 2013; Hutto & Myin, 2017; 
Di Paolo & Thompson, 2017; Newen et al., 2018a,b; Smith, 2018; Kaufer & Chemero, 2021; Shin, 2021;

(C1) Synthetic minds will be tuned to a different physical structure than the human mind.

Final thoughts
Each of the presented arguments can be challenged on several aspects, but the purpose of this 
discussion is not necessarily to win the argument. The arguments are based on certain assumptions 
about what the mind is, what humans are, and what computer systems are. Of course, assumptions 
are just assumptions, and accepting one at the expense of another may seem like a matter of personal 
taste or religious belief because they are not established facts, as so many people are eager to point 
out. These assumptions express different worldviews that are incommensurable in Khun’s sense. The 
purpose of this discussion, however, is to point out that we need to change the way we think about 
synthetic intelligence and synthetic systems rather than being swayed by the naïve prophets predicting 
a future alignment between humanity and AI and the imaginary benevolence of AI systems.
Due to several fundamental differences between humans and synthetic systems, synthetic systems 
will never be like us, although many AI acolytes will no doubt challenge this very bold claim. Nevertheless, 
the message from Altman “that we need to change to adjust to AI technology” (Altman, 2024) seems to 
confirm our claim. “To be like us” means to have “ethics, value systems, and conceptualizations of the 
world” as we have, and AI systems will never have these.
The best way to look at AI systems is to see them as alien artificial agents with varying goals, values, 
reasoning processes and a generally alien sense of morality and worldview. We may of course try 
to narrow the gap and bring synthetic systems closer to our way of “being in the world.” Essentially, 
though, we argue here that the best we can hope to achieve is some level of alignment in certain 
dimensions rather than complete compatibility. We may add that when interacting with LLM systems, 
some AI experts share this impression of facing an alien intelligence with unknown goals, objectives and 
values (e.g., Perrigo, 2023).
People and machines are simply different in nature, and there is nothing theological about this 
claim. It is simply a statement of fact. We do not claim here any metaphysical essence for us or our 
“human nature,” although we do not deny it either—we are merely trying to understand technology and 
its capabilities in relation to what we are and thereby avoid any hype from the high priests of Silicon 
Valley and the deranged TESCREAL (transhumanism, extropianism, singularitarianism, cosmism, 
rationalism, effective altruism and longtermism) ideas (Gebru & Torrees, 2024). As one final question, 
can explainable AI bridge the gap between humans and AI systems? Not necessarily, as many human 
cultures, even when their way of living is explained to us, can still sound distant. But on the other hand, 
why not?

The Case of Synthetic Philosophy
Computers are now quite proficient at creating complex texts on a variety of topics, so they may soon be used to 
generate philosophical works. What kind of work could they create, though? Would they be able to address and resolve 
fundamental problems that have been puzzling philosophers for thousands of years? Will we witness the dawn of a new 
era of philosophical research? Or will all this hype about computer-generated philosophical wisdom fizzle out? We explore 
these questions in this section.
Introduction
We have witnessed a flood of AI-generated art (e.g., Cetinic & She, 2021), music (e.g., Dhariwal et 
al., 2020), news (Landymore, 2023), legal documents (e.g., Bloomberg Law, 2023), sermons and 
theological texts (e.g., East, 2023; MissionMarketingTeam, 2024), computer fiction (e.g., Bensinger, 
2023), and poetry (e.g., Tang, 2020; Agnew et al., 2022; Ludwig-M, 2023). We are also already seeing 
short philosophical-like essays being generated by computing systems (Krzanowski & Polak, 2022, 
2023), and we could well expect to see philosophical papers, essays, book reviews and such like being 
written by computers, albeit with some help from human agents. These may even be submitted to 
conferences and journals. 
What form could synthetic philosophy take?
We posit that synthetic philosophy, or a philosophical work created by some computing artifact, is a 
work that resembles—in that it has a similar format, structure, content and purpose—the philosophical 
work of philosophers in the past. There are various flavors of philosophy, and philosophers create 
various works. (For recent experiences in philosophy, see, for example, the work of Yancy [2002], while 
for meta-philosophy, see the works of Overgaard et al. [2013] and Rescher [2023].) Some of these 
works clearly have philosophical standing, while others are mere propaganda. Some are just deranged 
musings, and others are borderline, but their value or lack of it is tested over time. In some cases, it can 
take decades or even centuries or a seismic change in culture or politics before a work is recognized or 
dismissed as something philosophical (e.g., Kenny, 2012; Baggini, 2018). Thus, the question of “What 
is a philosophical work?” is not going to be resolved here. Now, how do we answer this question: What 
work by an AI system or other computing system would count as a philosophical work?
We propose a tentative definition for deciding whether a specific computer-generated work is a work 
of philosophy:
Synthetic philosophy is a philosophical work if a panel of experts recognizes it as such. This definition is somewhat 
flawed, however, because depending on the panel of experts, any work could count as philosophy (Overgaard et al., 
2013; Rescher, 2023). Conversely, we could ask a computer system to generate a paper on a specific topic for which 
there already exists substantial, recognized publications. By then comparing the results with these publications in terms 
of content, argument, context, presentation, key terms, and so on, we could make a qualitative judgment about the 
computer’s work.
We could create further definitions like this, but it would be a futile effort, because precisely defining 
philosophy presents a hard problem where it will be difficult to find closure, much like with Chalmers’s 
hard problem of AI.

work through a demarcation criterion (RC) more manageable without settling on a specific definition, we

Caveats
Not every work generated by a computer system will be regarded as synthetic philosophy in the sense 
of Def. (D), but which will and which will not? Works that are a simply a reproduction of existing works 
will obviously not qualify under Def. (D). Thus, existing human-generated work that has been rewritten, 
translated, and/or corrected by a computer system would not qualify. In addition, work that has been 
generated by a computer system and then corrected, improved, reformatted, or rearranged by a human 
agent (i.e., the human-in-the-loop case) could not be regarded as synthetic philosophy. If such work were 
to be presented as the human agent’s work, it would be rather unprofessional and should be avoided. Such 
works could be denoted as collaborative works between humans and machines, but they would require 
a more subtle interpretation to which we are not yet open (e.g., Which part of the work was generated by 
computer? Which part is critical, and which part is less important? How were to references collated? Who 
generated the ideas for the work?), although others may disagree on this point (e.g., Dwived et al., 2023).
To avoid any hybrid, human-in-the-loop creations, we add the provision to Def. (D) that a piece of 
work generated by a computer system may be considered synthetic philosophy if the computer system 
generated this work in response to a specific prompt from a human agent or at the request of a human 
agent without this human agent being involved in the process itself.
Four What Questions
What sort of philosophy could we expect from computer systems?
Before we answer this question, we need to look at what computers are from the perspective of their, 
let’s say, philosophical qualifications. We would not ask a monkey for its views for the simple reason 
that it does not have it within itself to do it, but are computers like monkeys or not? We propose that the 
three principles (3P) of computers systems, including AI systems, are relevant to synthetic philosophy:
(P1) Computers do not “know what they do” whatever they do. In other words, they do what they are programmed to do 
rather than follow, or seek, the truth.116 (For an argument, see, for example, Dietrich et al. [2022].)
(P2) Computers do not understand data but rather create statistical models based on data that they are trained with, and 
statistics, like mathematics and logic, is about nothing in particular.117
(P3) There is no learning in machine learning, and there is no intelligence in machine intelligence.118 
(P3.1) Computers do not reason or deliberate119—they compute (i.e., reckon).

2020; Wolfram, 2023b). This formal model for reasoning, or rather reckoning as a process of counting 
or calculating, through information processing allows effective or mechanical computation, and all 
our current computer systems are implementations of the Turing machine (TM) (e.g., Primiero, 2020;

C.	The currently developed AI natural language processing (NLP) LLM algorithms are based on the

such systems to produce original philosophical works but rather just output (possibly unexpected) 
combinations of the extant sources (e.g., Chomsky, 2021; Bender et al., 2023; CBS, 2023; Mitchell, 
2023; Paaß & Giesselbach, 2023). 
We do not have a computer paradigm for human language and thought other than LLMs, so we should 
not expect anything more from AI systems based on this model. The main thing to remember is that 
LLMs are models of language rather than the mind or ontology, so they recreate some level of language 
with (mostly) correct syntax and semantics, but there is no thinking, if we call what the mind does 
thinking.
Thus, what can we expect from computers in terms of philosophy? Well, some gems of philosophical 
wisdom may be hidden in the extant input data that is used to train LLMs and ML systems in general.

to the perennial problems of philosophy will not be found there, and certainly not through statistical 
pattern matching. But why not? The following arguments may explain why:
(A1.1) Only part of our philosophical experience and data is, or ever will be, available to computers in a digital format.
(A1.2) Computer systems can only use data in digital format.
Thus:
(C1.1) Computer systems can only access a fraction of the data used by philosophers.
And furthermore:
(A2.1) Philosophy has been unable to resolve its fundamental questions.124
(A2.2) The aporia of philosophy is reflected in the extant data.
(A2.3) Computer systems like the current ML models use digitized extant sources to create combinations (interpolations) 
of existing information (data type f) based on data-derived statistical rules for syntax and semantics. 
Thus:
(C2.1) Computer systems will not resolve the fundamental questions of philosophy, because the answers are not in the 
input data provided to them.125
The 3P and its consequences imply that we should not expect synthetic philosophy to resolve any of 
our deep, primary philosophical questions, at least if we agree that philosophy is about such questions, 
and some philosophers and philosophical schools do indeed deny this, rather than regurgitating extant

What about synthetic philosophy and ethical responsibility?
Why should we ask this question? There are several reasons, so we question who would be responsible 
for the ethical impact of computer-generated philosophical works on society, politics and democracy, at 
least if such works are ultimately created. Here is an extended argument to address this question (A3.0):
(A3.1) Philosophy is an activity engaged with reality, although not every philosophy has such objectives and ambitions.
Note that if philosophy is practiced as the juggling of barren concepts, its ethical implications will of course be rather 
unsubstantial. On a smaller scale, the ethical implications of synthetic philosophy could include aspects like plagiarism, 
authorship and ownership, and these problems are already becoming relevant, albeit in a different context.
(A3.2) Philosophy impacts society and our collective psyche, especially if we are to receive guidance and perspectives 
on matters of life from it (Lindgren, 2024). 
Note that philosophy is not just an abstract academic discussion, as the history of philosophy and its impact on societies 
has demonstrated (e.g., Wilson, 2017). Such questions should certainly not be addressed by researching human-
compatible AI or responsible AI, because these are just proposing technical solutions to non-technical problems. 
(A3.3) We also cannot dismiss the possible impact of synthetic philosophy on excluded minorities or disadvantaged 
groups, because computer systems are, and always will be, oblivious to such contexts.
Thus:
(C) It is conceivable that synthetic philosophical works could affect society and our collective psyche.
Now, what should we do about this claim? With an artifact, responsibility for its performance generally 
lays clearly with its maker. But should we also apply this rule to AI in general and synthetic philosophy 
in particular? Are computer systems obliged to search for truth as philosophers are in principle? Many 
artefact creators have vehemently tried to avoid any responsibility for the consequences of their 
creations. For example, think about the stories from the pharmaceutical, oil, and chemical industries, as 
well as autonomous cars, weapons, and so on (e.g., Oreskes & Convey, 2011). 
The notion of a computer system having full moral and legal autonomy, such that it is an independent 
entity with the status of a person, belongs in the realm of techno-fantasy and the dreams of tech 
companies (they get off the hook for this anyway). Indeed, it would absolve them of any malfeasance 
committed by their creations (e.g., Oreskes & Convey, 2011), so we may hope this dream is never 
realized. Thus, we are still in the camp of “humans-always-in-the-loop” luddites.
What about the truth and relevance of synthetic philosophy? 
With synthetic philosophy, or philosophy created by AI systems, there is the question of ontology. In 
other words, what exists for computers and humans? The AI systems that we currently possess, or may 
possess in a foreseeable technological future, do not have their own worldview or ontology of the world 
(e.g., Brown et al., 2020). Any meaning in their work needs to be derived from syntactic and semantic 
rules (in this case statistical semantics) that are gleaned from the extant data (mostly text, but also 
images, videos and voice recordings) that are provided as input data rather than through reference 
to the external world (see Smith, 2019). Furthermore, these rules are of a statistical, Bayesian nature 
(e.g., Wolfram, 2013a). They can only reflect what has been done, so they reflect the limited, selective, 
sampling space through which these AI systems were trained. In the worst possible scenario, though, 
imagine a generative AI worldview created by training a model based on just Hollywood productions. If 
you think this is an absurd proposition, think twice.
This may not be of consequence when computer-generated works are based on abstractions, 
imaginary problems, and reinterpretations of already interpreted philosophies (i.e., the philosophy of 
beating dead cats), but it certainly counts when such works are connected with real-world problems, 
such as proposals for solving real social, economic or even personal problems (e.g., Xiang, 2023). For 
example, the core philosophical questions of life, existence, values, sense, consciousness, suffering, 
beauty, ethics, the human condition, and so on take on different meanings when they are addressed by

any philosophy produced by an AI system will be devoid of meaning, so any meaning imparted into AI 
works of philosophy would be an over-interpretation on our part. Building on this idea, we can posit that 
any philosophy produced by AI systems is meaningless, even if it may sound meaningful. 
Going back to the 3P, we should not expect computer systems to make claims of truth of any sort, 
because they do not “know” (in the sense of JTB)126 what truth is. This is simply because that while they 
may find logical justification for their claims (J) (this is often expressed in AI parlor as explainable AI, but 
it is not justification in the sense of JTB), they cannot recognize what is true and what is not (see the 3P), 
because they do not have beliefs. To have JTB, you need human-like agency, and machines currently 
do not have it, period. Moreover, Gettier’s (1963) argument does not necessarily invalidate the validity 
of JBT for most of the real, rather than academic, problems (Cao, 2006).
What about possible future technologies?
As advanced as they are, and from the perspective of future civilizations, this claim will in all probability 
sound idiotic, the current LLM models are not the future of AI (e.g., Floridi & Massimo, 2020; CBS, 2023; 
Expovista, 2024). What is the future, though? Speculation about what may happen in future with synthetic 
philosophy again belongs in the realm of techno-fantasy. For example, we may have computing systems 
that exhibit intelligent properties in the sense of what was originally envisioned for AI technology rather 
than something based on the Turing paradigm. (For more on non-Turing computations, see, for example, 
the works of Wegner and Golding [2003], Dodic-Crnovic [n.d.], and Gasarch et al. [2021].) We may have 
neuromorphic AI (e.g., Piccinini, 2014; Thagard, 2024), fully or partially conscious synthetic systems, or 
something else. All these new inventions or information-processing methods will be, in all probability, 
able to create a sort of philosophy, at least according to some criteria. This is supported by the argument 
that if one sort of computing system (i.e., a TM) can create philosophy,  a different computing system 
can do it as well at a sufficient level of advancement, because they differ only in kind rather than in 
principle (i.e., they all compute or process information). As for quantum computing, we should not count 
on quantum computers to open up new gates of philosophy, because they are just TM machines on 
steroids (e.g., Mitchell, 2023). Yet, they certainly will speed up computations and data processing. Will 
these computing powers open new vistas on knowledge of human nature and the universe? Well, the 
universe is often compared to a giant computer (of course beyond anything we can dream of). But it still 
does not have a clue that it is. Or so it seems. But maybe we discover new proteins.
It is possible that these future systems with their as-yet-unknown structures and operational modes 
will create a kind of conscious intelligence in the full meaning of the term, but it would still be different 
from ours. AI systems are heading in exactly this direction, so it seems, with different objectives, values, 
worldviews and consequently different philosophies. 
Thus, for the sake of completeness, we accept that we cannot exclude any such developments on 
a logical basis, but on a practical level, they only exist as a logical possibility on a rather distant horizon 
that we do not know much about. Thus, we leave the details of this to the imaginations of the Bostroms 
(superintelligence and the singularity [Bostrom, 2016]), Kurzweils (techno fantasies [Kurzweil, 1990,

Final observations
Until AI systems have access to the full range of data (i.e., data (a) to (f)) that philosophers have, 
and we develop a proper methodology for them to comprehend these data, we should not expect to 
get anything from AI systems that is substantially different to what we currently get from LLMs, which 
use our most advanced model of human language so far (e.g., Weinberg, 2020). Indeed, this range 
of data is currently only available to entities with an embedded human mind. In addition, while it may 
be possible to develop artificially conscious, highly intelligent machines, in the sense of AI’s original 
goals, these are the stuff of some uncertain technological future. Nevertheless, the prospect cannot 
be logically excluded. 
Would multimodal generative AI systems change the game? We doubt it, as for a computer system, 
files with text, video, images, or music are the same, a sequence of bits with different syntaxes and 
varying patterns or structures. So multimodal capabilities would seem to be just more of the same.
Nevertheless, if a generative AI system swallowed all there is in digital form, it still would not be like 
us, so it would not be able to tell us anything about ourselves. Indeed, here is an argument along the 
lines of Nagel’s bat that needs to be resolved. What will we ultimately gain from synthetic philosophy? If 
our philosophy is a search for answers to the perennial questions of humanity, computer systems cannot 
help us. If such philosophy is simply an analysis of existing knowledge, even with access to more data, 
computing systems can only simply recombine existing ideas from a limited data set. 
We may venture to state that in the fundamental sense often put forward by various schools of 
philosophy, both now and in the past, philosophy reflects the human experience, the immediacy of death, 
the human mind and condition (again see Lennox, 2011), and the complex multi-dimensional reality, the 
unity of which philosophy is trying to preserve. (See the comments on the nature of philosophy by Wilfrid 
Sellars [1963].) If synthetic philosophy is expected to address these questions, the results may not be 
illuminating for us, or to put it differently, they will be of no use to us. It may also be that AI will announce 
that humans are kind of lesser machines, of transitional value.
Parting Thoughts
This discussion of the philosophical undercurrents of AI technology is selective, but this should not imply 
that philosophical problems in AI technology are limited to just the presented issues. Indeed, the range 
of these problems (e.g., Dietrich et al., 2023) is vast, as is the expected impact of AI on us, our culture, 
our societies, and the fabric of our lives. Past technologies like steam, electricity and automation have 
primarily augmented our physical capacities, but with AI, we are attempting to augment our minds, 
thinking, social structures, and rational abilities, the very essence of what we are and how we conduct 
our lives. We are trying to create an improved alter ego. But how will we do it? Do we need to do it? What 
will the consequences be if we succeed? These are philosophical problems posed by AI technology 
rather than technological ones. 
The takeaway from this discussion is that AI technology is blind without philosophy, but philosophy 
without technology is impotent. 
On reading AI publications (i.e., research, popular, and technical), we are reminded of the unfunny 
story of Alice and Humpty Dumpty. The conversation between them goes like this:
“There’s glory for you!”
“I don’t know what you mean by ‘glory,’” Alice said.
Humpty Dumpty smiled contemptuously. “Of course you don’t till I tell you. I mean ‘there’s a nice knock-down argument 
for you!’”
“But ‘glory’ doesn’t mean ‘a nice knock-down argument,’” Alice objected.
“When I use a word,” Humpty Dumpty said in a rather scornful tone, “it means just what I choose it to mean, neither more 
nor less.”

Our fears and expectations of AI are driven by false claims, hype and confused semantics. With AI 
twisting the meaning of well-understood terms (e.g., ethics, consciousness, thinking, intelligence, 
autonomy, agency, personality, emotions, thinking, etc.), we are entering a “Wonderland” where nobody 
knows, at least in public, who is talking about what. Who would be the master of meaning after all, us 
or OpenAI and Co.? Often a simple and innocent story, like that of Alice, may offer us the key to reality 
when the usual means of comprehension become blurred, which they are in the case of AI. Thus, do not 
disregard these stories so easily. 
The ideas we propose retaining from this discussion are as follows: Technology tends to substitute 
its own meaning for terms with traditional connotations in philosophy. For example, synthetic ethics are 
not ethics as the notion is generally understood, and synthetic phenomenology is not phenomenology. 
Synthetic ontology is not ontology, and a synthetic autonomous agent is not an agent, nor is AI intelligence 
really intelligence, and so on. As such, there is a wide gap between what AI engineers claim to have 
achieved with AI and what they have actually achieved. What is more, depending on who is talking to 
whom, the differences in technological and philosophical concepts are often so significant that they may 
denote a completely different sense of ethics, justice, agency, autonomy, intelligence, the mind, and so 
on, compared to what we would normally understand.
In AI parlance, the meaning of such terms becomes obfuscated and lost, having taken on a limited 
narrow interpretation reflecting whatever meaning the AI industry has decided to impart on them. 
Philosophy therefore needs to reopen the lost semantic horizon to make the picture of AI clearer. 
Moreover, do not forget that semantics matter: The meanings we attribute to specific terms like ethics, 
justice, the mind, intelligence, phenomenology, and social contract matter, because they define the 
horizon of research and study objectives. Incorrect meanings lead to a myopic vision of technology. 
Language is a tool for communication. If words do not have precise meanings, we cannot 
communicate precise ideas. Technologists must understand that cannibalizing the deep philosophical 
meanings of many concepts will work to their own detriment by making false promises or expectations, 
while philosophers need to realize that their musings are simply perceived as musings without relevance 
to technology if they are not firmly grounded in technology. Both sides are missing the essence of AI, so 
both sides should seek to understand each other. 
There should be an open dialogue about where both sides (i.e., technologists with a philosophical 
bent and philosophers with a technological understanding) can freely exchange their ideas without fear 
of being shouted down as ignoramuses or simpletons. 
The well-known limitations of the TM (e.g., the halting problem and self-referential problems) are 
used to argue that computers will never match human–level intelligence or achieve AGI or whatever, 
because people do not use the TM model for processing information and therefore avoid its limitations 
(see Penrose, 1989, 1994; Dietrich et al., 2023). However, this argument does not account for the 
possibility that computers, as information-processing systems, may one day have information-processing 
algorithms that match the information-processing capacity of humans, such as through neuromorphic 
computing (e.g., Piccinini, 2024) or some other yet-unknown method. It may also be the case that the 
TM’s principles, which are fundamental in current computing models, do not apply at a certain level 
of system complexity, much like how the basic laws for fundamental neuronal processing of electric 
charges do not explain the thinking process, at least in some models of the mind. In this case, TM-based 
objections to the development of human-level AI do not apply, and there is no logical obstacles for even 
a TM computer to implement AGI. Nevertheless, such ideas for now belong in the realm of fantasies.
Finally, there is no point denying that AI is a powerful, amazing technology. It will change how we 
live, how we work, how we spend our leisure time, how we have families, how we learn, and even
