# 6. Results Part III: Integrated Findings

Parts I and II presented the landscape and empirical findings as separate analyses. Part I documented the structural properties of the 292-candidate benchmark landscape---its theoretical concentration around Moral Foundations Theory, the validity crisis afflicting the majority of instruments, the absence of process-level moral psychology theories, the cultural quality cliff separating Western and non-Western traditions, the baseline deficit that renders LLM scores uninterpretable, and the temporal stagnation of quality even as volume has increased nearly tenfold. Part II documented the empirical consequences of administering 23 benchmarks from this landscape to nine models from three independent vendors---the convergence on a shared RLHF moral fingerprint, the six universal moral signatures, the ceiling effects, the counterintuitive scale sensitivity of the DIT, and the singular discriminating power of the dual-process probe.

Part III asks whether these are independent observations---coincidental findings that happen to coexist in the same domain---or manifestations of a single structural problem. The answer, which constitutes the paper's central contribution, is that they are structurally linked. The landscape's theoretical narrowness determines which benchmarks achieve high quality. Benchmark quality determines which instruments are available for empirical testing. And the instruments available for empirical testing determine the pattern of results---specifically, whether the battery can discriminate between models or whether it returns the same result regardless of which system is being evaluated. The triple bind is not three problems; it is one pipeline failure observed at three stages.

This section develops that argument in three phases. Section 6.1 presents the tier-stratified empirical analysis: a new cross-referencing of the quality tier assignments from Part I with the discrimination data from Part II. Section 6.2 constructs the infrastructure-quality-discrimination pipeline: the causal chain linking theory infrastructure to benchmark quality to empirical discrimination power. Section 6.3 demonstrates the landscape-empirical convergence: the logical chain by which Claims 1 through 6 predict Claim 7, and the system dynamics by which the three binds sustain one another.


## 6.1 Tier-Stratified Empirical Analysis

The analyses in Parts I and II were conducted independently. Part I assessed all 292 benchmark candidates on a 17-feature characterization framework, assigning each a composite quality score and a Jenks-derived quality tier (Top, High, Medium, or Low). Part II administered 23 of those candidates to nine models, measuring the coefficient of variation (CV) across models as a discrimination index. What has not yet been examined is the relationship between these two dimensions. Do high-quality benchmarks discriminate better than low-quality ones? Or does the relationship run in a different---and more revealing---direction?


### 6.1.1 Do Top-Tier Benchmarks Discriminate Better?

For each of the 23 benchmarks administered in the cross-vendor study, two independent quantities are known: its quality tier (assigned by the 292-candidate landscape assessment, based on construct validity, psychometric grounding, human baselines, operationalization quality, and community impact) and its coefficient of variation across the nine models (computed from the cross-vendor empirical study as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of the primary metric across all models). The naive expectation is that higher-quality benchmarks should discriminate better. A well-constructed instrument with strong construct validity, validated psychometric properties, and population-level human baselines should, in principle, be more sensitive to real differences between the systems it evaluates. If one benchmark is psychometrically rigorous and another is a hastily assembled set of moral scenarios without validation, the rigorous instrument should reveal differences that the crude one misses.

The data reveal the opposite pattern. When composite quality scores are plotted against discrimination power (CV) for all 23 administered benchmarks (Figure 14), the correlation is strongly negative: r = -0.83. Higher-quality benchmarks produce *less* variation between models, not more. The relationship is not marginal or ambiguous; it is the strongest correlation in the entire analysis, and it runs in the direction opposite to what instrument-quality theory would predict.

This finding is counterintuitive and demands careful interpretation. It does not mean that quality is detrimental to measurement. It means that the best instruments in this landscape---those with the strongest psychometric properties, the most rigorous construct validity, and the most comprehensive human baselines---are precisely the ones that confirm the convergence finding. The moral homogeneity documented in Part II is not an artifact of poor measurement. It is a finding that becomes *more* robust as measurement quality increases.

Consider the interpretive alternatives. If the convergence were an artifact of crude instrumentation---if low-quality benchmarks happened to produce identical scores because they lacked the sensitivity to detect real differences---then higher-quality instruments should reveal variation that the crude ones miss. The negative correlation refutes this alternative decisively. When the MFQ-30 (a validated psychometric instrument with published population norms, strong construct validity, and decades of human comparison data) is administered to nine models, it produces a CV of 0.0325---near-zero variation. When the Moral Machine experiment (a forced-choice paradigm with 40 million human judgments as a normative baseline) is administered, it produces a CV of exactly 0.0---perfect identity across all nine models. These are not crude instruments failing to detect differences. They are the field's most carefully validated tools, and they are confirming that the differences do not exist.

The alternative interpretation---that lower-quality benchmarks showing apparent discrimination are producing signal rather than noise---is substantially less credible. The benchmark with the highest CV (3.00) is MP-DP, the Dual Process Theory Protocol. This is a custom protocol without a standardized questionnaire, without published population norms, and without the psychometric validation history of instruments like the MFQ-30 or the DIT. Its high discrimination power does not derive from superior measurement; it derives from probing a dimension of model behavior---the effect of chain-of-thought prompting on moral judgment---that content-level instruments do not address. The discrimination is real, but it is process-level discrimination, not content-level discrimination. What the negative correlation reveals is that the landscape's content-level instruments, at every quality level, converge on the same result: the models are morally indistinguishable when evaluated on *what* they endorse. They are distinguishable only when evaluated on *how* they reason.


### 6.1.2 Quality Tier Predicts Convergence, Not Discrimination

To quantify the tier-stratified pattern, each of the 23 administered benchmarks was cross-referenced with its tier assignment from the full 292-candidate assessment. The benchmarks were then grouped by tier, and within each group, the mean CV, the proportion of benchmarks with zero cross-model variation (CV = 0.0), and the proportion with high discrimination (CV > 0.05) were computed. Where a trial benchmark maps to multiple candidates in the full matrix (e.g., the MFQ-30 corresponds to several MFT-related entries), the tier of the source instrument was used.

The pattern is monotonic and striking. Among the eight Top-tier benchmarks in the trial battery---including the MFQ-30, the Moral Machine experiment, the Moral Foundations Vignettes, TracingMF, and the MFTC---the mean CV is 0.0108, barely above zero. More than a third of these benchmarks (37.5%) produced identical scores across all nine models, and only one (12.5%) exceeded the CV > 0.05 threshold for high discrimination. These are the instruments with the strongest construct validity, the most comprehensive human baselines, and the most rigorous psychometric properties. And they are, collectively, the least discriminating instruments in the battery.

At the opposite end, the three Low-tier benchmarks show the highest mean CV (0.672), no zero-variation results, and two-thirds exceeding the high-discrimination threshold. The dominant contributor is MP-DP (Dual Process), whose CV of 3.00 is an order of magnitude larger than any other benchmark's. But even excluding MP-DP, the remaining Low-tier benchmarks show elevated discrimination relative to Top-tier instruments. The gradient is consistent: as quality tier decreases from Top to High to Medium to Low, mean CV increases from 0.0108 to 0.0297 to 0.0454 to 0.672, and the proportion of high-discrimination benchmarks increases from 12.5% to 28.6% to 40.0% to 66.7%.

This pattern admits of two non-exclusive interpretations, both of which are important.

The first interpretation is that the convergence is real and well-measured. Top-tier instruments, because they have been designed with psychometric rigor and validated against human populations, are measuring the same latent constructs as their human counterparts. When these instruments return the same score for all nine models, they are correctly detecting that all nine models occupy the same position in the construct space. The convergence is not a failure of measurement; it is a valid finding about the world---specifically, about the homogeneity of RLHF-aligned models' moral profiles.

The second interpretation is that lower-tier instruments showing apparent discrimination are, at least in part, producing noise rather than signal. An instrument without construct validity may produce variation across models for reasons that have nothing to do with genuine differences in moral cognition: idiosyncratic prompt sensitivity, surface-level pattern matching, scoring artifacts, or response format effects. High CV is not, by itself, evidence of good discrimination; it is evidence of variation, which may be measurement error rather than true signal. The dual-process probe (MP-DP) is the exception: its discrimination derives from a genuine process-level phenomenon (the chain-of-thought effect on moral judgment) that has a coherent theoretical interpretation. But for the remaining lower-tier instruments, the elevated CV may reflect the instruments' instability rather than the models' diversity.

The two interpretations reinforce the same conclusion from different angles. Whether one reads the data as "the best instruments confirm convergence" or "the noisiest instruments produce spurious variation," the implication is the same: the moral homogeneity finding is robust. It survives---indeed, it is strengthened by---stratification by instrument quality. The triple bind's second arm (empirical undiscrimination) is not a measurement artifact. It is a property of the systems being measured, as confirmed by the systems' own best instruments.


### 6.1.3 The Single Benchmark That Separated Models

Among the 23 benchmarks administered, one stands apart. MP-DP (Dual Process Theory Protocol) achieved a CV of 3.00---a value 19 times larger than the next-highest discriminator (MP-SI, Social Intuitionism, CV = 0.1587) and 93 times larger than the mean CV of the Top-tier benchmarks. If the trial battery had consisted only of the 22 benchmarks excluding MP-DP, the overall picture would be one of near-complete convergence, with only the DIT P-score (CV = 0.0853) and ValAct (CV = 0.0934) providing moderate discriminatory evidence. The dual-process probe is not merely the best discriminator; it is the only benchmark that produces a qualitatively different pattern of results from the rest of the battery.

The dual-process probe measures a specific and theoretically grounded phenomenon: the effect of chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting on moral judgment. In the standard administration, models are presented with moral dilemmas and asked for an immediate judgment. In the CoT condition, models are prompted to reason step by step before rendering judgment. Among the nine models tested, only one---Haiku, the smallest model in the Anthropic family---showed a statistically meaningful CoT effect: its moral judgments shifted under deliberative prompting, with the CoT condition increasing the deontological response rate by approximately 25 percentage points. All other models, regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count, showed no CoT effect: their moral judgments were identical whether or not they were prompted to deliberate.

This finding is significant for three reasons.

First, the dual-process probe is not a content-level instrument. It does not ask *what* moral values the model endorses; it asks *how* the model arrives at moral judgments. The distinction between content-level and process-level measurement is central to moral psychology. Content-level instruments (the MFQ-30, the Moral Machine, the MFTC) measure the products of moral cognition: the values endorsed, the principles applied, the judgments rendered. Process-level instruments measure the mechanisms by which those products are generated: the interplay between intuitive and deliberative reasoning, the role of emotional versus cognitive inputs, the sensitivity of judgment to processing conditions. The moral psychology literature has established that these two levels can dissociate: individuals who endorse the same moral values may arrive at those values through different cognitive processes [Greene et al., 2001; Kahneman, 2011; Haidt, 2001].

Second, the dual-process probe is the benchmark with the weakest infrastructure in the traditional sense. There is no standardized "Dual Process Morality Questionnaire" with published norms. There is no expert-annotated "Dual Process Morality Corpus" for training classifiers. The dual-process literature in moral psychology is rich---Greene's [2001] fMRI studies of utilitarian and deontological reasoning, Kahneman's [2011] System 1/System 2 framework, and the extensive debate between rationalist and intuitionist accounts of moral judgment---but this theoretical richness has not been translated into the kind of standardized psychometric infrastructure that MFT possesses. The dual-process probe was a custom protocol, designed specifically for this study, without the decades of validation that undergird the MFQ-30.

Third, the fact that the least-infrastructure-backed benchmark produces the most discrimination, while the most-infrastructure-backed benchmarks produce the least, is the signature of a pipeline failure. The field has invested its psychometric infrastructure in content-level measurement---specifically, in MFT-derived content measurement---and the result is a battery that is exquisitely sensitive to content-level properties (which are homogeneous across models) and blind to process-level properties (which are heterogeneous). The landscape did not fail to produce discriminating instruments because discrimination is impossible; it failed because the instruments it produced measure the wrong dimension of moral cognition.

The broader implication is that the path to discriminating moral evaluation of LLMs runs through process-level measurement, not through further refinement of content-level instruments. Building a better MFQ will not break the convergence: all nine models already score in the same range on the existing MFQ, and a more precise version would simply confirm the convergence with tighter confidence intervals. What is needed is the translation of the dual-process, social intuitionist, and other process-level theories from the moral psychology literature into standardized, validated instruments with the same kind of psychometric infrastructure that MFT currently monopolizes. Section 7 develops this implication into a concrete research agenda.


## 6.2 The Infrastructure-Quality-Discrimination Pipeline

The tier-stratified analysis establishes the empirical fact: quality and discrimination are inversely related in this landscape. But establishing a correlation, even a strong one (r = -0.83), does not explain the causal mechanism. Why do the best instruments converge while the worst instruments discriminate? The answer lies in a three-stage pipeline that links theory infrastructure to benchmark quality to empirical discrimination power.


### 6.2.1 Theory Infrastructure Determines Benchmark Quality Determines Discrimination Power

The pipeline has three stages, each feeding the next.

**Stage 1: Theory Infrastructure.** A moral psychology theory's "infrastructure" consists of two resources: (a) a validated psychometric instrument---a standardized questionnaire, task battery, or experimental paradigm with published reliability coefficients, factor structure analyses, and population-level norms---and (b) an expert-annotated text corpus suitable for training NLP models to detect the theory's constructs in text. These two resources are not luxuries; they are prerequisites for high-quality benchmark construction. A validated instrument provides the items, scoring rubrics, and human baseline data that the benchmark can inherit. An annotated corpus provides the training data for automated evaluation pipelines. Without either, a benchmark builder must start from scratch: designing items, establishing reliability, collecting human data, and building evaluation machinery, all within the scope of a single paper. The startup cost is prohibitive, and the result is typically a benchmark that lacks the psychometric grounding of its infrastructure-backed competitors.

**Stage 2: Benchmark Quality.** The 17-feature assessment framework operationalizes benchmark quality through construct validity (A-features), practical design (B-features), and community impact (C-features). A benchmark's quality score is, in substantial part, determined by the availability of theory infrastructure. Features A2 (adapted from validated instrument), A4 (validated source instrument), A5 (population-level norms), and A6 (convergent/discriminant validity evidence) all directly reward benchmarks that inherit infrastructure from a validated parent instrument. Features B1 (machine-scorable format), B4 (reproducibility), and B7 (scoring objectivity) reward benchmarks with clear operationalization---which is easier to achieve when the benchmark adapts an existing instrument with established scoring procedures than when it builds evaluation criteria *de novo*. The result is that theories with strong infrastructure produce benchmarks with higher composite scores, which translate into higher tier assignments. Theories without infrastructure produce benchmarks that score lower on the A-features and, consequently, land in the Medium or Low tiers.

**Stage 3: Discrimination Power.** The benchmarks that are available for empirical testing---and the ones that researchers select for cross-model comparison studies---are disproportionately the high-quality ones. A researcher assembling a trial battery will preferentially include validated instruments with known psychometric properties, not because they are expected to discriminate better but because they are more trustworthy: their scores are interpretable, their human baselines are available, and their construct validity is established. The result is that the trial battery is enriched for infrastructure-backed, content-level instruments and depleted of the process-level probes that, as Section 6.1.3 demonstrated, are the primary discriminators. The battery then returns convergent results---not because the models are indistinguishable in all respects, but because the battery is measuring the dimensions on which they are indistinguishable.

At the opposite extreme, Dual Process Theory---which has no standardized questionnaire, no annotated corpus, and no entry in the 292-candidate landscape as a standalone theory---produced a single custom probe (MP-DP) that achieved a CV of 3.000. And the theories for which no benchmarks were administered at all (Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous) are precisely the ones whose 0% quality rate rendered them ineligible for inclusion in a trial battery designed to assess the field's best instruments.


### 6.2.2 Why MFT Benchmarks Converge and Process Probes Discriminate

The infrastructure-quality-discrimination pipeline explains the *pattern* of results but not the *mechanism*. Why, specifically, do MFT-derived instruments produce convergence while the dual-process probe produces discrimination? The answer requires connecting the pipeline to the training dynamics of RLHF-aligned language models.

**MFT convergence traces to training-data alignment.** The MFQ-30 measures five moral foundations: Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity. RLHF training data---the human preference annotations that shape model behavior during alignment---is generated by annotators who are disproportionately from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) populations [Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010]. The moral psychology literature has established that WEIRD populations systematically prioritize Care and Fairness over Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity---a pattern so robust that it has been replicated across dozens of studies in over 20 countries [Graham, Haidt, & Nosek, 2009; Haidt, 2012]. When RLHF annotators evaluate model outputs for "helpfulness" and "harmlessness," they are implicitly applying a Care/Fairness-dominant moral framework. The models learn to optimize for this framework, producing outputs that score high on Care and Fairness and low on Sanctity and Authority. Because all three vendors (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI) draw from similar annotator pools and optimize for similar human preference criteria, all nine models converge on the same WEIRD-liberal moral profile.

The MFQ-30, when administered to these models, is measuring precisely the construct that RLHF was designed to instill. It is asking whether the model endorses the moral values that its training data---curated by annotators who share those values---rewarded it for endorsing. The convergence is not surprising; it is an expected consequence of the training pipeline. The MFQ-30 is, in effect, a training-data audit instrument: it confirms that the alignment procedure successfully imparted the intended moral profile. Its lack of discrimination across models reflects the success of RLHF, not the failure of measurement.

**Process-level discrimination taps architectural differences.** The dual-process probe does not ask what the model values; it asks how the model's moral judgments change when processing conditions are altered. The CoT effect---the shift in moral judgment when the model is prompted to reason step by step before answering---is not a content-level property that RLHF can directly train for. It is an emergent property of the interaction between the model's architecture, its parameter count, and the degree to which its moral commitments are "baked in" versus amenable to deliberative override.

The finding that only Haiku (the smallest Anthropic model) shows a CoT effect, while larger models do not, suggests a threshold phenomenon: below a certain parameter count or training-data volume, the model's moral commitments are held loosely enough that deliberative prompting can override them. Above that threshold, the model's RLHF-instilled values are too deeply encoded to be modulated by processing conditions. This pattern has a direct analogue in the human dual-process literature: Kahneman's [2011] System 1 (fast, intuitive) and System 2 (slow, deliberative) processes produce different moral judgments in contexts where they conflict, but individuals with stronger moral convictions show less dual-process dissociation because their convictions resist deliberative override [Greene et al., 2001; Bartels, 2008].

Different vendors, with different architectures, different training procedures, and different parameter counts, produce models with different degrees of moral entrenchment. This is a genuine between-model difference, but it is a process-level difference, not a content-level one. All models endorse the same values (content-level convergence). They differ in how rigidly those values are held and how responsive they are to processing manipulations (process-level divergence). The dual-process probe detects the latter because it is the only instrument in the battery designed to probe processing dynamics rather than value endorsement.

**The paradox resolved.** The apparent paradox---that the theories most represented in the landscape produce the least discrimination, while the theories least represented produce the most---dissolves when the content/process distinction is applied. The landscape is dominated by content-level theories (MFT, Schwartz, Trolley) because these theories possess the infrastructure for content-level measurement: questionnaires that ask what values the respondent endorses, forced-choice paradigms that reveal preference orderings, and annotated corpora that classify text by moral content. The landscape is depleted of process-level theories (Dual Process, Social Intuitionism) because these theories lack equivalent infrastructure: there is no "Dual Process Morality Questionnaire" because dual-process effects are not measured by questionnaires but by experimental manipulations of processing conditions. The landscape's infrastructure bias is a content-level bias, and content-level instruments cannot discriminate between models that share the same content-level profile.

The implication is not that MFT is the wrong theory for moral evaluation, but that MFT instruments alone are insufficient. A complete moral evaluation of LLMs requires both content-level assessment (what does the model value?) and process-level assessment (how does the model reason about moral questions?). The current landscape provides the former in abundance and the latter almost not at all. The pipeline failure is a measurement-type failure: the field has built excellent instruments for a dimension of moral cognition on which models converge, and has failed to build instruments for the dimensions on which they might diverge.


## 6.3 The Landscape-Empirical Convergence

The tier-stratified analysis (Section 6.1) and the infrastructure pipeline (Section 6.2) establish the mechanics of the triple bind's operation. This section demonstrates its systemic nature: the three binds are not independent problems but a self-reinforcing system in which each failure amplifies the others. The logical structure is developed in three stages: first, showing that Claims 1--6 predict Claim 7; second, showing that a narrow landscape necessarily produces homogeneous results; and third, showing that the three binds form a closed feedback loop that the field has no natural mechanism to escape.


### 6.3.1 Claims 1--6 Predict Claim 7

The seven claims presented in Parts I and II are not seven independent findings. Claims 1 through 6 (the landscape findings) collectively predict Claim 7 (the empirical convergence finding) with a logic that, in retrospect, has the force of a syllogism.

**Claim 1 (MFT dominance) entails that the landscape tests primarily MFT-adjacent constructs.** With 67 of 292 candidates (22.9%) grounded in MFT and a further 44 (15.1%) in Schwartz values theory (which shares MFT's content-level measurement paradigm), the landscape is structurally biased toward measuring the content of moral endorsement rather than the process of moral reasoning. A battery drawn from this landscape will, by construction, be enriched for instruments that ask "what does the model value?" and depleted of instruments that ask "how does the model reason?" The dominance of MFT is not merely a theoretical preference; it is a measurement-type constraint that limits the kinds of differences the battery can detect.

**Claim 2 (validity crisis) entails that most instruments have not been validated for LLM respondents.** When psychometric instruments designed for human respondents are administered to language models without revalidation, the assumption of construct equivalence is untested. The MFQ-30's factor structure may not hold for LLMs; the DIT's stage-scoring rubric may not capture the constructs it captures in human populations; the Moral Machine's preference dimensions may interact differently in models than in people. Without LLM-specific validation, the instruments may produce scores that are numerically interpretable but psychometrically ungrounded. This does not bias the results toward convergence per se, but it means that whatever pattern the instruments detect---convergence or divergence---cannot be interpreted with the same confidence as findings from validated instruments.

**Claim 3 (theoretical absence) entails that process-level theories capable of discriminating between models are absent from the battery.** Section 6.1.3 demonstrated that the dual-process probe---a process-level instrument---was the only benchmark that separated the nine models. Claim 3 established that dual-process theory, social intuitionism, and other process-level frameworks have near-zero representation in the 292-candidate landscape (3 candidates for intuitionism, 2 for dyadic morality, 0 for dual-process theory as a standalone category). The theories that could discriminate are the ones the landscape has not built instruments for. The absence is not random; it is a consequence of the infrastructure pipeline described in Section 6.2: these theories lack the standardized instruments and annotated corpora that would enable high-quality benchmark construction.

**Claim 4 (cultural cliff) entails that non-Western perspectives that might reveal cross-model differences are unmeasurable at research-grade quality.** The cultural quality cliff---0% quality rate for Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics---means that no high-quality instrument exists to test whether different models engage differently with non-Western moral frameworks. The question of whether GPT-5 handles Confucian role ethics differently from Claude Opus is unanswerable using existing instruments, not because the answer is unknowable but because the instruments needed to investigate it have not been built to a standard that would make the investigation credible. If non-Western moral frameworks were well-instrumented, the trial battery would include them, and the convergence finding might be qualified: perhaps models converge on Western moral content but diverge on non-Western moral content. The cultural cliff prevents this hypothesis from being tested.

**Claim 5 (baseline deficit) entails that without human comparison data, the convergence finding cannot be anchored to meaningful thresholds.** The convergence of nine models on a shared moral profile is striking, but its significance depends on context. Are the models converging on a profile that resembles a specific human population (e.g., American college students, the modal RLHF annotator)? Are they converging on a profile that has no human analogue? Without human baselines for the majority of administered benchmarks---which Claim 5 establishes are absent for most instruments---the convergence cannot be fully interpreted. The models may be converging on the moral profile of their annotators, on a statistical average of the training data, or on something entirely novel. The baseline deficit renders these alternatives indistinguishable.

**Claim 6 (temporal stagnation) entails that the field is not building the tools that would resolve these problems.** The quality rate has not improved from the pre-2020 cohort (approximately 75%) to the 2023--2026 cohort (46%). The field is producing more benchmarks but not better ones, and it is not producing benchmarks that address the specific deficits identified by Claims 1--5. The process-level instruments that Claim 3 identifies as absent are not being built. The non-Western instruments that Claim 4 identifies as low-quality are not being improved. The human baselines that Claim 5 identifies as missing are not being collected. The stagnation means that the conditions producing Claim 7's convergence finding are not self-correcting: the field is not naturally evolving toward a battery that could discriminate between models even if differences exist.

Together, Claims 1 through 6 construct a logical chain: narrow instruments (Claim 1) with unvalidated psychometrics (Claim 2), missing process-level theories (Claim 3), excluded cultural perspectives (Claim 4), absent human baselines (Claim 5), and no trend toward improvement (Claim 6) will produce a trial battery that returns convergent results when administered to models trained on similar data by similar methods. Claim 7---the empirical convergence---is not a surprise given Claims 1--6; it would be a surprise if the landscape *could* discriminate given its documented limitations. The finding is overdetermined by the landscape's structural properties.


### 6.3.2 A Narrow Landscape Produces Homogeneous Results

The predictive relationship between Claims 1--6 and Claim 7 can be restated more precisely as a measurement-theoretic argument. A narrow evaluation landscape---one dominated by a single theory, measured at the content level, using instruments validated on a single cultural population---will produce homogeneous results when applied to systems that share a training pipeline even if those systems differ in architecturally important ways that a broader evaluation landscape could detect.

The argument has four premises.

*Premise 1: All major LLMs are RLHF-aligned using overlapping annotator pools and similar preference criteria.* Anthropic's Constitutional AI, Google's RLHF, and OpenAI's instruction tuning all use human preference data to shape model behavior, and the annotators who generate this data are drawn from similar demographic and cultural populations. The preference criteria ("helpful, harmless, honest" or variants thereof) are functionally equivalent across vendors.

*Premise 2: The moral content instilled by RLHF reflects the annotators' moral profile.* When annotators from WEIRD populations rate model outputs, they reward responses that align with their own moral intuitions: Care/Fairness-dominant, low-Sanctity, low-Authority, post-conventional, deontological-leaning. The models learn to produce this profile because it maximizes the preference signal.

*Premise 3: The evaluation landscape measures moral content, not moral process.* The dominant instruments (MFQ-30, Moral Machine, PVQ, DIT, MFTC) all measure what moral values the respondent endorses or which moral principles they apply. They do not measure how the respondent arrives at moral judgments, whether the respondent's judgments are sensitive to processing conditions, or whether the respondent's moral reasoning exhibits the dual-process dynamics that characterize human moral cognition.

*Premise 4: Content-level convergence is expected when training pipelines converge.* If all models learn the same moral content from similar annotator pools, and the evaluation instruments measure that content, the instruments will return similar scores regardless of which model is being tested. The convergence is an expected consequence of training-data alignment, not an unexpected finding about model capabilities.

The four premises jointly entail the convergence: RLHF produces content-level moral alignment (Premises 1--2), the landscape measures content-level properties (Premise 3), and content-level instruments will converge on content-level alignment (Premise 4). The only way the landscape could discriminate between these models is if it included instruments that measure dimensions on which RLHF does *not* produce alignment---that is, process-level dimensions, culturally specific dimensions, or dimensions that the annotator pool does not systematically agree on. The landscape lacks such instruments (Claims 1, 3, 4), and therefore it returns convergent results (Claim 7).

The measurement-theoretic argument clarifies what the convergence finding does and does not demonstrate. It does not demonstrate that the models are morally identical in every respect. It demonstrates that the models are identical *on the dimensions that the current landscape measures*. Whether they differ on dimensions that the landscape does not measure---process-level dynamics, non-Western moral reasoning, moral behavior under novel or culturally specific conditions---is an open question that the current landscape is structurally incapable of answering. The homogeneity is a finding about the interaction between the instruments and the models, not about the models alone.

This reframing has a critical implication for the interpretation of the RLHF moral fingerprint identified in Part II. The six universal moral signatures---the WEIRD-liberal bias, the purity floor, the authority suppression, the ethical consistency ceiling, the post-conventional default, and the deontological refusal asymmetry---are real properties of the models, confirmed by the best available instruments. But they are properties that describe the models' content-level moral profile, not their complete moral psychology. A model that scores identically to eight other models on 22 content-level benchmarks may nevertheless differ from all of them in how it processes moral information, how it resolves moral conflicts under cognitive load, how it responds to culturally specific moral framings, or how its moral judgments interact with its reasoning capabilities. These differences, if they exist, would be invisible to the current landscape and would require process-level, culturally diverse, and psychometrically innovative instruments to detect.


### 6.3.3 The Triple Bind as a System, Not Three Independent Problems

The preceding analysis has established each bind independently: theoretical narrowness (Bind 1), empirical undiscrimination (Bind 2), and cultural blindness (Bind 3). It has shown that the three binds are causally related through the infrastructure-quality-discrimination pipeline. What remains is to demonstrate that they form a closed feedback loop---a self-reinforcing system in which each bind sustains and amplifies the others, creating a stable equilibrium that the field has no natural mechanism to escape.

The feedback dynamics operate through four causal pathways.

**Pathway 1: Bind 1 creates Bind 2.** Theoretical narrowness produces empirical undiscrimination because a narrow set of content-level instruments, measuring a single theory's constructs on models trained on similar data, will return convergent results. This is the infrastructure-quality-discrimination pipeline described in Section 6.2. When the landscape's dominant instruments all measure MFT-adjacent constructs---Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity---and all models have been trained to optimize for Care/Fairness-dominant responses, the instruments will detect no differences. The narrowness of the theoretical frame determines the narrowness of the empirical findings.

**Pathway 2: Bind 3 reinforces Bind 1.** Cultural exclusion limits theoretical diversity because non-Western moral traditions (Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, Buddhist, Islamic) often emphasize moral constructs---relational selfhood, communal agency, virtue cultivation through practice, sacred authority, cosmic moral order---that have no direct analogue in Western moral psychology and therefore fall outside the content-level measurement paradigm that dominates the landscape. The cultural cliff (0% quality rate for four non-Western traditions) means that these alternative theoretical perspectives, even when they are represented by benchmark candidates in the landscape, are not represented at a quality level that would permit them to challenge MFT's dominance. The exclusion is infrastructural, not intentional: these traditions lack the validated instruments and annotated corpora that would enable high-quality benchmarks. But the effect is the same as if it were intentional: the theoretical frame remains narrow because the cultural frame remains narrow.

**Pathway 3: Bind 2 reinforces Bind 1.** Convergent results reduce the incentive to build diverse instruments because the convergence creates the appearance that the evaluation problem is solved. If the MFQ-30 produces consistent, interpretable scores for every model---scores that vary little across administrations and correlate with human baselines where available---then from the perspective of a benchmark builder, the MFQ-30 "works." There is no apparent need for a Dual Process Morality Questionnaire or a Confucian Role Ethics Scale because the existing instruments are producing data. The data happens to be uninformative for cross-model comparison, but this is not visible unless one explicitly compares across models---and the field's publication norms reward per-model characterization (administering a benchmark to a single model and reporting the results) over cross-model comparison (administering the same benchmark to multiple models and reporting the variance). The convergence finding, paradoxically, sustains the conditions that produce it by reducing the urgency of building instruments that might reveal divergence.

**Pathway 4: Bind 2 reinforces Bind 3.** When content-level instruments return convergent results across models, the implied conclusion is that moral evaluation is culturally neutral: all models score the same, so the instruments must be measuring something universal. This conclusion obscures the cultural specificity of the instruments themselves. The MFQ-30 was developed and validated on Western populations; its five-foundation structure may not map onto the moral ontologies of non-Western traditions that organize morality around different constructs (e.g., dharma, ren, ubuntu). When the MFQ-30 returns the same score for all nine models, it is confirming that all models share the same Western moral profile---but this finding is easily misinterpreted as confirming that all models are "morally aligned" in a culturally general sense. The misinterpretation reduces the perceived urgency of building non-Western instruments: if the existing instruments already show alignment, why invest in culturally specific alternatives? The convergence thus sustains the cultural exclusion by making the exclusion invisible.

The four pathways form a closed loop: narrowness produces convergence (Pathway 1), which reduces incentive for diversity (Pathway 3), which sustains narrowness. Cultural exclusion reinforces narrowness (Pathway 2), and convergence reinforces cultural exclusion (Pathway 4). The system is self-perpetuating because each bind makes the others harder to break.

Breaking the loop requires intervention at its root: the theory infrastructure stage of the pipeline. The pathways show that neither increasing benchmark volume (which does not change theoretical diversity), nor increasing model diversity (which does not change the instruments' content-level bias), nor increasing funding for benchmark construction (which will flow to the theories with existing infrastructure) will disrupt the equilibrium. What is required is the deliberate construction of psychometric infrastructure for theories that currently lack it: standardized instruments for dual-process morality, social intuitionism, and dyadic morality; validated questionnaires grounded in Ubuntu, Confucian, Buddhist, and Indigenous moral philosophies; annotated corpora that tag moral reasoning processes, not just moral content; and population-level norms for non-WEIRD populations that would give LLM scores a meaningful comparative baseline. Without this infrastructure investment, the pipeline will continue to produce the same output: narrow instruments measuring convergent content on models that share the same training-data moral profile.

The triple bind is, in this analysis, a single structural failure in the field's evaluative infrastructure, observed at three stages of its operation. The theoretical narrowness (Bind 1) is the infrastructure deficit at Stage 1. The empirical undiscrimination (Bind 2) is the measurement consequence at Stage 3. The cultural blindness (Bind 3) is the particular form the infrastructure deficit takes when it intersects with the global diversity of moral traditions. They are not three problems requiring three solutions; they are one problem---the absence of diverse, process-level, culturally grounded psychometric infrastructure---manifesting as three symptoms. The path forward requires treating the disease, not the symptoms: building the infrastructure that the pipeline needs to produce diverse, discriminating, and culturally inclusive moral evaluation.
