ABSTRACT
In addressing challenges within the field of Natural Language  Processing (NLP), supervised fine-tuning 
is an efficient technique that allows pre-trained Large Language Models to adapt to specific tasks. This is 
especially crucial for low-resource languages, such as Tibetan, where the demand for high-quality fine-
tuning datasets is particularly pronounced. This paper introduces the Tibetan Instruction-Following Dataset 
(TIFD), comprising 11,535 JSON objects, each with four attributes: a unique identifier, instructions, 
input, and output. These attributes correspond to the task’s unique ID, the instruction describing the 
task, supplementary input for the task instruction, and the response generated by GPT-4. In the data 
preprocessing phase, unsuitable data for Tibetan fine-tuning were first filtered out. To ensure the diversity of 
instructions, the Tibetan instructions were effectively vectorized using LaBSE, followed by the selection of 
diverse Tibetan instruction data using a method based on the K-Center-Greedy algorithm. After preliminary 
English-Tibetan machine translation of responses generated by GPT-4, a web program was developed to 
facilitate Tibetan proofreaders in referencing the original English and corresponding Chinese translations 
for rectifying errors in words, grammar, and structure in the Tibetan data. Subsequently, five Tibetan 
language professionals were invited to manually proofread the data, checking and modifying the data 
format and Tibetan content, and removing erroneous and meaningless tasks, thereby ensuring the quality 
and reliability of the TIFD. Using TIFD to supervise the fine-tuning of the Tibetan large language model

1.  INTRODUCTION
Early Transformer [1] -based Language Models (LMs) such as Bert [2], Bart [3], and T5 [4] were capable 
of executing basic tasks in Natural Language Processing (NLP), but their understanding of and responses 
to human instructions were suboptimal. In contrast, recent Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 [5], 
LLaMA-2 [6], and Claude [7] demonstrates a remarkable ability to accurately interpret user intent and 
generate relevant, grammatically correct, and logically coherent responses. These models, often referred to 
as Instruction-following models [8-10], are aligned models resulting from supervised fine-tuning (SFT) [11] 
of pre-trained Language Models (PLMs). Most current LLMs are predominantly pre-trained in high-resource 
languages such as English and Chinese. In the absence of large-scale pre-trained models for languages 
like Tibetan, it becomes especially critical to efficiently fine-tune open-source base models using Tibetan 
instruction datasets.
Addressing the lack of instruction fine-tuning data for low-resource languages like Tibetan, The main 
contributions of this paper are as follows.
(1) This study constructs an instruction fine-tuning dataset, TIFD, consisting of 11,535 entries for fine-
tuning a Tibetan large language model. The instructions undergo vector representation and clustering 
selection to ensure diversity. Through manual review, the accuracy and usefulness of the instructions are 
confirmed.
(2) Using TIFD, supervised fine-tuning is performed on the Tibetan large language model, TiLamb, which 
has been incrementally pre-trained based on LLaMA2-7B. The fine-tuned model’s Tibetan comprehension 
capabilities are subsequently validated.
2.  RELATED WORK
Prominent open-source base models include LLaMA [12], LLaMA-2,  Falcon [13], among others, 
with LLaMA-2 being Meta’s latest offering. LLaMA-2 was pre-trained with an extensive 2T tokens of 
publicly available data, with model parameters ranging from 7B to 70B. However, due to the lack of 
alignment, these models require fine-tuning to enhance their alignment. In the realm of supervised 
fine-tuning, Ouyang [11] and others have experimented with fine-tuning models on various tasks 
using manually annotated prompts and feedback. Similarly, Wang [14] and others have employed 
public benchmarks and datasets for supervised fine-tuning, augmented with manually or automatically 
generated instructions. However, these methods largely rely on human-written instruction data, which is 
often limited in quantity, diversity, and creativity, thus restricting the versatility of the fine-tuned models. 
To address this, Wang [14] et al. proposed the Self-Instruct framework, which generates instructions,

3.  DATA COLLECTION AND PROCESSING METHODS
3.1  Data Collection
The dataset in this study utilizes a seed pool of 175  different task-specific seed instructions derived 
from the self-instruct framework in Alpaca. LLMs automatically generate new instructions based on this 
seed pool. Subsequently, the LLMs produce responses to these instructions. The generated data, after 
undergoing a process of selection and optimization, is incorporated back into the seed pool. This iterative 
process continues until the desired volume of data is achieved.
However, different from Alpaca’s approach of employing GPT-3.5 for generating responses, this 
study utilizes the more advanced GPT-4 for data generation. For identical task instructions and inputs, 
GPT-4 demonstrates superior proficiency in text generation compared to GPT-3.5, along with significant 
enhancements in capabilities such as instruction comprehension, logical reasoning, and definition 
interpretation.
3.2  Data Selection
3.2.1  Tibetan Word Segmentation and Vectorization
In this paper, Tibetan word segmentation tools are employed to tokenize Tibetan instructions, 
aiming to meticulously differentiate various morphemes. Subsequently, the Term Frequency-Inverse 
Document Frequency (TF-IDF) algorithm is utilized to vectorize the tokenized results. This algorithm, by 
calculating the product of each term’s frequency and its rarity across the dataset, effectively quantifies 
the uniqueness of each instruction, providing a reliable quantitative foundation for subsequent data 
selection.
To further enhance the quality of semantic representation of Tibetan instructions, this paper introduces 
the Language-agnostic BERT Sentence Embedding (LaBSE) model [18] to obtain more refined sentence 
embeddings. LaBSE is a multilingual model based on the BERT architecture, which, by training on 
a large number of bilingual sentence pairs including Tibetan among 109 languages, generates high-

quality sentence-level embeddings. This is particularly suitable for semantic understanding tasks in a 
multilingual environment.
In the experimental section, this paper compares the embeddings generated by LaBSE with traditional 
TF-IDF vectors in an information retrieval task. The experimental data includes 1,000 labeled Tibetan 
instructions, each marked with relevant topic labels. This data serves to evaluate the performance of both 
methods in clustering and classification tasks. The results show that LaBSE significantly outperforms TF-IDF. 
Specifically, the method using LaBSE achieves a 15% improvement in clustering purity and an 18% 
increase in classification accuracy.
These results confirm LaBSE’s profound understanding of semantics and its cross-lingual adaptability 
when processing Tibetan data, thus the LaBSE model is chosen for vectorizing Tibetan instructions in 
this study.
3.2.2  Selection of Representative Instruction Subset
Next, to select a representative subset of instructions from the original dataset, this paper adopts a 
method based on the K-Center-Greedy algorithm [19]. The K-Center-Greedy algorithm is a well-known 
clustering optimization algorithm that ensures coverage breadth by maximizing the minimum distance 
between selected and unselected points. In this study, the algorithm is used to ensure that each selected 
command is as dissimilar as possible from the already selected instructions, thereby ensuring the overall 
diversity of the dataset.
Specifically, the algorithm initially selects a command at random from the dataset as the initial ”center.” 
Subsequently, in each iteration, it selects a command that has the maximum sum of distances to all 
instructions in the current selected set, and adds it to the selected set. This distance is calculated using 
the embedding vectors generated by the LaBSE model described in Section 3.2.1, ensuring accuracy in 
assessment and multidimensional considerations.
Furthermore, to further clarify the meaning of ”representativeness of instructions” and to quantify 
the selection criteria, this paper employs information entropy as a method to evaluate the richness of 
information in instructions. Information entropy is a statistical measure of information uncertainty, where 
high information entropy indicates a command contains a broad and evenly distributed vocabulary, 
thus providing more information. The information entropy is calculated by evaluating the distribution of 
similarity between each command and others in the dataset, as shown in Equation (1).

Through the advanced vectorization using the LaBSE model and the precise selection with the K-Center-
Greedy algorithm, we have successfully condensed the original Tibetan Instruction-Following Dataset from 
52,002 instructions down to 15,000. This reduction not only significantly decreases the volume of data but 
also ensures that the diversity and representativeness of the dataset are maintained.
3.3  Manual Review and Correction
The original dataset used by Alpaca was found to have several issues in subsequent use. For example, 
(1)  many inputs in the original dataset reference internet links, leading to hallucinatory answers from 
GPT-3.5; (2) a number of instructions are erroneously merged together in the original dataset for 
unspecified reasons; (3) some entries in the original dataset lack outputs; (4) some answers in the original 
dataset are incorrect, such as failing to accurately convert 25 meters into a kilometer measurement, with 
an estimated 80% of mathematical problem answers being incorrect.
In light of these errors in the original dataset, a thorough individual examination is conducted on 
the data generated by GPT-4 in response to the same input instructions. It is observed that the accuracy 
of GPT-4’s responses significantly improved, necessitating only minimal corrections. The instructions, 
inputs, and outputs from the original dataset are then subjected to preliminary machine translation, 
yielding corresponding Chinese and Tibetan data.In practice, the results of direct machine translation 
are often unsatisfactory, especially when dealing with specialized terminology and complex sentence 
structures. Therefore, to ensure data quality, this paper adopts a hybrid method combining machine 
translation with human review. This approach not only improves the accuracy of the translations but 
is also a cost-effective choice from an economic perspective. To further enhance translation quality, 
this paper has developed a web interface that displays the preliminary machine-translated versions in 
Chinese and Tibetan alongside the original English text. This allows multiple Tibetan language experts 
to directly reference the original and Chinese translations on the web page, making corrections to 
vocabulary use, grammar, and structure based on the machine-translated Tibetan text. This significantly 
increases the efficiency of revisions and reduces time costs while ensuring the quality and accuracy of 
the TIFD.
As illustrated in Figure 1, an example of correction involves the “instruction” field, where the 
description initially lacked the Tibetan phrase “

instruction more coherent and explicit. Furthermore, the word “

is replaced with the more contextually appropriate Tibetan word “

the overall semantic accuracy. In addition, the proofreaders eliminate erroneous and meaningless tasks, 
resulting in a total of 11,535 valid entries retained in the TIFD.

5.  DATA USAGE
The TIFD is a Tibetan language dataset specifically designed for SFT of PLMs. The TIFD focuses on instruction-
following, containing three key attributes: instruction, input, and output. The dataset provides PLMs with 
rich, task-specific training samples, enabling them to effectively learn and master terminologies and task 
instructions unique to the Tibetan domain. Each sample in the dataset is assigned a unique identifier, 
facilitating effective data management and precise analysis. Utilizing the TIFD for SFT with PLMs 
significantly enhances the models’ performance and adaptability in handling specific Tibetan language tasks.

In recent experiments, this paper solely utilizes the TIFD dataset to perform supervised finetuning 
on the large Tibetan language model base, TiLamb [20], resulting in a Tibetan dialogue model. 
TiLamb, based on the open-source LLaMA2-7B model, expands the existing LLaMA2 vocabulary 
by adding approximately 30,000 Tibetan tokens, thereby enhancing Tibetan encoding efficiency 
and semantic understanding capabilities. It is developed using 26.43GB of Tibetan data through 
incremental pre-training. Due to the limited Tibetan pre-training corpus and TIFD fine-tuning data, 
although it cannot effectively respond to all Tibetan commands, the fine-tuned TiLamb significantly 
improves command compliance and Tibetan dialogue capabilities. Examples of some dialogues are 
shown in Table 2 below.
6.  VALUE OF DATA
In contrast to high-resource languages like English and Chinese, the low-resource nature of Tibetan 
presents a significant challenge for the effective fine-tuning of PLMs due to the scarcity of annotated data 
available for training and fine-tuning. In this paper, the TIFD presented in this paper offers a valuable 
resource for researchers, helping to bridge the gap in this field of study and promote the development of 
NLP technology in Tibetan.
