# A SCHEMATIC FOR APPROVAL REGULATION OF FRONTIER AI

## Metadata

**Authors:** Salvador, Cole

**Type:** journalArticle

## Links

- **Arxiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06210

## Abstract

Recent and unremitting capability advances have been accompanied by calls for comprehensive, rather than patchwork, regulation of frontier artificial intelligence (AI). Approval regulation is emerging as a promising candidate. An approval regulation scheme is one in which a firm cannot legally market, or in some cases develop, a product without explicit approval from a regulator on the basis of experiments performed upon the product that demonstrate its safety. This approach is used successfully by the FDA and FAA. Further, its application to frontier AI has been publicly supported by many prominent stakeholders. This report proposes an approval regulation schematic for only the largest AI projects in which scrutiny begins before training and continues through to post-deployment monitoring. The centerpieces of the schematic are two major approval gates, the first requiring approval for large-scale training and the second for deployment. Five main challenges make implementation difficult: noncompliance through unsanctioned deployment, specification of deployment readiness requirements, reliable model experimentation, filtering out safe models before the process, and minimizing regulatory overhead. This report makes a number of crucial recommendations to increase the feasibility of approval regulation, some of which must be followed urgently if such a regime is to succeed in the near future. Further recommendations, produced by this report's analysis, may improve the effectiveness of any regulatory regime for frontier AI.

## Detailed Summary

This 61-page policy paper proposes an FDA/FAA-style approval regulation framework for frontier AI systems. The author argues that existing governance approaches (voluntary commitments, post-hoc liability, sectoral regulations) are insufficient for managing the unique risks posed by highly capable AI systems. The proposed framework would require developers to obtain pre-deployment approval for AI systems exceeding specified capability thresholds, similar to how the FDA regulates pharmaceuticals or the FAA certifies aircraft.

The paper presents a detailed regulatory architecture including: capability thresholds for triggering oversight, training authorization requirements, deployment approval gates, ongoing monitoring obligations, and enforcement mechanisms. The framework distinguishes between "frontier" AI systems (those pushing capability boundaries) and routine applications, focusing regulatory burden on the former. Key innovations include treating model training itself as a regulable activity and proposing standardized safety evaluation protocols.

The significance lies in providing a concrete, implementable proposal rather than abstract principles, drawing lessons from successful regulatory regimes in other high-risk domains.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs comparative regulatory analysis, examining how existing approval regimes (FDA drug approval, FAA aircraft certification, nuclear facility licensing) address analogous challenges of high-stakes, technically complex systems. The author maps lessons from these regimes onto AI governance, adapting mechanisms for AI's unique characteristics (rapid capability gains, dual-use potential, global development). The methodology is primarily legal and policy analysis rather than empirical.

## Results Summary

This is a policy proposal without empirical results. Key proposed mechanisms include: (1) compute-based thresholds (e.g., training runs exceeding 10^26 FLOP) for triggering oversight; (2) mandatory pre-training risk assessments and training authorization; (3) staged deployment approval requiring safety testing against standardized red-team protocols; (4) ongoing monitoring obligations with mandatory incident reporting; (5) enforcement through civil penalties and potential criminal liability for willful non-compliance.

## Conclusions

The author concludes that approval regulation, while imperfect, offers the best available model for governing frontier AI risks. They acknowledge limitations including potential innovation costs, enforcement challenges with international development, and the difficulty of defining appropriate capability thresholds. Future work should address international coordination mechanisms and the design of an independent regulatory agency with adequate technical expertise.
