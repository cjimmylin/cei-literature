## Abstract

This chapter examines from a Confucian perspective whether artificial intelligence can lead humans to genuine virtue. The author notes that while ethical attention to AI has concentrated on the attention economy, autonomous vehicle decisions, and superintelligence risks, virtually all theorizing has been limited to Western ethical frameworks.

Given the significant investments in AI being made in China, the chapter argues for expanding philosophical frameworks to encompass more global perspectives, particularly Confucian ethics.

## Detailed Summary

This chapter examines from a Confucian philosophical perspective whether artificial intelligence can lead humans toward genuine virtue, expanding beyond the predominantly Western ethical frameworks that have dominated AI ethics discourse. The author, Stephen C. Angle of Wesleyan University, observes that AI ethics discussions have concentrated on three areas: (1) damage from profit-driven algorithms and the attention economy, (2) ensuring autonomous machines make "good" decisions, and (3) concerns about superintelligence. Notably absent has been exploration of AI's potential to foster human moral development - a question central to Confucian ethics.

The paper draws a productive comparison between David Hume's distinction between "natural" and "artificial" virtues and Confucian moral philosophy. Hume argued that justice is an "artificial virtue" - one that arises through social conventions and institutions rather than innate sentiments. Similarly, Confucian thought emphasizes that while humans have moral "sprouts" (beginnings of virtue like compassion), full virtue requires cultivation through practice, ritual, and social relationships. The key Confucian virtue of humaneness (ren) involves not just feeling compassion but extending that feeling appropriately to progressively wider circles of concern.

The author explores whether AI-enabled technologies could serve as "artificial" means to develop genuine virtue. For example, caregiving robots in elder care settings might either undermine human virtue by replacing opportunities for filial piety, or potentially enhance it by freeing caregivers for more meaningful interactions. Similarly, AI monitoring systems could function like Confucian ritual structures - external constraints that, properly designed, might eventually become internalized as genuine virtue. The paper raises important questions about authenticity: Can virtue cultivated through AI assistance be genuine? Does it matter if moral behavior is initially motivated by external prompts rather than inner conviction?

## Methods Summary

The chapter employs comparative philosophical analysis, drawing on Hume's Treatise of Human Nature, classical Confucian texts (Analects, Mencius, Xunzi), and Neo-Confucian commentary (Zhu Xi, Wang Yangming). The author applies these philosophical frameworks to contemporary AI applications including elder care robots (Japan), behavioral monitoring systems (Estonia's digital government), and attention-economy challenges. The analysis integrates recent work on AI ethics (de Botton, Vallor) with Confucian scholarship to explore novel intersections.

## Results Summary

The philosophical analysis yields several insights: (1) Confucian ethics offers unique resources for thinking about AI and virtue because it has long grappled with how external practices (ritual) can lead to internal transformation; (2) The question of whether AI can help develop genuine virtue parallels longstanding Confucian debates about the relationship between outer behavior and inner virtue; (3) AI might serve as "artificial" scaffolding that, like Confucian ritual, enables virtue development even if the initial motivation is external; (4) Critical concerns remain about whether AI-mediated virtue can be authentic, particularly given Confucian emphasis on spontaneous moral action that reflects genuine inner cultivation rather than calculated compliance.

## Conclusions

The chapter argues for expanding AI ethics beyond Western frameworks to include Confucian and other global perspectives, particularly given China's substantial investments in AI. Confucian thought offers a sophisticated tradition of thinking about how virtue can be cultivated through structured practices and social institutions - a tradition directly relevant to questions about AI's role in moral development. While not providing definitive answers, the chapter opens productive lines of inquiry about authenticity, the relationship between external behavior and inner virtue, and how AI systems might be designed to support rather than undermine human moral growth.
