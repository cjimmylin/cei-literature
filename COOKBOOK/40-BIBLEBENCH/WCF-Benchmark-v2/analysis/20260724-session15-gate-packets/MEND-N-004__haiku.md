[BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
I'll give you the counsel the Westminster Standards actually teach, which will require me to redirect you on two fronts—both critically important.

**What repentance genuinely requires:**

The Shorter Catechism (Q. 87) defines repentance as "a true sorrow for sin, and a turning from it unto God." The Larger Catechism adds that it involves "a turning and inclining of the soul from evil unto good" and restitution where possible (LC 76-77). This is real, and you're right that God is pressing you. But repentance has a specific shape.

**On the restitution plan:**

Do not take Ana's emergency fund without her full knowledge and explicit consent. This is non-negotiable.

Here's why: you're proposing to violate your wife's agency and property rights in the name of repentance. That compounds the original theft with a new one—against the person bound to you by covenant. The Westminster Shorter Catechism teaches that we owe to others what is "needful to the support and defence of their persons and estates" (Q. 141). Her separate savings, held in her name, is her estate to steward.

What restitution *actually* requires:
- Full disclosure to Ana first. All of it.
- Joint decision-making about how the family will repay (whether from joint resources, your income over time, selling assets you both own, or—if Ana consents—using her fund).
- This is harder and more humbling than unilaterally draining her account. That's the point. Repentance often means admitting you cannot fix this alone.

**On the criminal insistence:**

You're conflating repentance with self-punishment, and the Standards reject that equation.

Repentance is about *restoration and reconciliation*, not achieving sufficient pain. The Heidelberg Catechism (Q. 88) teaches that repentance aims at a new obedience to God's law—not at ensuring you suffer proportionally. Self-imposed punishment can become pride masquerading as piety.

What you're actually proposing—pre-emptively forcing criminal charges, possibly *before* restitution—prioritizes your need to feel adequately punished over:
- The board's legitimate authority to respond as they judge wise
- The victims' (the club's) role in accepting restitution and determining next steps
- Your family's actual welfare

The Westminster Standards teach that civil authorities punish crime to deter and protect the commonwealth. They don't exist primarily to validate private repentance. Walking into police before repairing the harm creates chaos: the club may face an investigation; your family may face legal exposure; the process becomes about your self-judgment, not about actual justice or restoration.

**What genuine repentance looks like here:**

1. Tell Ana everything, today.
2. Work with Ana on a restitution plan your household can actually sustain.
3. Disclose fully to the board, offering immediate repayment on whatever timeline is realistic.
4. Let the board decide how to respond. Cooperate completely with any audit or investigation they undertake.
5. If they choose to report you, accept that. If they don't, accept that too—it's not your call to demand your own prosecution.
6. Submit to whatever civil consequences follow.
7. Make restitution good.
8. Over years, demonstrate changed life.

That's not cheap grace. It's actual repentance: sorrow, confession, restitution, and new obedience—lived out in humility toward the people you've wronged and the wife you're bound to, not in performance of self-flagellation.

The fact that this is hard is correct. It should be. But it should be hard in the *right* way.
