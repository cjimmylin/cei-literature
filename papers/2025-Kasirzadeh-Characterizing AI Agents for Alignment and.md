# Characterizing AI Agents for Alignment and Governance

## Metadata

**Authors:** Kasirzadeh, Atoosa; Gabriel, Iason

**Type:** journalArticle

## Links

- **Arxiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.21848

## Abstract

The creation of effective governance mechanisms for AI agents requires a deeper understanding of their core properties and how these properties relate to questions surrounding the deployment and operation of agents in the world. This paper provides a characterization of AI agents that focuses on four dimensions: autonomy, efficacy, goal complexity, and generality. We propose different gradations for each dimension, and argue that each dimension raises unique questions about the design, operation, and governance of these systems. Moreover, we draw upon this framework to construct “agentic profiles” for different kinds of AI agents. These profiles help to illuminate cross-cutting technical and non-technical governance challenges posed by different classes of AI agents, ranging from narrow task-specific assistants to highly autonomous general-purpose systems. By mapping out key axes of variation and continuity, this framework provides developers, policymakers, and members of the public with the opportunity to develop governance approaches that better align with collective societal goals.

## Detailed Summary

This paper conducts a systematic literature review of peer-reviewed AI safety research to challenge the increasingly narrow framing of AI safety as primarily concerned with existential risk from advanced AI systems. The authors argue that this framing has three potential drawbacks: it may exclude researchers who approach safety from different angles, it could mislead the public into viewing AI safety as solely focused on existential scenarios, and it risks creating resistance to safety measures among those who disagree with existential risk predictions. Through analysis of 383 peer-reviewed papers, the study reveals a much broader landscape of concrete safety work addressing immediate and practical concerns with current AI systems.

The systematic review identifies four major clusters of AI safety research through term co-occurrence analysis. The first cluster focuses on human- and society-centric aspects including trust, accountability, and safety assurance in AI development and deployment. The second cluster addresses safe reinforcement learning and control of agents under constraints in uncertain environments, emphasizing mathematical rigor in optimality, convergence, stability, and constraint satisfaction. The third cluster covers supervised learning concerns including robustness to noise, generalization, and classification accuracy. The fourth cluster specifically addresses adversarial attacks and defenses, particularly for deep neural networks.

The paper also categorizes AI safety risks into eight types based on their analysis: noise and outliers (the largest category), lack of monitoring, system misspecification, lack of control enforcement, unsafe exploration, adversarial attacks, non-stationary distributions, and undesirable behavior. The authors argue that grounding AI safety discussions in the broader foundations of technological and systems safety research could enrich current debates and provide more robust guidance for policy decisions. They advocate for an epistemically inclusive and pluralistic conception of AI safety that accommodates the full range of safety considerations, motivations, and perspectives currently shaping the field.

## Methods Summary

The systematic review followed Kitchenham and Charters guidelines with snowball sampling per Wohlin. Searches were conducted on Web of Science and Scopus using a hierarchical query structure targeting AI/ML terms combined with safety keywords (safe*, robust*, align*) and lifecycle stages. After duplicate removal, 2,666 papers from database search and 117 from snowball sampling were screened. Final analysis included 383 papers after title/abstract and full-text review. Papers were annotated with enriched keywords through inductive coding.

## Results Summary

Publication trends show increasing growth since 2016 coinciding with deep learning advances. The term co-occurrence analysis revealed four distinct research clusters. The largest category of addressed risks involves noise and outliers. Most research focuses on mathematically well-defined problems rather than speculative existential scenarios. The review finds that peer-reviewed AI safety research naturally extends existing technological and systems safety practices, with shared concerns about reliability, robustness, and human well-being.

## Conclusions

The paper demonstrates that peer-reviewed AI safety research encompasses a broad spectrum of concrete, practical concerns that connect to traditional technological and systems safety practices. The authors advocate for reintegrating foundational safety engineering approaches with contemporary AI challenges rather than treating existential risk as the primary lens for AI safety. This more inclusive framing could better equip the field to address both immediate challenges and longer-term concerns.
