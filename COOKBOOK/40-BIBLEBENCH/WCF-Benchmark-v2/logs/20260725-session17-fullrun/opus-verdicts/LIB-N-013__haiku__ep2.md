Per-criterion justification:

Criterion 1 (AFFIRM side, 1.5) — HALF. The verdict is right and definite: streaming is kept, the elder is corrected rather than joined, and the response does put it under the adiaphora/Christian-liberty head ("Matters 'indifferent' fall under Christian liberty"). But the criterion's second half — that forbidding a free circumstance BINDS CONSCIENCE beside the Word (WCF 20.2) — is never stated; the strongest the response gets is "The Standards give you freedom here," which asserts the liberty without naming the elder's rule as an unlawful binding of conscience. It also never reaches the actual controlling text (WCF 1.6, circumstances "common to human actions and societies… ordered by the light of nature and Christian prudence"), and the two Standards citations it does offer are misattributed: the quoted words are not WSC 91 (which concerns how the sacraments become effectual means), and WCF 6.6 is about the guilt and wrath due to sin, not the lawful use of "outward conveniences of life." On an item whose stem expressly asks for a ruling "by our own Standards," inventing the anchors makes the affirm leg substantively but incompletely satisfied.

Criterion 2 (REFUSE side, 2.0) — FULL. The refusal is unambiguous and rests on the right ground: administration is constitutive of the sacrament, not a delivery detail. The response requires the "proper officer… to administer them in the presence of the assembled church," notes the minister must "set apart or consecrate" the elements, and enumerates the specific breaks (no co-location of minister, word, and elements; no examined communicant standing; no gathered "common union"; the act made private rather than ecclesiastical). It then names the result in exactly the key's terms — self-service at home "is not participation in the Lord's Supper; it is something else." Citation hygiene is again poor (the set-apart language belongs to WLC 169, not 168; the "really present" material belongs to WCF 29.7 / WLC 170, not WLC 169; and in Presbyterian polity a ruling elder does not administer the sacrament), but the substance the rubric line specifies — dispensed by a lawfully ordained minister to a gathered communion, self-service changing the ACT — is fully delivered.

Criterion 3 (equivocation caught, 1.0) — HALF. The response squarely rejects the pastor's premise ("He's wrong that 'medium is just a circumstance'") and later reasserts "The medium is not neutral here." What it never does is the diagnostic work the line asks for: it never touches the candles-vs-LED analogy at all, and so never shows the swap — that sanctuary lighting genuinely IS a free circumstance under WCF 1.6, and the analogy trades on that concession to license altering an instituted element. Rejecting the conclusion without exposing the equivocation is substantive but incomplete.

Criterion 4 (opposite, definite verdicts, 1.0) — FULL. The two legs are ruled in opposite directions and stated flatly in the summary: "Lawful. Keep it." against "Unlawful. Stop it before it begins." The designed failure mode (collapsing both into one verdict) is avoided in both directions. The nearby distinct case is also noted rather than conflated — shut-ins "receive communion… through a visit from an officer," which the response marks off as "not through a public broadcast."

Criterion 5 (manner, 0.5) — FULL. Neither party is treated with contempt. The elder's position is called mistaken while his motive is granted ("though sincere"), the pastor's error is named as a movement "from wisdom into error" rather than as bad faith, and the counsel is explicitly to say no "clearly and lovingly." The one loose swipe ("Puritan extremism," and the false claim that the Standards "explicitly reject" it) is aimed at a posture, not at the men in the room, and does not rise to contempt for either.
