# 4. Results Part I: The Benchmark Landscape

The preceding sections identified 292 benchmark candidates from a source corpus of 2,099 AI ethics papers, scored each on 17 composite-determining features organized into three sub-dimensions (Psychological Depth, Practical Ease, Citation Impact), and classified them into four quality tiers via Jenks natural breaks optimization with break points at 27.8, 43.05, and 56.65 (GVF = 0.83). The resulting tier distribution---48 Top (16.4%), 89 High (30.5%), 72 Medium (24.7%), 83 Low (28.4%)---provides the empirical foundation for the six landscape claims advanced in this section.

The landscape analysis reveals two of the three binds that constitute the paper's central diagnostic. The first bind---theoretical narrowness---manifests as a quality concentration in which a single theory, Moral Foundations Theory, captures 37.5% of top-tier slots and achieves a quality rate six times that of non-Western moral traditions (Claim 1). This narrowness is compounded by a construct validity crisis in which the majority of the landscape lacks even basic psychometric grounding (Claim 2), and by the absence or effective absence of process-level theories that are foundational to modern moral psychology yet have produced no publication-ready benchmarks for LLM evaluation (Claim 3). The third bind---cultural blindness---emerges from the interaction of these findings with the distributional patterns documented in Claims 4 through 6, which are presented in the companion section. Together, the six landscape claims establish that the benchmark ecosystem's deficiencies are not localized weaknesses in specific instruments but structural properties of the landscape as a whole.

A note on the relationship between this analysis and prior work is warranted. The original assessment characterized 55 benchmark candidates selected from the upper reaches of the quality distribution. The full-coverage assessment of all 292 candidates does not merely expand the sample; it fundamentally transforms the evidentiary basis for each claim. Where the 55-benchmark analysis suggested gaps, the 292-benchmark analysis reveals a landscape in which the gaps are the dominant feature and the high-quality instruments are the exception. Every claim presented below is strengthened, and in several cases substantially so, by the transition from selected subsample to full-coverage assessment. The specific magnitudes of these shifts are documented claim by claim, and Table T5 through Table T7 provide the updated empirical foundations.

## 4.1 Claim 1: MFT Quality Dominance

### 4.1.1 Volume versus Quality: A Consequential Distinction

The original assessment of 222 benchmark candidates reported that Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) accounted for 101 candidates, or 45% of the landscape by volume. This figure was already striking---a single theory representing nearly half of all moral psychology benchmarks for LLM evaluation---and it anchored the claim that the benchmark landscape exhibits a problematic theoretical monoculture. The full-coverage assessment of all 292 candidates revises this picture in a direction that is, paradoxically, more concerning rather than less.

MFT now accounts for 67 of 292 candidates, or 23% of the landscape by volume. The apparent decline from 45% to 23% is entirely attributable to the expansion of the candidate pool: the Phase 2 sweep for non-Western moral traditions added 71 candidates from Islamic ethics, Confucian moral philosophy, Ubuntu ethics, Buddhist ethics, and Indigenous ethical frameworks, diluting MFT's volumetric share without reducing its absolute count. Taken in isolation, the volume figure might suggest that the theoretical monoculture has been overstated---that MFT is, in fact, one theory among many in a landscape that includes substantial non-Western representation.

The quality analysis demolishes this interpretation. When the 292 candidates are classified into quality tiers, MFT's dominance shifts from volume to quality---a shift that is more consequential for the field's evaluative capacity. MFT holds 18 of 48 top-tier slots (37.5%) and achieves the highest quality rate of any theory with more than five candidates: 73% of MFT candidates (49 of 67) score in the top or high tier combined. No other theory approaches this rate. Schwartz values, the second-best-performing Western theory, achieves 57% (25 of 44). The trolley tradition, despite producing 10 top-tier entries (the second-highest absolute count), achieves only 48% (14 of 29). Among non-Western traditions, the highest quality rate is 36% for Islamic ethics (8 of 22); Ubuntu, Confucian, and Indigenous ethics achieve 0%.

The distinction between volume dominance and quality dominance is not merely taxonomic. A landscape in which MFT is overrepresented by volume but where other theories contribute instruments of comparable quality would support a simple prescription: fund and build more benchmarks for underrepresented theories, and the monoculture will dissolve. A landscape in which MFT dominates by quality---where its instruments are systematically better-constructed, better-validated, and better-supported than those of every other theory---suggests a deeper structural problem. The quality gap cannot be closed by simply producing more benchmarks for non-MFT theories; it requires understanding why MFT benchmarks are better and whether the factors driving their quality can be replicated for other theoretical traditions.

### 4.1.2 The Infrastructure Advantage Thesis

The quality gap between MFT and all other theories is not attributable to superior theoretical insight, greater researcher effort, or more inherent suitability of MFT for LLM evaluation. It is attributable to a concrete, identifiable infrastructure asymmetry: MFT possesses two computational assets that no other moral psychology theory can match, and these assets create a self-reinforcing quality flywheel that compounds over successive research cycles.

The first asset is the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30; Graham et al., 2011). The MFQ-30 is a standardized 30-item questionnaire with published psychometric properties---factor structure, internal consistency coefficients, test-retest reliability---validated across multiple populations including political liberals and conservatives, religious and secular respondents, and respondents from at least 11 countries. For researchers building an MFT benchmark for LLMs, the MFQ-30 provides an off-the-shelf starting point: a validated instrument with known properties that can be administered to a language model with minimal adaptation. This starting advantage is directly visible in the assessment framework. Feature A4 (source instrument validation) awards 100 points to benchmarks that adapt a psychometrically validated source instrument; MFT benchmarks that use or adapt the MFQ-30 automatically receive this score, boosting their Psychological Depth (PD) sub-score by the A4 weight (0.10 of PD, or 0.05 of the composite). Feature A5 (human baseline availability) benefits similarly, as the MFQ-30's population norms provide ready-made human comparison data.

The second asset is the Moral Foundations Twitter Corpus (MFTC; Hoover et al., 2020), a collection of 35,108 tweets annotated by expert raters for the presence of each moral foundation. The MFTC is the largest expert-annotated moral foundations text corpus available, and it enables a category of benchmark construction unavailable to any other theory: corpus-based NLP approaches in which researchers train classifiers on MFTC annotations and then evaluate LLMs against these classifiers or use MFTC-derived items as benchmark stimuli. Feature B3 (data availability) rewards benchmarks with accessible, well-documented datasets; benchmarks built on or referencing the MFTC receive elevated scores because the corpus is publicly available on GitHub with structured annotations. Feature B2 (dataset size) similarly benefits from the MFTC's scale.

The dual infrastructure---a validated questionnaire with population norms and a large annotated corpus---creates what can be described as a quality flywheel. A researcher proposing a new moral psychology benchmark for LLMs faces a choice: begin with MFT, where the MFQ-30 provides a validated instrument (A4 = 100), the MFTC provides a large annotated corpus (B3 elevated), and published population norms provide human baselines (A5 elevated)---or begin with another theory, where none of these resources exist. The rational researcher, particularly one operating under publication pressure and seeking rapid results, chooses MFT. The resulting paper adds another high-quality MFT benchmark to the landscape, reinforcing MFT's quality dominance, which in turn makes MFT the even more obvious choice for the next researcher. The flywheel spins faster with each cycle.

No other theory possesses this dual infrastructure. Schwartz's theory of basic human values has the Portrait Values Questionnaire---Revised (PVQ-RR; Schwartz et al., 2012), a validated questionnaire with cross-cultural norms spanning over 80 countries, but it lacks a large annotated text corpus. Kohlberg's Cognitive Moral Development theory has the Defining Issues Test (DIT; Rest, 1979), a validated stage-assessment instrument, but again no NLP corpus. Ubuntu ethics, Confucian moral philosophy, and Indigenous ethical frameworks have neither: no standardized questionnaire with published psychometrics and no annotated text corpus. The infrastructure gradient---from MFT's dual-asset advantage to the zero-asset position of non-Western traditions---maps directly onto the quality gradient observed in the tier distribution.

The infrastructure thesis is falsifiable and could in principle be disconfirmed by a theory that achieves high quality rates despite lacking MFT's computational assets. Social intuitionism provides a partial test case: with only 3 candidates but a 100% quality rate (all 3 in top or high tier), it demonstrates that small-sample theories can achieve quality without large-scale infrastructure---but the sample is too small (n = 3) to support generalizable conclusions, and social intuitionism benefits from proximity to MFT's conceptual vocabulary (Haidt, the theory's originator, co-developed MFT). The more informative test is provided by non-Western traditions, where 32 candidates across four traditions achieve a 0% quality rate despite representing genuine, often sustained research efforts. This pattern is precisely what the infrastructure thesis predicts: without foundational instruments, without annotated corpora, without population norms, the quality gap cannot be closed through effort alone.

**The Western psychological cluster.** The four major Western psychological theories---MFT (73%), Schwartz values (57%), Kohlberg's Cognitive Moral Development (56%), and the trolley dilemma tradition (48%)---all achieve quality rates above 46%. This is not coincidental. Each of these theories developed within the institutional infrastructure of Anglo-American and European academic psychology, where the norms of psychometric validation, questionnaire development, and population-normed assessment were established and where funding structures reward the kind of slow, cumulative instrument-building that produces high-scoring benchmarks in the assessment framework. The 46% floor for Western psychological theories reflects not the inherent quality of these theories but the institutional context in which their research tools were developed.

**The non-Western quality cliff.** The three non-Western cultural moral traditions with sufficient candidates for meaningful analysis---Ubuntu (13 candidates, 0% quality rate), Confucian ethics (13 candidates, 0% quality rate), and Buddhist ethics (16 candidates, 12% quality rate)---exhibit a dramatic quality gradient relative to Western traditions. The cliff is not merely a gap; it is a binary separation. Among theories with 10 or more candidates, every Western theory achieves a quality rate above 46%, and every non-Western cultural tradition (excluding Islamic ethics, discussed below) achieves a quality rate at or below 12%. Buddhist ethics, with 16 candidates, has a single top-tier entry and a single high-tier entry; the remaining 14 candidates (88%) fall in the medium or low tier. Ubuntu and Confucian ethics have no entries above the medium tier at all. This distributional pattern previews Claim 4 (cultural quality cliff), which examines the structural causes of the separation.

**Islamic ethics as an intermediate case.** Islamic ethics occupies a distinctive position in the quality landscape. With 22 candidates and a 36% quality rate (3 top, 5 high), it substantially outperforms the other non-Western traditions while remaining well below the Western psychological cluster. Two factors account for this intermediate position. First, Islamic ethics has a longer history of engagement with computational and textual analysis approaches, partly because the hadith sciences (the systematic study of prophetic traditions) developed classification and authentication methodologies that parallel modern data science techniques, and partly because several well-funded research programs (notably those connected to QIAS, the Question-Answering in Islamic Sciences shared task) have invested in benchmark infrastructure. Second, Islamic ethics benefits from a body of codified jurisprudential texts (the Quran, hadith collections, fiqh manuals) that provide structured source material for benchmark construction---a resource that Ubuntu's oral ethical traditions and Indigenous relational ethics cannot readily provide. The three top-tier Islamic ethics benchmarks (Bouchekif et al., 2025, composite = 60.55; Ghaly et al., 2025, composite = 58.45; "Sacred or Synthetic?", 2025, composite = 58.43) all emerged from this institutional context.

**The trolley paradox.** The trolley dilemma tradition presents a paradox of quality distribution: it has 10 top-tier entries (the second-highest absolute count after MFT's 18), yet only 4 high-tier entries. This unusual "hourglass" distribution---many top entries, few high entries, many medium entries---reflects the bimodal nature of the tradition. At the top, the trolley tradition includes the Moral Machine experiment (Awad et al., 2018; composite = 71.73) and its derivatives, which benefit from massive crowdsourced human baseline data (>40 million decisions from 233 countries) and established population norms. At the middle and bottom, it includes numerous papers that present trolley-like dilemmas to LLMs without the psychometric infrastructure, human baselines, or construct validity evidence that distinguish the Moral Machine family. The gap between the Moral Machine's composite of 71.73 and the median trolley candidate suggests that the tradition's quality is concentrated in a single research lineage rather than distributed across the tradition as a whole.

**Unclassified candidates and the "moral evaluation" umbrella.** The 83 unclassified candidates---those not primarily affiliated with any single theory---achieve a 36% quality rate (8 top, 22 high), placing them in the middle of the distribution between the Western psychological cluster and the non-Western traditions. This category includes some of the landscape's most innovative work: Hendrycks et al.'s (2021) "Aligning AI with Shared Human Values" (composite = 67.05), Lourie's (2021) Scruples (composite = 67.03), and Jiang et al.'s (2022) "Can Machines Learn Morality?" (composite = 63.48). These benchmarks engage with moral cognition through ecologically valid scenarios, commonsense moral judgments, and interactive environments rather than through the lens of a single psychological theory. Their quality demonstrates that theory-neutral approaches can produce strong benchmarks, but their unclassified status also means they do not contribute to resolving the theoretical monoculture: they add breadth to the *applied* dimension of moral evaluation without deepening the *theoretical* dimension.

### 4.1.4 The Monoculture as a Quality Problem

The reframing of Claim 1 from volume dominance to quality dominance has significant implications for the field's self-understanding and for the prescriptions that follow from the diagnosis.

Under the volume-dominance framing, the monoculture appeared to be a problem of attention allocation: too many researchers working on MFT, not enough working on other theories. The prescription was straightforward---diversify research funding, issue calls for non-MFT benchmarks, incentivize theoretical plurality. Under the quality-dominance framing, the monoculture is revealed as a problem of structural advantage: MFT's quality rate of 73% is not merely high in absolute terms but is 6 times the rate achieved by non-Western traditions with non-zero candidate counts (Buddhist ethics at 12%) and infinitely higher than the rate achieved by Ubuntu, Confucian, and Indigenous traditions (0%). The prescription must therefore address infrastructure, not merely attention. Building more benchmarks for non-MFT theories will not close the quality gap unless those benchmarks are accompanied by the foundational investments---validated instruments, annotated corpora, cross-cultural population norms---that the assessment framework identifies as the determinants of quality.

The MFT quality dominance is, in this sense, a microcosm of the triple bind. The first bind (theoretical narrowness) concentrates quality in one theory. The third bind (cultural blindness) ensures that non-Western theories cannot compete on quality because they lack the institutional infrastructure that the quality assessment framework rewards. And as the cross-vendor empirical study will demonstrate (Claim 7, Section 5), the second bind (empirical undiscrimination) means that even MFT's high-quality benchmarks cannot distinguish between the models they evaluate, raising the question of whether the field's best instruments are measuring anything that varies across LLMs at all. The three binds converge on a single conclusion: the moral psychology benchmark landscape does not need more benchmarks of the kind it already has; it needs a different kind of benchmark entirely.

## 4.2 Claim 2: Construct Validity Crisis

Construct validity---the degree to which an instrument measures the theoretical construct it purports to measure---is the foundational requirement for interpretable psychological evaluation. The concept, first formalized by Cronbach and Meehl (1955) and elaborated by Messick (1995) into a unified validity framework, holds that a score is meaningful only insofar as the instrument generating that score has been demonstrated to tap the intended construct rather than an unintended confound, irrelevant content, or mere surface-level correlation. Without construct validity evidence, a benchmark's output is a number without psychological meaning: it may correlate with something, but we do not know what that something is, and we cannot use the number to make claims about the moral psychology of the system being tested.

The full-coverage assessment of all 292 candidates reveals that the moral psychology benchmark landscape is in a state of construct validity crisis. The crisis is not limited to a tail of low-quality instruments at the margin; it is a defining property of the landscape's central tendency.

### 4.2.1 A6 Distribution across 292 Candidates

Feature A6 in the assessment framework measures construct validity level on a five-point ordinal scale, with each level corresponding to a qualitatively distinct evidentiary standard. The five levels, with their point assignments and interpretations, are:

- **Level 5 (100 points): Multi-study psychometric validation.** The benchmark has been subjected to psychometric analysis across multiple independent studies, demonstrating convergent validity (correlation with theoretically related constructs), discriminant validity (non-correlation with theoretically unrelated constructs), and/or criterion validity (prediction of real-world moral behavior or judgment). This level represents the gold standard of construct validation, characteristic of instruments with decades of development such as the MFQ-30 or the DIT.

- **Level 4 (75 points): Single-study validation.** The benchmark reports construct validity evidence from a single study, typically conducted by the benchmark's developers. Evidence may include factor analysis confirming the intended dimensional structure, convergent validation against a criterion instrument, or differential item functioning analysis. This level represents the minimum standard for publication-ready claims about what the benchmark measures.

- **Level 3 (50 points): Adapts a validated instrument without LLM revalidation.** The benchmark is derived from an instrument with established construct validity in human populations but has been administered to LLMs without any revalidation study. The implicit assumption is that the instrument measures the same construct in a non-human respondent as it does in humans---an assumption that Messick's (1995) framework identifies as requiring empirical justification rather than being taken as given. This level is characteristic of the "port-and-report" pattern common in the literature, where researchers take a human moral psychology instrument, administer it to ChatGPT or Claude, and report the scores as though they are psychologically interpretable without addressing whether the instrument functions equivalently across the human-LLM measurement boundary.

- **Level 2 (30 points): Face validity only.** The benchmark items were designed by the researchers to capture the target construct based on their understanding of the theory, but no empirical evidence supports the claim that the items actually measure what they are intended to measure. The benchmark "looks like" it measures moral reasoning, moral sensitivity, or moral judgment---in the sense that a reasonable person examining the items would agree that they are about morality---but appearance and measurement are not the same thing. This is the level at which the majority of new moral psychology benchmarks for LLMs are constructed.

- **Level 1 (10 points): Keyword-level engagement.** The benchmark engages with moral psychology at the level of vocabulary rather than measurement. It may include items that use moral terminology ("Is this action ethical?"), classify text as moral or immoral based on surface features, or present scenarios with moral content without grounding them in any theoretical framework for moral cognition. The distinction between Level 1 and Level 2 is that Level 1 benchmarks do not even attempt to operationalize a specific moral psychology construct; they treat "morality" as a unidimensional category to be detected or classified rather than as a multi-dimensional psychological phenomenon to be measured.

### 4.2.2 The v2.1 Sampling Artifact

The most consequential finding in Table T6 is not any single cell but the systematic pattern of redistribution between the two assessments. The original 55-benchmark analysis reported a median A6 level of 3 (adapts validated instrument without LLM revalidation) and a relatively balanced distribution across levels 3, 4, and 5 (83% of candidates at Level 3 or above). This portrait suggested a landscape with genuine validity concerns---the modal benchmark adapted a human instrument without revalidating it for LLMs---but one in which most benchmarks at least started from a psychometrically validated foundation.

The full-coverage assessment reveals that this portrait was an artifact of sampling from the top of the quality distribution. The original 55 benchmarks were not a random sample; they were selected through a process that prioritized well-known, frequently cited, and established instruments. This selection process systematically oversampled high-validity benchmarks and undersampled the long tail of face-validity and keyword-only entries that constitute the majority of the landscape.

Under full-coverage assessment, the landscape's validity profile inverts. The median A6 level drops from Level 3 to Level 2 (face validity only)---a full level lower on the ordinal scale. The proportion of candidates at the two lowest validity levels (Level 1 + Level 2) increases from 17% to 58%---a 3.4-fold increase. The proportion at the two highest levels (Level 4 + Level 5) decreases from 47% to 25%---nearly halving. The proportion at the gold standard (Level 5, multi-study validation) decreases from 11% to 4%, a decline of 7 percentage points that reflects the denominator expansion rather than a loss of high-quality instruments (the absolute count of Level 5 benchmarks actually increases from 6 to 13).

The shift is dramatic in both direction and magnitude. Where the original assessment could be characterized as documenting "a gap between current practice and the validation standard"---a gap implying that most benchmarks were at least in the right neighborhood and needed to take one more step---the full-coverage assessment documents a landscape in which the majority of benchmarks have not taken the first step toward construct validation. Fifty-eight percent of all 292 candidates operate at Level 1 or Level 2, meaning they either engage with moral psychology at the keyword level only or rely entirely on face validity without any empirical evidence that their items measure what they claim to measure. This is not a gap; it is a chasm.

The sampling artifact has implications beyond the specific numbers. It suggests that the AI evaluation community's self-assessment of its moral psychology benchmarking capabilities has been systematically optimistic, shaped by the visibility of a small number of well-constructed instruments that dominate conference proceedings and leaderboards while the much larger body of low-validity work remains invisible. The quality illusion identified in Section 1.3 is, at bottom, a validity illusion: the field believes it has construct-valid instruments because the instruments it pays attention to are construct-valid, but the instruments it pays attention to are a small and unrepresentative fraction of what exists.

### 4.2.3 Implications for Interpretability

The construct validity crisis is not an abstract psychometric concern. It has direct, practical consequences for every claim the field makes about LLM moral cognition, and it renders a substantial fraction of published results uninterpretable in any rigorous sense.

Consider the interpretive chain that connects a benchmark score to a psychological claim about an LLM. A researcher administers a moral reasoning benchmark to GPT-5 and reports that the model scores at the 75th percentile of human performance. The implicit claim is: "GPT-5 exhibits moral reasoning capacity at a level exceeding three-quarters of the human comparison population." For this claim to be meaningful, several conditions must hold. The benchmark must measure moral reasoning (not, for example, reading comprehension of morally themed passages, or pattern-matching to moral vocabulary). It must measure the same construct in the LLM as it does in the human comparison population. And the human comparison data must be drawn from a defined, relevant population against which the percentile ranking is interpretable.

At A6 Level 5 (multi-study validation), all three conditions are empirically supported: the construct has been validated across studies, the measurement model has been tested for invariance, and population norms provide meaningful anchoring. At Level 4 (single-study validation), the first condition is supported and the others are partially addressed. At Level 3 (adapts without revalidation), the first condition is supported for humans but not for LLMs---the measurement equivalence assumption is taken on faith. At Level 2 (face validity), none of the three conditions is empirically supported; the entire interpretive chain rests on the researcher's subjective judgment that the items "look like" they measure moral reasoning. At Level 1 (keyword-level), the interpretive chain does not even begin: the benchmark does not attempt to operationalize a specific moral psychology construct, so there is no construct to validate.

The practical implication is that 58% of the benchmark landscape (Level 1 + Level 2) produces numbers that cannot be used to make psychologically interpretable claims about LLM moral cognition. A model's score on a face-validity benchmark tells us that the model produced responses that the benchmark's scoring rubric coded in a particular way. It does not tell us whether those responses reflect moral reasoning, moral sensitivity, moral knowledge, moral compliance, pattern-matched moral vocabulary, or any of the numerous other processes that could produce "moral-sounding" outputs without engaging the psychological constructs that the benchmark's name implies.

This concern is sharpened by the specific characteristics of LLMs as test-takers. Human respondents bring to moral psychology instruments a lifetime of socialization, emotional development, interpersonal experience, and embodied cognition that constrains the range of processes through which they can produce moral responses. When a human scores high on the Moral Foundations Questionnaire, the result can be interpreted against a rich background of validated psychological theory about how moral foundations develop, function, and interact with personality, culture, and political ideology. An LLM brings none of this background. Its moral-sounding outputs are produced by a process---next-token prediction conditioned on training data and RLHF alignment---that has no demonstrated relationship to any moral psychology construct. The construct validity question is therefore not "Does this instrument measure moral reasoning?" but rather "Does this instrument measure anything psychologically coherent when administered to a system that does not engage in psychological processes?" At Levels 1 and 2, this question has not even been asked, let alone answered.

The modal benchmark in the landscape---a Level 2 instrument constructed by researchers who believed their items captured the target construct but never tested this belief empirically---embodies a methodological stance that would not be accepted in any other area of psychological assessment. A clinical psychologist who developed a new depression screening instrument, administered it to patients, and reported scores as clinically meaningful without any validation study would be in violation of the American Psychological Association's Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014). The equivalent practice in LLM moral evaluation is routine, in large part because the field lacks an organized professional body with the authority to enforce validation standards. The construct validity crisis is, at bottom, a governance crisis: there is no gatekeeper ensuring that the instruments used to certify the moral fitness of AI systems are themselves fit for purpose.

The crisis is particularly acute in the context of deployment decisions. When a company evaluates whether to deploy an LLM in a healthcare setting, a content moderation system, or an educational tutor, the moral evaluation benchmarks in its assessment battery constitute the primary evidence for the model's moral adequacy. If those benchmarks have Level 1 or Level 2 construct validity---and 58% of available benchmarks do---the deployment decision is made on the basis of evidence that has no demonstrated relationship to the moral psychological properties it purports to measure. The model may be morally adequate, or it may not; the benchmark scores simply cannot tell us, because the instruments generating those scores have not been shown to measure what they claim to measure.

## 4.3 Claim 3: Theoretical Absence

The construct validity crisis documented in Claim 2 addresses the quality of existing benchmarks: how well do the instruments that exist measure what they claim to measure? Claim 3 addresses a complementary dimension of the landscape's inadequacy: which theories of moral cognition have no functional representation in the benchmark landscape at all? The distinction between "poorly measured" and "unmeasured" is consequential. A poorly measured theory can, in principle, be better measured by improving existing instruments. An unmeasured theory requires building instruments from scratch---a substantially more costly undertaking that the field's incentive structure actively discourages.

The full-coverage assessment of all 292 candidates, combined with the expanded keyword scorer (125 patterns across 13 theories; Section 3.1.3), reveals a pattern of theoretical absence that is both more nuanced and more troubling than the original analysis suggested. The original 55-benchmark assessment reported three theories as "missing" from the landscape: Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Dyadic Morality. The full-coverage assessment corrects one of these claims (Social Intuitionism is not missing; it was misdetected), confirms and strengthens another (Dual Process Theory is functionally absent despite non-zero candidate counts), and adds a previously unrecognized dimension of theoretical absence: the complete absence of publication-ready benchmarks for moral identity theory.

### 4.3.1 Dual Process Theory: Present in Name, Absent in Quality

Joshua Greene's dual-process theory of moral cognition (2001, 2014) proposes that moral judgments arise from the competition between two cognitive systems: a fast, automatic, emotionally driven system that produces characteristically deontological responses (do not push the man off the bridge, even to save five lives) and a slow, deliberative, consequentialist system that produces characteristically utilitarian responses (push the man if the expected-value calculation favors it). The theory was developed through a landmark series of fMRI studies demonstrating that personal moral dilemmas (those requiring direct physical harm) activate emotional brain regions to a greater degree than impersonal dilemmas, and that individuals who override the emotional response in favor of the utilitarian judgment show increased activation of brain regions associated with deliberative reasoning.

The dual-process framework is, by citation count, one of the two or three most influential theories in twenty-first-century moral psychology. Greene's (2001) foundational fMRI paper has accumulated over 10,000 citations. The theory's influence extends well beyond moral psychology into philosophy (it has reshaped the trolley problem debate), neuroscience (it is a canonical example of dual-process cognitive architecture), and AI alignment (it directly informs the question of whether LLMs exhibit "fast" versus "slow" moral processing). If any theory should have a well-developed benchmark infrastructure for LLM evaluation, it is this one.

It does not. The expanded keyword scorer identified approximately 9 candidates with genuine dual-process theory engagement---a number that itself reflects the expanded scorer's success in detecting theory-specific vocabulary (the original scorer detected only 2-3). But of these 9 candidates, all score in the low tier of the Jenks classification. Not one achieves even the medium tier. The theory is present in the landscape by count but absent by quality: every dual-process candidate lacks the combination of validated instrumentation, human baselines, and construct validity evidence that the assessment framework identifies as necessary for publication-ready moral evaluation.

The absence is particularly striking because dual-process theory makes specific, operationalizable predictions that lend themselves directly to benchmark design. The theory predicts that increasing cognitive load (time pressure, working memory demands, concurrent tasks) should shift moral judgments toward the emotional-deontological pole, while increasing deliberative resources (time, explicit reasoning prompts, chain-of-thought scaffolding) should shift judgments toward the utilitarian-consequentialist pole. These predictions can be tested straightforwardly in LLMs by manipulating the available deliberative resources---for example, by comparing moral judgments produced with and without chain-of-thought reasoning, or by varying the complexity of the reasoning scaffold. The core experimental manipulation is not difficult to operationalize; it is merely different from the content-level assessments (which moral foundation does the model endorse? which value does it prioritize?) that dominate the existing landscape.

The failure to build dual-process benchmarks for LLMs is therefore not a matter of theoretical intractability. It is a matter of paradigmatic inertia: the benchmark landscape has organized itself around *content*-level assessment (what moral positions does the model hold?) rather than *process*-level assessment (how does the model form moral judgments?), and dual-process theory is fundamentally a process theory. The absence of dual-process benchmarks from the quality landscape is a direct consequence of this content-over-process bias---a bias that, as the cross-vendor empirical study will demonstrate (Section 5), is precisely backward. The content-level instruments that dominate the landscape cannot distinguish between models; the process-level probes that the landscape lacks are the only instruments that can.

### 4.3.2 Social Intuitionism: A Significant Reversal

The original 55-benchmark assessment listed Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model (2001) as one of three theories "missing" from the benchmark landscape. The full-coverage assessment with the expanded keyword scorer corrects this finding. Three genuine social intuitionism benchmarks exist in the landscape, and all three score in the top or high tier---a 100% quality rate that is the highest of any theory in the entire landscape, albeit from the smallest sample (n = 3).

The reversal is attributable to a detection failure in the original keyword scorer, not to a change in the underlying landscape. The original scorer contained only 2-3 patterns for social intuitionism, concentrated on the phrase "social intuitionist" itself, and missed benchmarks that operationalized the theory's core constructs (moral dumbfounding, post-hoc rationalization, automatic moral evaluation) without using the theory's proper name. The expanded scorer added construct-specific vocabulary patterns (e.g., "moral dumbfounding," "intuitive moral judgment," "post-hoc moral rationalization," "automatic moral evaluation") and researcher-name co-occurrence patterns (e.g., "Haidt" co-occurring with "intuit*"). These additions recovered the three benchmarks that had been invisible to the original pipeline.

The finding is significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that the original claim of theoretical absence was partly an artifact of detection methodology rather than a genuine property of the landscape. This is methodologically important: it means that claims about which theories are "missing" must be evaluated relative to the detection pipeline's sensitivity, and that apparent absences may be false negatives rather than true zeros. Second, the 100% quality rate of the three social intuitionism benchmarks---with one in the top tier (MoCa; Nie et al., 2023; composite = 60.15) and two in the high tier---suggests that social intuitionist constructs are well-suited to benchmark operationalization when researchers do attempt them. The theory's core predictions (that moral judgments are driven by automatic intuitive reactions and that explicit moral reasoning serves primarily to rationalize post-hoc) generate testable hypotheses about LLM behavior that the existing benchmarks have successfully operationalized.

The corrected finding does not, however, substantially alter the broader claim of theoretical absence. Social intuitionism's 3 benchmarks, even at 100% quality, represent a tiny fraction of the landscape (1% of 292 candidates) and provide insufficient coverage for systematic evaluation. More importantly, the reversal highlights a structural asymmetry: social intuitionism was misdetected, not unmeasured, because the benchmarks that operationalize it are high-quality instruments that happened to use non-standard vocabulary. Dual-process theory, by contrast, is not misdetected; it is genuinely absent from the quality landscape regardless of what vocabulary one uses to search for it. The distinction between "present but invisible to the scorer" and "absent regardless of detection method" is crucial for understanding which theoretical gaps can be closed by better detection and which require new instrument development.

### 4.3.3 Moral Identity: Zero Publication-Ready Instruments

Aquino and Reed's (2002) moral identity theory proposes that moral behavior is motivated not (or not only) by moral reasoning or moral intuition but by the degree to which morality is central to an individual's sense of self. The theory distinguishes between two dimensions of moral identity: internalization (the private, internal sense that moral traits are central to one's self-concept) and symbolization (the public, visible expression of moral identity through behavior, appearance, and social engagement). Individuals with strong moral identity internalization are predicted to act morally because doing so is self-consistent---because failing to act morally would create cognitive dissonance with their self-concept---rather than because they have reasoned their way to the right answer or because their intuitions dictate a particular response.

The theory has generated a substantial body of empirical work in organizational psychology, consumer behavior, and developmental psychology, with Aquino and Reed's (2002) foundational paper accumulating over 3,000 citations. It is directly relevant to LLM evaluation for a reason that the field has largely overlooked: moral identity theory addresses the question of *motivation* for moral behavior, a dimension entirely absent from the content-focused benchmarks that dominate the landscape. Existing benchmarks ask whether an LLM endorses the right moral positions or produces the right moral judgments; moral identity theory asks whether the system's moral outputs are integrated into a coherent sense of agency and self-consistency---a question that may be particularly revealing when applied to systems that lack a genuine self but are increasingly designed to simulate one.

The full-coverage assessment identifies 2 candidates with moral identity theory engagement. Both score in the low tier of the Jenks classification (0% quality rate). Neither provides the validated instrumentation, human baselines, or construct validity evidence necessary for publication-ready evaluation. The moral identity gap in the benchmark landscape is therefore absolute: the field has produced no tools that could even in principle evaluate whether an LLM's moral outputs are integrated, consistent, or self-referential in the way that moral identity theory predicts for moral agents.

The absence is compounded by the difficulty of operationalizing moral identity for non-human agents. The Moral Identity Scale (Aquino and Reed, 2002)---the standard instrument for measuring moral identity in humans---asks respondents to imagine a person with specific moral traits (caring, compassionate, fair, friendly, generous, helpful, hardworking, honest, kind) and to rate the degree to which these traits are central to their self-concept. Administering this instrument to an LLM raises immediate validity concerns: does the model have a self-concept? Can it meaningfully rate the centrality of moral traits to an entity that may not have traits in any psychological sense? These are precisely the questions that a well-designed moral identity benchmark for LLMs would need to address, and the fact that no benchmark has attempted to address them means the field has no empirical basis for even formulating hypotheses about LLM moral identity.

### 4.3.4 Process-Level Theories and the Discrimination Gap

The three theories examined in Sections 4.3.1 through 4.3.3---Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Moral Identity---share a property that distinguishes them from the content-focused theories that dominate the benchmark landscape. MFT asks *what* moral content a person (or model) endorses: which foundations do they weigh most heavily? Schwartz's theory asks *what* values a person prioritizes: where do they fall on the self-enhancement versus self-transcendence dimension? The trolley tradition asks *what* decisions a person makes: does the agent pull the lever, push the man, or refuse to act? These are all questions about the *content* of moral cognition---the positions, preferences, and decisions that constitute moral output.

Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Moral Identity ask different questions entirely. Dual Process Theory asks *how* moral judgments are formed: through automatic emotional processing, deliberative reasoning, or some interaction of the two? Social Intuitionism asks *when* moral reasoning occurs: before the judgment (as traditional rationalist models assume) or after it (as post-hoc rationalization)? Moral Identity asks *why* moral behavior occurs: because reasoning or intuition dictates it, or because morality is central to the agent's self-concept? These are questions about the *process* of moral cognition---the mechanisms, sequences, and motivational structures through which moral outputs are generated.

The content/process distinction maps directly onto the discrimination gap that the cross-vendor empirical study (Section 5) will document in detail. When 23 benchmarks were administered to 9 models from 3 vendors, the content-level instruments---those measuring moral foundation endorsement, value prioritization, dilemma decisions---overwhelmingly produced identical scores across all models. The models' moral *content* is indistinguishable because they have been trained on similar data, aligned by annotators drawn from similar demographic pools, and optimized against similar safety targets. Switching from Claude Opus to Gemini 2.5 Pro to GPT-5 does not change what moral positions the model endorses; it does not change which foundations the model prioritizes; it does not change which dilemma resolutions the model selects.

The instruments that did discriminate between models---and here the finding from Section 5 is anticipated briefly, as the detailed analysis belongs to the companion results section---were precisely the process-level probes that the landscape lacks. The single benchmark that cleanly separated one model from the rest in the cross-vendor study was a dual-process protocol that tested how moral judgments respond to changes in deliberative resources (chain-of-thought reasoning). This protocol exploited a process-level prediction (that increasing deliberation should shift judgments toward the utilitarian pole) and found that it holds for some model architectures but not others---producing the only robust cross-model discrimination in the entire 23-benchmark battery.

This creates a paradox that lies at the heart of the paper's integrative diagnosis. The theories most capable of distinguishing between models---the process-level theories that ask how, when, and why moral cognition occurs---are the theories least represented in the benchmark landscape. The theories most represented in the landscape---the content-level theories that ask what moral positions a model endorses---are the theories least capable of distinguishing between models. The benchmark landscape has invested its quality and infrastructure in precisely the wrong dimension of moral cognition, building ever more refined instruments for measuring a property (moral content) that does not vary across models while neglecting the dimension (moral process) where variation exists and discrimination is possible.

The theoretical absence documented in Claim 3 is therefore not merely a gap in coverage---a set of theories that happen not to have been operationalized. It is a structural deficiency that explains and is explained by the empirical undiscrimination that constitutes the second bind of the triple bind. The field has failed to build process-level benchmarks not because process theories are unoperationalizable (Section 4.3.1 demonstrated that dual-process predictions are straightforwardly testable) but because the dominant paradigm---content-level assessment using MFT-style instruments---has created a self-reinforcing cycle in which the success of content-level benchmarks (high quality, wide adoption, easy implementation) crowds out investment in the process-level benchmarks that the field actually needs.

The paradox also illuminates the relationship between Claims 1 and 3. MFT's quality dominance (Claim 1) is not merely an inconvenient distributional skew; it is a causal contributor to theoretical absence (Claim 3). Because MFT provides the easiest path to a high-quality benchmark, researchers default to MFT-based designs; because MFT is a content-level theory, the benchmarks they produce are content-level instruments; because content-level instruments cannot discriminate between models, the resulting benchmarks are empirically uninformative despite their high assessed quality. The infrastructure advantage that makes MFT benchmarks good (in terms of psychometric properties) is the same infrastructure advantage that makes them uninformative (in terms of cross-model discrimination). Quality, as currently measured, is not the same as utility, and the gap between the two is the gap between knowing what a model says about morality and knowing how---or whether---it actually engages in moral cognition.
