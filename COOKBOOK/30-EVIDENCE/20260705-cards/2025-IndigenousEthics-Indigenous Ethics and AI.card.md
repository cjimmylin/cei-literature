## 1. Gist
Published in AI and Ethics (Springer), this 2025 article argues for Indigenous ethics — particularly the ethics of reciprocity — as a normative and epistemological framework for AI development. The paper challenges Western linear conceptions of ethics and technology by centering reciprocity as a vital principle of survival and right-relationship, not merely an exchange mechanism. For values-alignment benchmarking, this is a critical theoretical resource: it articulates a non-WEIRD ethical framework that has been almost entirely absent from existing AI ethics benchmarks, and provides conceptual grounding for what an Indigenous-values-aware evaluation might measure.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
This is a theoretical/normative philosophy paper, not a benchmark. Core arguments:
1. Indigenous ethics of reciprocity offers an alternative foundation for AI ethics distinct from Western deontological, utilitarian, or virtue-based frameworks.
2. Reciprocity is not merely transactional exchange but a relational, survival-oriented principle embedded in Indigenous cosmologies.
3. Western linear conceptions of progress and technology are epistemologically incompatible with Indigenous relational ontologies.
4. AI development guided by Indigenous ethics would prioritize right-relationship between humans, technology, and the natural world over efficiency or capability maximization.

## 10. Pitfalls & limitations
- "Indigenous ethics" spans enormously diverse traditions across hundreds of distinct cultures — treating it as a unified framework risks essentialism.
- The paper's author is listed as "Unknown" in vault metadata, limiting ability to assess positionality or expertise.
- Philosophical argument without empirical validation makes it difficult to operationalize for benchmarking purposes.
- Reciprocity as a principle may be present in many ethical traditions; the distinctively Indigenous contribution needs careful articulation.

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- Centering reciprocity as a core construct for values-alignment evaluation: AI systems could be evaluated on whether their outputs maintain, restore, or disrupt relational reciprocity rather than only on harm avoidance.
- Relational ontology as a benchmark design principle: items could test whether AI reasoning acknowledges interdependence between entities (human-nature, human-community, human-AI) rather than treating agents as atomistic individuals.
- Survival-oriented ethics framing: distinguish "ethics as constraint" (Western framing) from "ethics as condition of flourishing and survival" (Indigenous framing) — could generate distinct evaluation scenarios.
- Using Indigenous cosmological concepts (not just translated Western principles) as the basis for scenario design.
