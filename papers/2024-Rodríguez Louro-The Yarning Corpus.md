## Abstract

The Yarning Corpus consists of acrolectal Aboriginal English data gathered in metropolitan Perth, WA. It features video-recorded interactions with 58 Aboriginal English speakers born between 1931 and 2009. Using this corpus, we sought to uncover variation and change in Aboriginal English in Nyungar Country, Southwest WA. We designed a cross-cultural research model that allowed us to document sociolinguistic aspects of Aboriginal English “yarning” – a First Nations cultural form of storytelling and conversation which we used to capture diverse voices in sociolinguistic research. The corpus features video-recorded interactions with 58 Aboriginal English speakers born between 1931 and 2009. These materials amount to 517,600 words of unscripted, interactional speech data from 36 women and 22 men aged 10–88 who speak Aboriginal English as their vernacular. We focus on speakers who use Aboriginal English as their main language of communication, and who do not speak ancestral languages fluently. The Yarning Corpus allowed us to enrich sociolinguistic research methods by utilizing an approach grounded in a relational ethic. It also made it possible to explore the quotative system of Aboriginal English, and to contribute to more inclusive models in the design of medical media and Artificial Intelligence-powered technologies for First Nations communities.

## Detailed Summary

The Yarning Corpus represents a groundbreaking contribution to sociolinguistic research on Aboriginal English, the English-lexified contact-based variety used by First Nations people in Australia. This corpus is the first large-scale systematic documentation of Aboriginal English usage in an urban setting, specifically metropolitan Perth in Nyungar Country, Southwest Western Australia. The corpus comprises 517,600 words of unscripted, interactional speech data from 58 speakers (36 women, 22 men) born between 1931 and 2009, ranging in age from 10 to 88 years at the time of recording.

The research methodology is particularly notable for its culturally grounded approach. Led by co-author Glenys Collard, a Nyungar woman, the project employed "yarning" as the primary data collection method. Yarning is a First Nations cultural form of storytelling and conversation that creates a more natural and culturally safe context for eliciting vernacular speech compared to traditional interview methods. This approach addresses known issues in sociolinguistic fieldwork with First Nations communities, including the inappropriateness of direct questioning, the phenomenon of "gratuitous concurrence," and the effects of outsider presence on speech patterns. The researchers collected data in everyday settings such as medical centres, parks, and streets where First Nations people naturally gather.

The corpus documents rich linguistic features of Aboriginal English including phonological, morpho-syntactic, semantic, and discourse-pragmatic elements, as well as culturally salient lexical items from Nyungar language. The yarning sessions captured powerful testimonies about racism, violence, the Stolen Generation, and ongoing social challenges faced by First Nations people. Beyond its primary sociolinguistic applications, the corpus has potential applications for developing more inclusive AI-powered technologies and medical media for First Nations communities.

## Methods Summary

Data collection employed video recording during unstructured yarning sessions led by Collard in various locations around metropolitan Perth. Sessions included 3-6 speakers at a time and lasted 22-60 minutes. Recordings were transcribed by a professional service and corrected by research assistants. Metadata captured included speaker demographics, language background, and kinship relationships. The methodology prioritized cultural safety by avoiding direct questioning and following First Nations protocols for respectful engagement.

## Results Summary

The corpus contains 517,600 words from 33 conversations with 58 speakers. Linguistic features documented include distinctive phonological patterns, morpho-syntactic structures (such as plural "womans"), discourse-pragmatic markers (like utterance-final "unna"), and Nyungar language terms. Thematic content centers on experiences of racism, discrimination, the Stolen Generation, family relationships, and cultural practices. The corpus enabled new research on the quotative system of Aboriginal English and has been used to improve cultural appropriateness of AI and medical communication tools.

## Conclusions

The Yarning Corpus fills a significant gap in Australian sociolinguistics by documenting urban Aboriginal English through culturally appropriate methods. The project demonstrates how First Nations leadership and relational ethics can enrich research methodology while producing valuable linguistic resources. The corpus supports both academic research on language variation and change, and practical applications in developing inclusive technologies.
