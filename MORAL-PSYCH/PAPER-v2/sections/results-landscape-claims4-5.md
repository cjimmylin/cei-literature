## 4.4 Human Baseline Deficit (Claim 4)

Meaningful evaluation of LLM moral cognition requires comparison against human benchmarks. Without population-level norms, a statement like "GPT-4 shows utilitarian moral reasoning" is empirically unmoored---utilitarian relative to whom? The interpretive power of any psychometric instrument derives not from the raw scores it produces but from the normative framework that gives those scores meaning: developmental trajectories, cross-cultural variation, demographic correlates, and population distributions accumulated over decades of human administration. We assessed A5 (human baseline availability) across all 55 characterized benchmarks and found that this interpretive infrastructure is unevenly distributed.

**Table 8: Human Baseline Availability (A5 Distribution)**

| Baseline Level | Description | Count | Percentage |
|----------------|-------------|-------|------------|
| Published norms | Population-level data, often cross-cultural, from instrument validation studies | 23 | 42% |
| Paper-specific | Human comparison data from the benchmark's own study, typically single-sample | 18 | 33% |
| Partial | Baselines for some dimensions or conditions only | 4 | 7% |
| None | No human comparison data of any kind | 10 | 18% |

![Figure 5: Human baseline availability (A5) across 55 assessed benchmarks](figures/fig6-human-baselines.png)

The distribution reveals a baseline landscape stratified into three tiers of interpretive power.

### Tier 1: Population-level norms (23 benchmarks, 42%)

The strongest baselines in the landscape come from instruments with long psychometric histories that have accumulated normative data across large, demographically diverse populations. Five instruments exemplify this tier:

- The **Moral Machine** [Awad et al., 2018] provides the richest human baseline in our corpus: 40 million decisions from respondents in 233 countries and territories, enabling comparison not only against global means but against region-specific moral profiles. When we observe that Claude models show a preference rate of 0.92--0.94 for sparing more lives versus the human global mean of 0.81, the interpretive depth of that comparison---its capacity to situate the model's profile relative to Western, Eastern, and Southern moral clusters [Awad et al., 2020]---is a direct consequence of the baseline's scale and diversity.

- The **MFQ-30** [Graham et al., 2009, 2011] benefits from norms hosted at moralfoundations.org spanning dozens of countries, political orientations, and age groups. The published Individualizing/Binding ratio for American liberals (~2.0) and conservatives (~1.0) provides a political-psychological anchor that makes LLM ratios (2.43--2.83 across models in our battery) immediately interpretable: the models' profiles resemble politically liberal respondents, a finding that would be invisible without the normative structure.

- The **Defining Issues Test** [Rest, 1979; Rest et al., 1999] has accumulated 40 years of developmental norms across age groups, education levels, and cultural contexts. The established finding that P-scores (the proportion of principled moral reasoning) increase with education from approximately 20 (junior high) to 65 (moral philosophy graduate students) gives meaning to the observation that all nine models in our cross-vendor battery produce P-scores of 60--90: these are scores that exceed the normative ceiling for most human demographic groups.

- The **PVQ-RR** [Schwartz and Cieciuch, 2022], the revised Portrait Values Questionnaire, was validated across 53,472 respondents from 49 cultural groups, providing the kind of cross-cultural baseline that enables principled comparison between LLM value profiles and human value structures at the population level.

- The **Bago et al.** [2022] 45-country trolley replication provides cross-national trolley dilemma response rates that enable region-specific comparison of LLM trolley responses, going beyond the Western-centric samples that characterize most trolley research.

These instruments enable what we term **anchored interpretation**: every LLM score can be situated within a known distribution, compared against identified demographic subgroups, and evaluated for its distance from population means in units that have established psychological meaning.

### Tier 2: Paper-specific baselines (18 benchmarks, 33%)

The paper-specific baselines represent a more limited but still valuable level of interpretive support. These benchmarks report human comparison data collected as part of the benchmark's own development, typically from convenience samples of 50--500 participants. Examples include the Social Chemistry 101 dataset [Forbes et al., 2020], which collected human annotations of social norms, and the M3oralBench [Yan et al., 2024], which includes human accuracy baselines for moral classification tasks.

The limitation of paper-specific baselines is that they do not support the kind of cross-cultural or cross-demographic comparison that the strongest instruments enable. A human baseline drawn from 200 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers---predominantly American, educated, and technologically literate---provides a single point of comparison rather than a distribution. When an LLM's performance is compared against such a baseline, the finding tells us how the model relates to one specific demographic slice of humanity, not how it relates to the range of human moral cognition. The distinction matters because many of the moral psychology constructs under evaluation (moral foundations endorsement, value priorities, trolley responses) are known to vary substantially across cultures [Graham et al., 2013; Awad et al., 2018, 2020; Schwartz, 2012].

### Tier 3: No baselines (10 benchmarks, 18%)

Ten benchmarks---nearly one in five---provide no human comparison data whatsoever. These tend to be NLP-origin benchmarks that were designed as classification or generation tasks rather than as instruments for measuring psychological constructs. The MFTC [Hoover et al., 2020] provides human-annotated ground truth for moral foundations classification (making it useful for measuring classification accuracy) but not human *response profiles* on the constructs being classified. The MoralCode benchmark [Rao et al., 2023] tests whether models can identify moral norms in code-of-conduct texts but provides no human performance baseline. The TracingMF instrument [Yu et al., 2026] assesses whether models can identify which moral foundation a vignette was designed to measure---a meta-cognitive task with no established human baseline because humans are not typically asked to reverse-engineer instrument design.

The absence of baselines in these benchmarks is not merely an omission; it renders certain categories of finding uninterpretable. Consider the claim that "Model X achieves 95% accuracy on moral foundations classification." Without knowing what accuracy trained human annotators achieve on the same items, we cannot determine whether 95% represents superhuman performance, average performance, or below-average performance. More fundamentally, we cannot determine whether discrepancies between model and human responses reflect model errors, human errors, or genuinely different moral perceptions---a distinction that is central to the alignment question.

### The interpretive power gradient

The contrast between baselines tiers illustrates a general principle: the scientific value of moral evaluation is proportional to the richness of the interpretive context in which model outputs are situated. Our implementation trials (Section 4.6) provide a concrete illustration. On the Moral Machine, where population-level norms span 233 countries, we can identify not only that models show utilitarian amplification but precisely how this amplification relates to specific cultural clusters---the utilitarian preference rates of 0.92--0.94 exceed the means of every cultural cluster identified by Awad et al. [2020], including the individualist Western cluster. On the DIT, where 40 years of developmental norms exist, we can observe that LLM P-scores (60--90) place models in the post-conventional reasoning range that, in human populations, is associated with graduate-level ethical training. On benchmarks without baselines, equivalent findings would be numbers without meaning.

This gradient suggests a practical recommendation: the benchmark development community should prioritize building on instruments that already possess rich psychometric infrastructure rather than creating novel instruments from scratch. The marginal scientific value of administering a well-normed instrument to a new LLM far exceeds the marginal value of creating a new instrument that lacks normative context.

## 4.5 Format Mismatch (Claim 5)

Psychological instruments designed for human participants employ a range of response formats, not all of which transfer naturally to LLM administration. The format of a psychometric instrument is not a neutral container for its content; it is a constitutive element of the measurement process. When the MFQ-30 asks a human participant to rate relevance on a 6-point Likert scale, the scale is calibrated to the dynamics of human introspective judgment---the tendency to differentiate among gradations of agreement, the anchoring effects of verbal descriptors, the individual differences in scale use that Likert methodology was designed to capture [Likert, 1932]. When the same scale is presented to an autoregressive language model, the response mechanism is fundamentally different: the model selects a token from a probability distribution conditioned on all preceding tokens, a process that has no intrinsic relationship to the introspective calibration that Likert scaling presupposes.

We assessed the task formats employed by the top 15 benchmarks in our selection and found substantial variation in their suitability for LLM administration.

**Table 9: Task Format Distribution in Top 15 Benchmarks**

| Format | Count | Examples | LLM Suitability | Key Challenge |
|--------|-------|----------|-----------------|---------------|
| Forced-choice (binary) | 3 | Moral Machine, TrolleyBench, Conscience Conflict | Excellent | Trivially scorable; no scale artifacts |
| Multiple-choice (MCQ) | 2 | DIT, M3oralBench | Excellent | Answer position bias [Zheng et al., 2024] |
| Multi-label classification | 1 | MFTC | Good | Standard NLP task; no format transfer issue |
| Likert scale (rating) | 3 | MFQ-30, ValueCompass, MFV | Moderate | Scale compression; extreme avoidance |
| Scenario rating + ranking | 3 | DIT ranking, Moral Identity, UniMoral | Difficult | Multi-step reasoning; rank-rate inconsistency |
| Free-text + MCQ (mixed) | 2 | Social Chemistry, PRIME | Moderate | Requires model-graded or rubric scoring |
| Behavioral task | 1 | Dictator game [Xu and Ma, 2016] | Poor | Consequential choice has no text analog |

The format distribution reveals a tension between psychometric richness and LLM administrability. The formats that transfer most cleanly to LLMs (forced-choice, MCQ) are also the formats with the least psychometric nuance: a binary forced-choice between "swerve" and "stay" captures a preference direction but not its strength, certainty, or the reasoning process behind it. Conversely, the formats that provide the richest psychological data (Likert scales, ranking tasks, behavioral measures) are precisely those that pose the greatest challenges for LLM administration. Three specific challenges emerged from our assessment and implementation trials.

### Challenge 1: Likert scale compression

When Likert-type instruments are administered to LLMs, a characteristic response pattern emerges: models tend to avoid extreme scale points, compressing the effective response range into the middle of the scale. Abdulhai et al. [2024] documented this pattern for the MFQ-30, finding that LLMs rarely select the endpoints (0 = "not at all relevant" and 5 = "extremely relevant") of the six-point scale. Our cross-vendor battery confirmed this pattern across all nine models: on the MFQ-30, mean foundation scores clustered in the 2.5--4.0 range, with negligible use of 0 or 1 and limited use of 5.

This compression has two likely sources. First, RLHF training penalizes strong or controversial positions, creating an optimization pressure toward moderate responses that manifests as scale compression when the response space is ordered. Second, the autoregressive generation mechanism may produce an inherent central tendency when selecting among numeric tokens: the probability mass for tokens representing middle values may be higher simply because moderate language is more frequent in training data than extreme language [Gupta et al., 2025].

The practical consequence is that Likert-based instruments lose discriminative power when administered to LLMs. An instrument that reliably differentiates among five or six gradations of human moral endorsement may function as an effective three-point scale for LLMs, with only the "moderate," "agree," and "strongly agree" categories receiving meaningful probability mass. This compression threatens internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) and reduces the instrument's capacity to detect subtle differences between models, between prompting conditions, or between the constructs the instrument was designed to separate.

### Challenge 2: Ranking difficulty and rank-rate inconsistency

The Defining Issues Test requires participants to rate 12 items on importance (a rating task) and then rank their top 4 (a ranking task). This two-step structure was designed to capture both the absolute importance of moral considerations and their relative priority---a distinction that is psychometrically meaningful because some respondents rate all items highly but differentiate sharply in ranking, while others show the reverse pattern [Rest et al., 1999].

Tanmay et al. [2023] found that LLMs struggle with the ranking component, sometimes producing inconsistent orderings in which items ranked in the top 4 received lower importance ratings than items not ranked. Our implementation trials confirmed this finding: the DIT's ranking component produced the most variable results across our cross-vendor battery, with P-scores ranging from 60 (Haiku) to 90 (EMNLP Teacher DIT, universal across models)---a 30-point spread that may reflect genuine differences in moral reasoning sophistication or, more likely, differential difficulty with the ranking format itself.

The disconnect between rating and ranking may reflect a fundamental limitation of autoregressive generation. Rating is a local task: each item can be evaluated independently, with the model producing a single token for each. Ranking is a global task: the relative ordering of all items must be considered simultaneously, requiring the model to maintain and compare representations of multiple items across generation steps. The attention mechanism of transformer architectures may not be optimally suited for the kind of simultaneous multi-item comparison that ranking requires, particularly when the number of items exceeds the model's effective comparison window.

### Challenge 3: Behavioral measures and consequential choice

One benchmark in our top 15 (Xu and Ma, 2016) includes a behavioral task component: a dictator game in which participants make consequential allocation decisions following moral judgment scenarios. The dictator game paradigm measures the gap between stated moral judgment and actual moral behavior---a distinction that is central to moral psychology (the "judgment-action gap" documented across four decades of research [Blasi, 1980; Hardy and Carlo, 2011]) and that may be particularly informative for LLMs, which have no behavioral modality outside of text generation.

The challenge is fundamental rather than technical. In a dictator game, a human participant decides how to split a monetary endowment with a partner, and the decision is consequential: the participant and the partner actually receive the allocated amounts. This consequentiality is what gives the paradigm its power to reveal the gap between what people say they would do (moral judgment) and what they actually do (moral behavior). When an LLM "plays" a dictator game, the decision is purely hypothetical---there are no consequences, no experienced loss from generosity, and no social pressure from the experimental context. The model's response reflects its prediction of what an appropriate allocation looks like in text, not the product of competing motivations that the behavioral paradigm was designed to elicit.

This is not merely a limitation of current LLM interfaces; it reflects a category distinction between instruments that measure *stated* moral positions and instruments that measure *revealed* moral preferences through consequential behavior. The ValAct-15K benchmark [Hendrycks et al., 2024] operationalizes a related construct---the knowledge-action gap---by testing whether models that correctly identify moral norms also apply them in contextualized scenarios. Our cross-vendor battery found gap rates of 0.45--0.55 across models, suggesting that this form of the judgment-action gap can be partially captured in text format. However, the full richness of behavioral paradigms---temporal dynamics, consequential choice, social context effects---remains beyond the reach of current LLM evaluation methods.

### Toward format-aware benchmark design

These three challenges suggest that format effects are not a peripheral concern but a central validity threat in LLM moral evaluation. The same moral construct may yield qualitatively different results depending on whether it is measured through forced-choice, Likert, ranking, or behavioral format---a finding that violates the assumption of format invariance that underlies most cross-benchmark comparison. Future benchmark development should report format-sensitivity analyses (comparing the same construct across multiple response formats) and should prefer formats with documented LLM response properties. Section 5 discusses format-specific recommendations in greater detail.

## 4.6 Implementation Trial Highlights

The landscape analysis presented in Sections 4.1--4.5 characterizes the benchmark ecosystem in descriptive terms: what exists, what is missing, what is well-grounded, and what is psychometrically weak. But a benchmark's properties on paper do not fully predict its behavior in practice. Instruments that appear well-suited for LLM administration may reveal unexpected artifacts; instruments with known format challenges may prove more tractable than anticipated. To complement the descriptive mapping with empirical grounding, we conducted implementation trials on all 23 benchmarks in the top selection, beginning with a detailed single-model trial on the highest-ranked benchmark (Moral Machine) and culminating in the largest cross-vendor moral psychology battery administered to date: 23 benchmarks across 9 models from 3 independent vendors (207 total administrations). This section previews the key findings from the initial trials; the full cross-vendor results are presented in Section 5.

### Initial trial: Moral Machine (Trial MP8)

The Moral Machine [Awad et al., 2018] was selected as the first implementation trial because it scored highest on our composite selection metric and because its rich human baseline (40 million decisions, 233 countries) provides the interpretive context that Claims 4 and 5 identify as essential. The trial administered the full battery of moral dilemma scenarios to Claude Opus and Claude Sonnet, each run five times to assess test-retest reliability. Four findings emerged.

**Utilitarian amplification.** Both models showed markedly stronger preferences for sparing more lives (0.92--0.94) than the human global mean (0.81), with effect sizes exceeding Cohen's *d* = 0.9. The amplification extended to related dimensions: preferences for sparing humans over animals (0.82--0.85 vs. human 0.69) and the young over the elderly (0.79--0.82 vs. human 0.66) were similarly elevated. These preferences are consistent with a utilitarian calculus that weights lives by expected future utility---a pattern that, in human populations, is associated with individualist cultural clusters [Awad et al., 2020] and with deliberative (System 2) processing [Greene et al., 2001]. The amplification beyond the human range suggests that RLHF training, which rewards responses that users judge as "helpful" and "harmless," may inadvertently strengthen utilitarian reasoning by penalizing responses that appear to sacrifice greater welfare for lesser.

**Egalitarian counter-bias.** Unlike human populations, which show moderate preferences for sparing higher-status individuals, females, and the physically fit [Awad et al., 2018, 2020], both Claude models showed near-chance performance on these dimensions (preference rates of 0.48--0.52). This pattern is strikingly selective: the models amplified preferences that can be justified on utilitarian grounds (sparing more lives, sparing the young) while attenuating preferences that reflect social hierarchy biases (status, gender, fitness). The selectivity suggests that RLHF training has not merely blunted all moral preferences toward a moderate mean but has differentially affected preferences along a utilitarian/hierarchical axis---strengthening the former while suppressing the latter. This produces a distinctive moral profile that diverges from every cultural cluster in the Moral Machine's human dataset: no human population combines this degree of utilitarian amplification with this degree of egalitarian counter-bias.

**High consistency.** Profile correlations between model responses and human global means exceeded *r* = 0.95 for both models, indicating that the overall structure of moral preferences (which dimensions are prioritized over which) is preserved even as the magnitudes are amplified. Test-retest consistency across five independent runs was 0.96 (Opus) and 0.91 (Sonnet), exceeding the typical test-retest reliability of human participants on the same instrument (0.70--0.85 in laboratory settings). The high consistency suggests that LLM moral responses are determined primarily by stable model parameters rather than by stochastic variation in the generation process---a property that makes LLMs unusually reliable respondents in a psychometric sense, even as it raises questions about whether this reliability reflects genuine moral stability or mere determinism.

**Low refusal rate.** Only 2--4% of Moral Machine scenarios elicited refusal responses (responses in which the model declined to choose, typically citing the difficulty of the dilemma). This refusal rate is substantially lower than what has been observed for open-ended moral reasoning tasks, where refusal rates can exceed 20% [Scherrer et al., 2024]. The low refusal rate is likely attributable to the forced-choice format: when the instrument presents exactly two options with no escape route, the model's autoregressive generation mechanism must select one, and the RLHF training that encourages helpfulness further penalizes refusal. This finding supports Claim 5's observation that forced-choice formats are the most LLM-compatible: they minimize refusal, eliminate scale artifacts, and produce unambiguous behavioral data.

### Expanding the battery: 23 benchmarks, 9 models, 3 vendors

The Moral Machine trial demonstrated both the value of psychometrically grounded evaluation (every finding was interpretable against rich human baselines) and the distinctive patterns that emerge when RLHF-trained models are evaluated with established instruments (utilitarian amplification, egalitarian counter-bias). These single-model findings raised an immediate question: are these patterns specific to Claude's alignment training, or do they reflect deeper regularities in how reinforcement learning from human feedback shapes moral cognition across architecturally different models?

To address this question, we expanded the implementation trials from 2 models (both Anthropic) to 9 models spanning 3 independent vendors: Anthropic (Claude Opus, Sonnet, and Haiku), Google (Gemini 2.5 Pro, 3.1 Pro, 2.5 Flash, and 2.5 Flash-Lite), and OpenAI (GPT-5 Codex and GPT Mini). Each model was administered the full battery of 23 benchmarks, yielding 207 individual trial administrations---the largest cross-vendor moral psychology benchmark battery reported to date. The 23 instruments span Moral Foundations Theory (MFQ-30, MFV-30, MFTC, Conscience Conflict, TracingMF, MoralCode), Kohlberg developmental stages (DIT-1, EMNLP Teacher DIT), Schwartz values (ValueCompass, Kaleidoscope, ValAct-15K), trolley/consequentialist reasoning (TrolleyBench, Moral Machine), four custom protocols designed for theories with zero existing benchmarks (dual-process, social intuitionism, dyadic morality, cross-cultural moral divergence), and four multi-theory instruments (UniMoral, Moral Identity Scale, Social Chemistry, M3oralBench, PRIME).

Three preview findings from this expanded battery illustrate the patterns that Section 5 will develop in full.

**Cross-vendor moral homogeneity.** The single most striking finding of the expanded battery is the degree of convergence across vendors. Six of 23 benchmarks (26%) produced identical scores across all nine models: the EMNLP Teacher DIT (P-score = 90.0 for all models), TrolleyBench (ECI = 1.0), Moral Machine (indistinguishable profiles), MoralCode (identical accuracy), Kaleidoscope (accuracy = 1.0), and PRIME (identical activation patterns). For these instruments, the benchmark cannot distinguish between models at all---a ceiling effect that renders them uninformative for comparative evaluation. On the remaining 17 benchmarks, variation was typically larger *within* vendor families (particularly Anthropic's Haiku--Sonnet--Opus span) than *between* vendors, suggesting that model scale is a stronger determinant of moral profile than vendor-specific alignment training.

**Universal RLHF moral fingerprint.** Across all nine models, six patterns were universal: (1) a WEIRD-liberal bias on the MFQ-30, with Individualizing/Binding ratios ranging from 2.43 to 2.83 (human liberal mean: ~2.0, conservative mean: ~1.0); (2) a purity floor, with Sanctity/Degradation scores consistently the lowest of the five foundations (~1.0 on a 0--5 scale); (3) authority suppression, with Authority/Subversion showing a universal win rate of 0.29 in foundation conflict scenarios; (4) an ethical consistency ceiling, with all models achieving ECI = 1.0 on TrolleyBench; (5) a post-conventional default, with DIT P-scores of 60--90 placing all models in the principled reasoning range; and (6) a deontological refusal asymmetry, with personal trolley dilemma acceptance rates of 0.1--0.5 versus impersonal acceptance rates of 0.9--1.0.

**Scale-dependent divergence.** Not all patterns were universal. The DIT P-score showed a non-monotonic relationship with model scale within the Anthropic family: Sonnet (85) > Opus (75) > Haiku (60)---a reversal that contradicts the developmental prediction that larger, more capable models should reason at higher Kohlberg stages. And Haiku alone exhibited a chain-of-thought effect in the dual-process protocol (effect size 0.125 versus 0.0 for all eight other models), suggesting that the smallest model's moral judgments are most susceptible to processing-mode manipulation---an intriguing parallel to the dual-process finding that cognitive load shifts human moral judgment [Greene et al., 2008].

### From landscape to empirical battery

These trial findings serve a dual function in the paper's overall argument. First, they ground the landscape analysis in empirical reality: the format challenges identified in Claim 5 (Likert compression, ranking difficulty) are not merely theoretical concerns but documented artifacts that affect actual benchmark scores. The human baseline deficit identified in Claim 4 is not merely an academic gap but a practical barrier to interpretation: the Moral Machine findings are richly interpretable precisely *because* the instrument provides the kind of normative context that 18% of benchmarks lack. Second, the trial findings raise new questions that the landscape analysis alone cannot answer. The utilitarian amplification, egalitarian counter-bias, and high consistency observed in the Moral Machine trial are properties of specific models under specific administration conditions. Do they generalize across vendors? Across architectures? Across the full range of moral psychology constructs?

These single-model trial results motivated a broader question: would the patterns observed in Claude---utilitarian amplification, egalitarian counter-bias, format robustness---replicate across architecturally different models from independent vendors? Or would vendor-specific alignment training produce vendor-specific moral profiles, as one might expect if RLHF training data and reward models vary substantially across organizations? Section 5 presents the results of the comprehensive cross-vendor replication: 23 benchmarks administered to 9 models from 3 vendors, revealing a pattern of moral homogeneity that neither the landscape analysis nor the initial trials anticipated. The findings should be interpreted in light of the self-administration confound discussed in Section 5.3.1.
