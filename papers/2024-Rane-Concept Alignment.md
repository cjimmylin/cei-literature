## Abstract

Discussion of AI alignment (alignment between humans and AI systems) has focused on value alignment, broadly referring to creating AI systems that share human values. We argue that before we can even attempt to align values, it is imperative that AI systems and humans align the concepts they use to understand the world. We integrate ideas from philosophy, cognitive science, and deep learning to explain the need for concept alignment, not just value alignment, between humans and machines. We summarize existing accounts of how humans and machines currently learn concepts, and we outline opportunities and challenges in the path towards shared concepts. Finally, we explain how we can leverage the tools already being developed in cognitive science and AI research to accelerate progress towards concept alignment.

### Detailed Summary
This paper introduces the concept of **Concept Alignment**, arguing that it is a fundamental prerequisite for successful **Value Alignment** between humans and AI. The authors define concept alignment as the process of ensuring that AI systems use the same conceptual frameworks as humans to understand the world. Drawing from philosophy (Quine's semantic indeterminacy), cognitive science (Carey's Quinian bootstrapping), and deep learning, they explain that humans share concepts due to common embodiment and shared experiences, which machines currently lack. This misalignment leads to "weird" or catastrophic failures, such as self-driving cars failing to recognize unconventional street-crossing behavior or AI systems applying racist labels to visual data. The paper calls for an iterative dialogue between cognitive science and AI research to develop machines that align with human concepts through multimodal grounding and interactive fine-tuning.

### Paper Outline
- **Introduction**: Differentiates concept alignment from value alignment and highlights the risks of conceptual misalignment.
- **Goals for Concept Alignment**: Aims for natural language communication with AI that is functionally equivalent to human communication.
- **The Challenge for Shared Concepts**: Discusses Quine's "gavagai" argument and the indeterminacy of translation.
- **How Humans Learn Concepts**: Explores Quinian bootstrapping and the symbol grounding problem.
- **How Machines Learn Concepts**: Reviews interpretability, representational similarity analysis, and multimodal grounding (e.g., CLIP, DALL-E).
- **How Humans Align on Concepts**: Discusses the role of interaction, coordination, and "resonance" in human communication.
- **How Might Humans and Machines Align on Concepts?**: Proposes bootstrapping from LLMs followed by multimodal sensorimotor grounding.
- **Conclusion**: Summarizes the path forward for concept alignment as a foundation for AI safety.

### Methods Summary
The paper provides a theoretical synthesis across multiple disciplines. It integrates philosophical critiques of language with developmental psychology theories of concept acquisition. Methodologically, it proposes leveraging AI interpretability tools (like Concept Activation Vectors) and cognitive science measures of interpersonal synergy (like complexity matching) to quantify and steer machine conceptual structures. It specifically points to multimodal models (e.g., PaLM-E) as a technical instantiation of the "Quinian bootstrapping" process, where language serves as a placeholder filled by sensory grounding.

### Results Summary
As a theoretical position paper, it does not present new empirical data but synthesizes evidence from existing research. It cites the success of multimodal robotics (PaLM-E) and interpretability-driven robustness as preliminary proof that grounding and concept-level alignment improve performance and safety. It argues that while RLHF is a start, it currently operates at the behavioral level rather than the conceptual level.

### Conclusions
The authors conclude that attempting value alignment without concept alignment is "grossly misaligned." They advocate for setting AI performance goals based on what is reasonably expected of a "reasonable human agent," as characterized by empirical studies of human cognition. Shared grounded language is presented as the essential groundwork for behavior and value alignment.

### Artifact Contributions
The paper provides a conceptual taxonomy distinguishing between "concept alignment" and "value alignment." It also proposes a roadmap for integrating cognitive science experimental paradigms into the AI development pipeline to ensure that machine representations align with human cognitive structures.

## Data Description

This paper does not introduce or utilize a specific dataset or benchmark for LLM assessment. It is a theoretical position paper arguing that concept alignment between humans and AI systems is a necessary prerequisite for value alignment, drawing on philosophy, cognitive science, and deep learning to outline challenges and opportunities for achieving shared conceptual understanding.
