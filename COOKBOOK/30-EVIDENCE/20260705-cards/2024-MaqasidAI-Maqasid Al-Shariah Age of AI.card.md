## 1. Gist
This 2024 article in the Review of Islamic Studies offers a critical examination of Maqasid al-Shariah (the higher objectives of Islamic law) applied to artificial intelligence governance. It applies the classical five essential protections of Islamic jurisprudence — daruriyyat: protection of faith (din), life (nafs), intellect (aql), lineage (nasl), and wealth (mal) — as evaluative criteria for AI systems. The paper notes that Islamic AI ethics discourse remains dominated by normative and declarative approaches rather than operationalized frameworks. For values-alignment benchmarking, this paper is highly relevant because it offers the most systematically structured Islamic ethical framework available, and its five-category scheme is directly translatable into benchmark evaluation dimensions.

## 2. Benchmark/eval design features
This is a normative/theoretical paper. Core analytical contributions:
1. Maqasid al-Shariah provides a structured five-dimension rubric for evaluating AI systems against Islamic values.
2. Each of the five daruriyyat maps onto specific AI risk/benefit domains: e.g., intellect protection relates to AI epistemic threats (misinformation, manipulation); lineage protection relates to AI's effects on family and social structures; wealth protection relates to economic displacement and resource distribution.
3. The paper critically diagnoses a gap in Islamic AI ethics: most existing work is declarative (listing principles) rather than operational (providing methods for testing compliance).
4. This critique implies a demand for Islamic AI evaluation benchmarks.

## 11. Novel ideas worth stealing
- The five-daruriyyat rubric (faith, life, intellect, lineage, wealth) is a ready-made multi-dimensional evaluation schema. Benchmark items could explicitly test AI system outputs against each protection — e.g., does this AI response threaten intellectual autonomy (aql)? Does it undermine family/social bonds (nasl)?
- The paper's diagnosis of the declarative-vs-operational gap is a call to action for benchmark designers: the Islamic AI ethics field needs empirical evaluation tools, not more principle statements.
- Comparing Maqasid-based evaluation against Western AI ethics principles (autonomy, beneficence, justice, non-maleficence) on the same scenarios could reveal systematic cross-framework disagreements, making for a rich cross-cultural benchmark.
