Abstract 
In recent years, there has been a growing interest in using generative 
artificial intelligence (AI) for machine translation (MT) tasks. A notable 
feature of generative AI systems is prompting, through which users can 
provide instructions to guide the output. Prompting presents both 
opportunities and challenges in Translation Studies. Therefore, this study 
aimed to evaluate the quality of MT output generated by ChatGPT. The 
impact of prompting techniques on translation quality was also analyzed to 
gain insights into optimizing the use of generative AI in translation 
workflows. The study employed three prompts – zero-shot prompt, context 
prompt, and few-shot with context prompt – to translate a creative text for 
the English-German language pair. The raw MT outputs were manually 
annotated and the results of these annotations were compared. The results 
revealed that most of the errors were related to style. Interestingly, the 
translation produced using the simple zero-shot prompt outperformed those 
generated using the other two more complex prompts. However, longer 
prompts led to a steady reduction in stylistic and fluency errors, while 
accuracy errors increased.

1. Introduction 
The use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in professional life has been increasing steadily, with AI 
chatbots boosting productivity by about 14% (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). Generative AI models like 
ChatGPT can be employed for various tasks, including machine translation (MT). ChatGPT, launched in 
November 2022, quickly gained over one million users within a week, significantly impacting AI chatbot 
adoption (OpenAI, 2022; Haque et al., 2022). The rise of generative AI is also acknowledged in the 
discipline of Translation Studies (TS), as evidenced by conferences on this interdisciplinary topic, such 
as the Artificial Intelligence in Translation Summit 20231. On the practical side, the translation industry 
also started to employ AI models. For instance, translation agencies introduced chatbots to communicate 
with language service providers (LSP) and assigned them tasks. In addition, software, such as 
CoTranslatorAI2, allows professionals to enhance their workflows by using generative AI models for 
various tasks, from writing emails to editing or translating text. One important aspect of working with 
generative AI models is prompting, i.e., instructing and guiding the desired output (Zhang et al., 2023). 
In parallel, the continuous advancements in MT empower LSPs to produce more translations in a given 
time. Neural machine translation (NMT) systems are the current state-of-the-art (Kenny, 2022), but their 
outputs are not error-free (Rossi & Carré, 2022). Evaluation of the output of MT systems is essential for 
their enhancement as well as to understand their strengths and limitations, and recognize the need for 
post-editing, i.e., the process of identifying and revising errors in MT output (O’Brien, 2022). 
The primary objectives of this study are to analytically assess translation quality by ChatGPT on a creative 
translation task in the English-German language pair, as well as to examine the effects of using different 
prompts. The study analyzes the strengths and weaknesses of ChatGPT’s translations through manual 
error annotation. By doing so, the research intends to shed light on its efficacy, shortcomings, and 
potential areas for improvement. 
When ChatGPT was first released, the majority of studies were holistically evaluating ChatGPT’s 
translation performance, and only a few analytic studies for other language pairs existed. Therefore, there 
is a gap when it comes to analytically assessing English-German translations. 
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses relevant existing literature and concepts, while 
Section 3 describes the research design, as well as the methods used for data collection and analysis. 
Section 4 presents the results of the error annotation of the raw MT outputs. The subsequent Section 5 
reviews the main results in light of the literature reviewed. Lastly, the Conclusion summarizes the results 
and suggests future research directions.

2. Theoretical contextualization 
2.1. Machine translation with ChatGPT 
ChatGPT was launched in late 2022 and within approximately one-year, numerous papers exploring its 
application for machine translation were published (e.g., Jiao et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023; Farrell, 2023). 
Jiang and Zhang (2024) used ChatGPT to translate sentences from Chinese into English. Their results 
(2024) showed that most errors are related to style. In particular, the researchers observed that ChatGPT’s 
translations contain more awkward and unidiomatic expressions than NMT systems. However, they were 
able to reduce style errors and other error types by providing context or examples in the prompts. In 
contrast, Wang et al. (2023) claimed that sentence-level translations may lack coherence and context. 
Translating sentences in isolation does not take advantage of ChatGPT’s ability to learn and adapt from 
context (Brown et al., 2020). However, translating on document-level1 can improve discourse awareness 
and translation quality (Wang et al., 2023). Findings from Karpinska and Iyyer (2023, p. 9) demonstrated 
that sentence “translations contain 29.5% more mistranslations, 65.4% more grammatical errors, over 12 
times more inconsistency errors, and three times more register errors”. Nevertheless, they still identified 
many critical mistranslations and other errors in the paragraph translations. 
Some studies focused on the translation of rhetorical devices, such as Yamada (2023), who demonstrated 
that GPT-4 provided literal translations of idioms for Japanese-English, using a simple prompt, while 
prompts with more information generated a more appropriate translation two out of three times. Similarly, 
Karabayeva and Kalizhanova (2024) observed that ChatGPT prioritises literal meaning over artistic 
expression when translating passages. Their findings (2024) showed acceptable accuracy for descriptive 
sentences, but figures of speech suffered in accuracy. According to Sahari et al. (2024), GPT-3 aims to 
preserve both meaning and structure, which negatively affects the quality of passage translations. 
Nonetheless, when Yamada (2023) gave specific instructions focusing on literary devices, ChatGPT 
demonstrated enhanced performance in certain creative aspects. 
Importantly, researchers should select texts published after September 2021 to avoid contamination from 
large language models (LLM) pretraining data when using ChatGPT for TS (Karpinska & Iyyer, 2023). 
Moreover, it has been found that GPT models performed well for high-resource European languages, but 
their abilities were limited for low-resource languages (Hendy et al., 2023). The translation quality varied 
not only between language pairs but also between the different GPTs. Recent studies predominantly used 
GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (e.g., Lyu et al., 2023; Kadaoui et al., 2023) and it was found that ChatGPT is the 
best translator among existing LLMs (Zhu et al., 2023; Kadaoui et al., 2023).

One of the strengths of ChatGPT lies in its capability to adapt a text to a certain audience (Lyu et al., 
2023). It also performs robustly on erroneous, short, or colloquial source sentences and produces 
reasonable translations for partial or incomplete sentences (Hendy et al., 2023). However, it has 
limitations. Hendy et al. (2023) found that the engine generated unnatural translations for uncommon or 
complex sentences. Additionally, accuracy mistakes such as misinformation and hallucinations may 
occasionally appear (Hendy et al., 2023). In addition, Wang et al. (2023) showed that translation outputs 
occasionally contained omissions and showed copying behaviors. One reason for these limitations could 
be that ChatGPT is mainly trained on English data (Hendy et al., 2023). 
2.2. Prompt writing 
The ability to prompt generative AI systems is the main difference from generic NMT systems (Lommel, 
2023). Consequently, designing effective prompts has emerged as a key aspect of MT, as they impact 
style, accuracy, and precision (Jiao et al., 2024). Prompt writing involves creating and refining 
instructions for an AI model to produce precise and relevant responses (Bammel, 2023). It is crucial to 
emphasize that no single method is universally effective for all scenarios and all LLMs. The key to finding 
the most effective prompt for a specific task is to continuously test and evaluate different prompts 
(Abdullahi et al., 2024). Consequently, current research is actively investigating the impact of prompts 
and strategies on machine translation tasks, e.g., Jiang and Zhang (2024) and Jiao et al. (2024), and the 
present study contributes towards this effort. 
2.2.1. General strategies for prompting 
Firstly, prompts must be correct, natural, and complete (Bammel, 2023). Users can also include examples 
of the desired output, known as shots, in prompts to demonstrate the expected output. 
Prompting strategies can be differentiated into zero-shot, one-shot and few-shot prompts (Dang et al., 
2022). As the name indicates, no examples are provided in zero-shot prompts, and LLMs execute a task 
purely based on the data it is trained on (Brown et al., 2020). One-shot prompts contain one example 
which serves as a reference to the LLM on the structure or context. Few-shot prompts incorporate multiple 
examples which can vary between three, five or more examples (Brown et al., 2020). They are called 
accordingly three-shot prompts, five-shot prompts, etc.  
Few-shot prompting enables in-context learning, which recent studies have shown to be beneficial (Peng 
et al., 2023), but improvements may be limited by the prompt (Gao et al., 2023). Therefore, few-shot 
prompting has its limitations (Brown et al., 2020). LLMs can occasionally forget context or interpret it 
incorrectly, and their capacity to learn complex concepts or tasks may be restricted (Brown et al., 2020). 
Thus, models can still produce inconsistent or unpredictable results (Brown et al., 2020). 
Providing context can further improve precision, which can include background information, domain-
specific knowledge, audience, purpose (Siu, 2023), or other relevant information. Any context

Moreover, there are two primary forms of presenting prompts: the single-turn prompt and the multiple-

several rounds of dialogue (Jiao et al., 2024). 
2.2.2. Prompt writing for machine translation 
Many recent studies on prompt writing for translation explored how prompting can improve the output 
without training or fine-tuning large parallel corpora. These works varied in their experiments. Indeed, 
while some researchers used simple prompts without further context information (e.g., Jiao et al., 2023), 
some chose to provide information in the prompt (e.g., Yamada, 2023). Besides, some studies exclusively 
focused on zero-shot prompts (e.g., Jiao et al., 2023) or few-shot prompts (e.g., Karpinska & Iyyer, 2023), 
while others compared zero-shot and few-shot prompts (e.g., Jiang & Zhang, 2024). 
According to Wang et al. (2023), even a simple prompt can effectively capture document-level context. 
Hendy et al. (2023) observed that a few-shot setup enhanced the performance over zero-shots on sentence-
level translation when translating from English into another language. Similar findings for sentence-level 
were reported by Kadaoui et al. (2023) and Peng et al. (2023). Similarly, Jiang and Zhang (2024) 
compared the results of sentence translations using three prompts, namely a zero-shot prompt, a one-shot 
prompt, and a prompt with context. Based on human evaluation, the researchers observed the lowest 
penalties for the one-shot and context prompt when compared to zero-shot (Jiang & Zhang, 2024). 
Likewise, Jiao et al. (2024) demonstrated that increasing the context in the prompt and using few-shots 
led to improved translation quality. For sentence translation, few-shots produced some steady 
improvements; however, this was not the case for the translation of passages (Hendy et al., 2023). Hendy 
et al. (2023) explained that document translation possibly provides sufficient context rendering few-shots 
redundant.  
Some studies included further context information in their prompts. Gao et al. (2023) found that ChatGPT 
performed better at sentence-level when the prompt contained the domain information, which is in line 
with the findings of Peng et al. (2023), whereas incorrect domain information hurt the translation quality. 
Results from Peng et al. (2023) regarding the task-specific prompt showed no improvement in two out of 
three automatic evaluation metrics for English-German. According to Yamada (2023), incorporating the 
purpose and target audience generally improved the quality of marketing phrases and culturally dependent 
idiomatic translations obtained from GPT-4. Additionally, introducing the concept of dynamic 
equivalence also enhanced creative translation quality (Yamada, 2023). Yet, ChatGPT performed 
similarly with several semantically similar prompts (Lyu et al., 2023).

2.3. Translation quality assessment 
The quality of a translation depends a lot on the context in which it is done (Rossi & Carré, 2022). A 
quality translation can be defined as one where the intended communicative function of the translation in 
the target culture and communicative situation for its specific audience is achieved (Nord, 1997). Analytic 
human evaluation can offer detailed insights into the nature of errors. In addition, the translation brief 
plays a crucial role in this context because it explicitly states the communicative situation and the purpose 
of the translated text, directly influencing the criteria used in translation quality assessment (TQA). The 
translation brief provides all necessary instructions and specifications for creating a high-quality 
translation, guiding both the translator and evaluators in their assessments (Nord, 2010; Lommel, 2018). 
An essential aspect of TQA is the categorization of errors. The simplest approach to analyze the 
translation output is to identify each error and assign it an error tag. For this process, it is crucial to define 
error categories in advance, encompassing linguistic and translation aspects (Popović, 2018). 
Additionally, evaluators should take the severity of errors into consideration (Lommel, 2018). Although 
it is a very challenging and time-consuming task, manual error classification has the possibility of 
providing more details on the quality of MT outputs than automatic metrics (Popović, 2018). 
In 2011, the Translation Automation User Society (TAUS) created the Dynamic Quality Framework 
(DQF) (Görög, 2014), and in 2012, the German Research Centre for Artificial Intelligence (DFKI) 
developed the Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM). Both frameworks emphasize that translation 
evaluation is flexible and aligns with the content type, purpose, and context of the translation (Görög, 
2014; Lommel, 2018). By 2015, TAUS and DFKI developed the harmonized DQF-MQM error typology 
(Lommel, 2018). The aim was to bridge the gap between the specifications and definitions (Görög, 2014). 
This harmonized typology categorizes errors into four severity levels and includes 50 error types 
(Lommel, 2018). DQF-MQM is now a frequently used industry standard for the evaluation of translations 
(Lommel, 2018), for example, see Diab (2021) and Bayón and Sánchez Gijón (2023). 
2.3.1. TQA for LLMs 
The existing research studies differ in their approaches to evaluating translations produced by LLMs. 
While some studies relied on human evaluation (e.g., Khoshafah, 2023), others employed automatic 
metrics (e.g., Jiao et al., 2023). The majority, however, used both human and automatic evaluation (e.g., 
Jiang & Zhang, 2024).

Research focused on human evaluation includes the work of Sanz-Valdivieso and López-Arroyo (2023), 
where annotators analyzed translations using the MQM framework. Their study highlighted the precision 
and detail that can be achieved when using a structured framework like MQM to analyze translations. 
Other studies that also utilized the MQM metric for human evaluation include Lu et al. (2023) and

worth noting that several studies incorporated the MQM metrics for error annotation. Human error 
analysis offers the advantage of critically engaging with a text on the micro and macro text level, 
providing a detailed overview of common and critical errors. For this reason, this study employs a human 
error analysis, which is described in Section 3. 
This section discussed key notions and previous research relevant to this study. The advances in 
technology, especially the emergence of AI and LLMs, highlight the need to explore MT in connection 
with these technologies. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by delving into the evaluation of MT 
output by ChatGPT, taking a creative text machine translated from English into German as a case study. 
It has two main aims: 1) to analyze and classify errors in translations obtained from ChatGPT and 2) to 
examine the impact of different types of prompts on the quality of the translated outputs. Hence, the first 
research question is “How is the quality of texts translated by ChatGPT?”, and the second research 
question is “How does prompt writing impact translation?”. The next section presents the methodology 
applied for collecting and analyzing data. 
3. Method 
The methodology draws on two rounds of literature review, as generative AI is a relatively new and 
driving field (Brühl, 2024). The literature review considers research until the end of February 2024, which 
informed the methodology of the current study. Additionally, ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) was selected as 
translation tool due to its ever-increasing popularity and improvements (Sanz-Valdivieso & López-
Arroyo, 2023; OpenAI, 2023).

3.2. Data analysis 
Once the machine translations were generated, the errors of all three outputs were manually annotated in 
Word and entered into a scorecard. The evaluation was conducted one text at a time, beginning with the 
translation of the creative translation using Prompt 1 (CTP1), followed by those using Prompts 2 and 3 
(CTP2, CTP3). To ensure consistent error tags, the translations were compared to each other. Before the 
start of the annotation process, the error typology and severity levels, including definitions and examples, 
were established as a measure to provide objective guidelines as emphasized by Rossi and Carré (2022). 
In the evaluation, a human translation was not used as a benchmark. 
3.2.1. Scorecard 
The three translations have identical scorecards which were adapted from an existing Excel spreadsheet 
downloadable from the MQM website (MQM, 2024a). Each error was recorded according to its severity. 
The penalty points 0 = Neutral, 1 = Minor, 10 = Major, and 100 = Critical, following Lommel (2018), 
were selected as they provide sufficient distinction between the severities. The scorecards contain the 
Maximum Score Value of 100, the Scaling Parameter of 1, and the word count of the translation, which 
are used to calculate the results (MQM, 2024b). The Scaling Parameter 1 facilitates the calculation of 
scores and conclusions as it provides a clear starting point. After recording the errors, the spreadsheets 
counted the number of errors for each category and calculated the Error Type Penalty Total, Absolute 
Penalty Total, Per-Word Penalty Total, and Overall Quality Score (MQM, 2024b), which were used to 
analyze and compare the translations. As far as repeated errors are concerned, each error decreases the 
overall quality, since the evaluation aims to assess the quality of the translation products. Therefore, 
regardless of repetition, any error is considered as such (MQM, 2024c).

3.2.2. Error typology 
For the evaluation, an error typology was created and defined, based on and adapted from the harmonized

strengths and limitations of the AI model for the text type as well as for the language pair, as highlighted 
by Popović (2018). Additionally, it covers linguistic and translation aspects relevant to the translations, 
resulting in a comprehensive error typology encompassing seven categories (level 1) and twenty 
subcategories (level 2) (see Table 3).

5.1. Translation quality of ChatGPT 
Given the constant evolution of MT, analytical evaluations of MT outputs provide insights into the nature 
of errors, allowing for targeted improvements (Lommel, 2018). In reviewing the literature, no studies 
were found that analyzed the errors of ChatGPT’s translations analytically concerning the English-
German language pair. Thus, since there was no prior work in this language pair that could be used as a 
basis for this study, this case study drew on previous studies on the topic investigating different language 
pairs. This section focuses on errors concerning style, accuracy, robustness, and rhetorical devices. 
Given the inherent variations between language conventions, the translation must align with the 
appropriate conventions of the target language (Nord, 2010). While meaning is not conveyed by words 
alone, a successful translation accurately captures the intended meaning using an appropriate language 
style (Nord, 2010). Thus, a literal translation is unsuitable for the text type involved in this study, yet it 
appears in all three outputs. Although some portions of the outputs are understandable, the literal rendition

occasionally leads to a loss in meaning and unnatural translation. As a result, style represents the category 
with the most errors recorded. This aligns with the results of Jiang and Zhang (2024), who translated 
sentences of diplomatic texts from Chinese into English using ChatGPT and found that most errors 
concern awkward and unidiomatic style. Similarly, Hendy et al. (2023) observed unnatural translations 
of uncommon or complex sentences. The subsequent error type noted by Jiang and Zhang (2024) concerns 
accuracy. Likewise, Karpinska and Iyyer (2023) found that paragraph translations into Polish, Japanese, 
and English contain numerous mistranslations and grammatical errors and occasionally omit content. 
According to Hendy et al. (2023) mistranslations appear especially when translating from English. These 
observations reflect the results of the present investigation, in which mistranslation is the leading 
subcategory of accuracy. The results outline that the categories unidiomatic style, language register, 
cohesion and mistranslations are the shortcomings of ChatGPT. The number of grammatical errors, such 
as those related to morphology and syntax, is limited, representing a relatively minor limitation.  
Notably, CTP2 and CTP3 contain fewer style and fluency errors but more accuracy errors compared to 
the translations with Prompts 1. During the annotation process, an improved style in some sentences in 
the translation with Prompt 3 was noted, giving the impression of improved output; however, this was a 
subjective observation. The results of Section 4 clearly show that CTP2 and CTP3 still lag behind CTP1. 
This observation suggests that the outputs of CTP2 and CTP3 could be more deceiving to a user unfamiliar 
with translation processes. This inference may explain why, in the study by Jiang and Zhang (2024), 
annotators assigned higher scores to translations obtained from a prompt with a one-shot or context. 
Indeed, the researchers (2024) noted that the style of the translations improved when an example or 
context was included in the prompt. 
Another merit of ChatGPT is that it performs robustly on erroneous sentences, as found by Hendy et al. 
(2023). Similar observations are made in the present study, where the LLM translated the misspelled word 
“Quairaing” two out of three times correctly. This suggests that ChatGPT can correct misspelled words 
in the source in most instances. 
The creative text involved in this study relies on the use of imaginative language. It includes many figures 
of speech and other stylistic devices. This highlights again that meaning is more than just the dictionary 
translation of words, but rather the nuanced expression of concepts within a specific linguistic and cultural 
context (Munday, 2016). As shown in the analysis, ChatGPT provides literal translations for some 
rhetorical devices which reflect the results of previous studies (Yamada, 2023; Karabayeva & 
Kalizhanova, 2024). In particular, simple, common metaphors are translated adequately, while other 
metaphors present challenges (Karabayeva & Kalizhanova, 2024). Like the findings by Karabayeva and 
Kalizhanova (2024) for English-Russian, ChatGPT neglects artistic expression, and whereas descriptive 
sentences are translated correctly, figurative language exhibits lower accuracy. Overall, their findings 
(2024) indicated that the LLM cannot replicate the nuances, fluidity, and aesthetics of rhetorical devices 
of the source. In addition, Sahari et al. (2024) highlighted that ChatGPT aims to preserve the sentence 
structure and maintain the meaning at the same time, which is not a suitable approach for creative 
translation, as emphasized by Yamada (2023). And this holds true for the creative translation of this study.

5.2. Impact of prompts on translation 
Based on the results of the annotation process, this section identifies prevalent trends and evaluates prompt 
strategies with reference to the literature. It is important to note that the studies used for this comparison 
involve different text types, text lengths, prompts, and language pairs. Thus, drawing direct parallels 
between the results in this study and those of previous studies necessitates careful consideration of these 
differences. 
The ability to prompt generative AI systems is the main difference from generic NMT systems (Lommel, 
2023). Users can instruct, add relevant information, and ultimately guide ChatGPT’s performance (Siu, 
2023). The study by Wang et al. (2023) demonstrated that a simple zero-shot is capable of effectively 
capturing the context of document translations, which is in line with the results of the previous section. 
Simple Prompt 1 obtained the lowest penalty points and generated a coherent translation. This highlights 
the robustness of zero-shot methods for translation tasks on document-level. 
As described in the Methodology, the prompt design of Jiang and Zhang (2024) shows parallels to the 
three prompts outlined for this study. Hence, it is reasonable to compare the results of these two studies. 
Importantly, the studies vary in their research design. In contrast to the present study, which focuses on 
English-German creative document translation, Jiang and Zhang (2024) focused on sentence translation 
of diplomatic texts in Chinese-English. The researchers (2024) found that the prompts with the example 
and context led to fewer errors under style. This mirrors the results reported in this study, where Prompts 
2 and 3 also result in a lower number of style and fluency errors. This suggests that context and examples 
can guide ChatGPT’s output to more closely resemble the desired style. In addition, the comparison of 
translations using zero-shot, one-shot, and context prompts revealed higher translation quality among the 
one-shot and context prompts (Jiang & Zhang, 2024). This contrasts with the results in the present study, 
where the simple zero-shot Prompt 1 yields the highest performance, while the other two more complex 
prompts are of lower quality. One explanation for these differences could be the use of different language 

--- Page 21 ---

Volume 3, Issue 1 
 
21 
pairs and text genres in each study. Moreover, Jiang and Zhang (2024) stated that their different prompting 
approaches did not lead to a significant enhancement in ChatGPT’s translation capabilities overall. 
Conversely, the present study did not identify any improvements in translation quality through the use of 
various prompting strategies. These different outcomes could be justified by the differences in the nature 
of the translation tasks in each study. Furthermore, as found by Hendy et al. (2023), ChatGPT achieved 
steady improvements for sentence-level translations, with the most gains from one high-quality shot. 
However, results for translation of passages indicated only slight quality enhancements, with overall 
results appearing relatively similar. Hendy et al. (2023) stated that the translation of passages provides 
sufficient context which makes few-shot prompts redundant. Likewise, Gao et al. (2023) found that well

across all scenarios. Whereas more complex prompts yielded improvements for some language pairs, for 
others, it led to a performance drop. Gao et al. (2023) explained that the additional information in the 
prompt might have introduced noise. Likewise, Wu et al. (2022) stated that instructions and long context 
bodies could conflict with each other, leading to no improvement in the output. This could be a possible 
explanation for the declining results in the study at hand and may clarify why Prompt 1 yielded the best 
results. Importantly, the ST was entered right below the instructions in the same message, resulting in a 
lengthy input text in ChatGPT. Thus, it is possible that the prompts introduced noisy data, which led to 
the lower quality. This hypothesis certainly needs to be addressed in future research. 
To address the second research question regarding the impact of prompts on translation, the results of the 
three prompts are briefly recapped. Prompt 1 achieves the highest translation quality. In this case, a zero-
shot prompt provided the better translation output, albeit not an error free translation. The creative 
translation generated with Prompt 2 receives the lowest quality score out of all three translations. 
Nevertheless, Prompts 2 and 3 reduce style and fluency errors consistently. In addition, the translation 
quality declines with the context and few-shot prompts. The decrease in quality is from 86.67 to 74.29. 
These observations contradict the expectation from the literature review that performance would be 
enhanced with more complex prompts.

6. Conclusions 
This study has provided insights into the nature of errors when using ChatGPT for translation tasks. It is 
one of the first studies to analytically evaluate machine translated output of ChatGPT for the English-
German language pair. The main objectives of this study were to gain an understanding of the translation 
quality and the impact of prompts, indicating the strengths and limitations of ChatGPT. To reach these 
goals, the translation outputs were evaluated, and the results of the translations as well as the results of 
the different prompts were compared. 
One of the most significant results of this case study is that the most common error produced by ChatGPT 
concerns style. In particular, language register errors and unidiomatic style errors prevail. Fluency errors 
rank second, with cohesion errors comprising the highest frequency, and accuracy ranks third with 
mistranslation errors. These results provide an overview of the main errors when translating a creative 
task with ChatGPT from English into German. In addition, the results highlight the importance of post-
editing translations produced by ChatGPT to detect errors and improve the raw output to align with the 
task brief. Nevertheless, raw MT outputs are coherent and can be used to understand the meaning of the 
texts. The study indicates that although the overall quality of the translations is satisfactory, the 
intervention of trained translators is required to correct errors and fine-tune the machine translated text. 
The study also evaluated the use of three different prompts. A key result is that more complex prompts 
(Prompt 2 and Prompt 3) consistently reduce style and fluency errors in the translations. This indicates 
that the information in the prompt provides guidance to ChatGPT regarding the stylistic and language 
aspect of the translation. However, the study shows that the better translation quality, though not error-
free, is achieved with a simple prompt (Prompt 1). Indeed, the translation quality decreases when 
including context or examples in the prompt. It is crucial to emphasize that the output generated with the 
longer Prompts 2 and 3 could be particularly misleading to users unfamiliar with translation processes, 
highlighting the need for professional translators to review MT and AI-generated content. Nonetheless,

by the utilization of different research design. It is possible that the long prompts in addition to the source

The implications of these results extend beyond the limits of this study, impacting LSPs working in the 
German language, translator trainers, as well as scholars in the field of computational linguistics. The 
study provides an overview of the errors made by ChatGPT for creative translation tasks, particularly 
noting that most errors are stylistic. Understanding the strengths and limitations of AI-driven translation 
help to indicate what to focus on during translation pedagogy. Hence, this study provides insights to 
translator trainers on what to focus on when training budding translators to detect and correct errors 
performed by GPTs. Furthermore, LSPs can decide whether to include ChatGPT as a translation tool, as 
the study offers a comprehensive overview of translation quality, types of errors, and the influence of 
prompts on quality. By suggesting future research directions, LSPs are encouraged to experiment to 
generate better translation outputs and enhance their workflows when using ChatGPT. Moreover, the 
result that a simple prompt is sufficient for translating documents suggests that LSPs can avoid spending 
time on crafting detailed prompts, as doing so may decrease translation quality and ultimately lead to 
more post-editing efforts. Yet, the results also show that LSPs cannot do without human post-editors as 
AI translations cannot be relied on blindly. 
The study has clarified and justified the methodology employed. However, it is important to acknowledge 
some limitations of this study. The annotation process proved to be a time-consuming task and although 
the annotation was carried out mainly by the first author, it was thoroughly discussed with the second 
author. A full, double annotation carried out independently by the two authors might have reduced 
subjectivity, as different people might categorize some errors differently, but this would have been even 
more time-consuming. Therefore, we attempted to find a balanced solution. Additionally, the evaluation 
encompassed only human assessment, and utilizing both automatic and human metrics could provide a 
more comprehensive overview of the translation quality (Hendy et al., 2023). 
Despite the interesting results, this study also raised further questions and hypotheses that need to be 
addressed in future studies. To begin with, the hypothesis that a lengthy input text – made of a complex 
prompt and a long text body – introduces noise and that the provided information is counterproductive, 
ultimately leading to a decrease in the translation quality, necessitates further research. One option could 
be to use the same three prompts but a translation with a lower word count than in the present study. 
Conducting a study with a shorter text could demonstrate whether the translations are of higher quality 
than in the current study and whether a long text body introduces noise. This would provide even further 
insights into how to translate and how to obtain a better translation quality using ChatGPT and could 
validate the results of the present study. Another research direction could be to explore different prompts 
and evaluate their impact. The present study was limited to the use of three prompts, but employing 
different prompts might yield different results (Zhang et al., 2023). For instance, as suggested by 
Yamada (2023), providing specific instructions focusing on literary devices could boost creative 
translation. Additionally, AI facilitates efficient collaboration between humans and machines, enabling
