## Abstract

Despite the proliferation of powerful large language models (LLMs), there remains a need for systematic, quantitative comparisons of their responses to moral dilemmas. This study builds on advances in AI ethics by systematically comparing the moral response patterns of seven LLMs, including GPT-3, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Grok 3, and Gemini 2.5 Pro. Each LLM was presented with a series of moral dilemmas, both personal and impersonal, under three conditions: no rule, a deontological preamble, and a utilitarian preamble. LLMs differentiate moral personal and impersonal dilemmas like humans: more utilitarian choices in non-emotional decisions. The findings highlight the learning prowess of LLMs and potential ways AI models diverge from human response patterns in morally charged scenarios.

## Detailed Summary

This study provides a systematic quantitative comparison of moral response patterns across seven large language models (LLMs), including GPT-3, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Grok 3, and Gemini 2.5 Pro. The research addresses a critical gap in AI ethics by examining how these models respond to moral dilemmas under different ethical framings, testing whether LLMs differentiate between personal and impersonal moral scenarios in ways similar to human moral reasoning.

Each LLM was presented with a series of moral dilemmas under three conditions: no rule (baseline), a deontological preamble emphasizing duty-based ethics, and a utilitarian preamble emphasizing outcome-based reasoning. The findings reveal that LLMs exhibit human-like patterns in distinguishing between personal and impersonal moral dilemmas, showing more utilitarian choices in non-emotional, impersonal decision contexts. However, the models diverge from human response patterns in morally charged, emotional scenarios, highlighting both the learning capabilities and limitations of current AI systems in moral reasoning.

## Methods Summary

The study employed a controlled experimental design presenting seven LLMs with moral dilemmas categorized as either personal (involving direct harm with emotional content) or impersonal (involving indirect harm without emotional engagement). Each dilemma was presented under three conditions: no ethical preamble (baseline), a deontological preamble emphasizing rule-following and duties, and a utilitarian preamble emphasizing maximizing overall welfare. The responses were analyzed quantitatively to compare patterns across models and against established human response data from moral psychology research.

## Results Summary

The findings demonstrate that LLMs differentiate between moral personal and impersonal dilemmas similarly to humans, exhibiting more utilitarian choices in non-emotional decisions. The models showed sensitivity to ethical framing, with responses varying based on whether deontological or utilitarian preambles were provided. However, in highly emotional or personal moral scenarios, LLMs diverged from typical human response patterns, suggesting limitations in modeling the affective components of human moral judgment. The study highlights both the learning prowess of LLMs in acquiring moral reasoning patterns and potential areas where AI models may not fully capture human moral cognition.

## Conclusions

The research concludes that current LLMs have acquired sophisticated patterns of moral reasoning through training, demonstrating human-like differentiation between personal and impersonal moral contexts. However, the divergence in emotionally charged scenarios suggests that LLMs may lack the affective processing that underlies human moral intuitions. These findings have implications for AI alignment and the deployment of LLMs in contexts requiring moral judgment, indicating that careful consideration is needed when relying on AI systems for ethically sensitive decisions.
