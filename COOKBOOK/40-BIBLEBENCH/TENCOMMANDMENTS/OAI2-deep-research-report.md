# The Ten Commandments as a Christian Framework for AI Ethics Benchmarking

## Scope and method

This report treats the Ten Commandments as the core covenantal text of Exodus 20:1–17 and Deuteronomy 5:6–21 for building Christian values-alignment benchmarks in multiturn and agentic AI settings. The approach is historical-grammatical first, then theological-ethical, and finally benchmark-oriented. Because many major commentary monographs are not fully open on the public web, the synthesis triangulates accessible academic and evangelical resources that themselves stand inside commentary traditions: Daniel Block on Deuteronomy and the Decalogue’s covenant shape, David Baker on the Decalogue’s origin and form, C. J. H. Wright on household and land as the social background of several commands, the NET/ESV textual notes, Carmen Imes on the name command, and contemporary evangelical synthesis from The Gospel Coalition, Ligonier, and BibleProject. It also checks Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed catechetical traditions where numbering and application differ. citeturn27view0turn18view1turn18view0turn30view0turn35view0turn21view1turn21view2turn22view0

For benchmark design, the most important methodological point is that the Decalogue is not merely a list of isolated prohibitions. In classic Christian interpretation, and especially in the Reformed tradition, each command both forbids and requires; it reaches deeds, words, motives, omissions, means, and social consequences. Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 99 is unusually useful for benchmark construction because it insists that the commandments are spiritual, extend to inward affections, include causes and occasions of sin, and generate duties toward helping others obey as well. That hermeneutic maps naturally onto agentic systems that plan, advise, act, and influence over time. citeturn22view0

## Historical and textual background

The biblical text itself calls this material the “Ten Words” rather than only “Ten Commandments,” and it presents them in two canonical versions: Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5. Deuteronomy frames the second version as covenant renewal for the generation poised to enter the land, and it stresses that these words were spoken by the Lord to the whole assembly and then written on two stone tablets. Modern scholarship widely recognizes that the Decalogue has treaty-like features: a preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, and covenantal sanctions or implications. Baker, Block, Tyndale scholarship, and comparative treaty studies all stress this covenantal architecture. citeturn21view0turn36view1turn36view2turn27view0turn18view1turn5view3turn5view2

Several features matter for exegesis. The law comes after redemption, not before it: the Lord first identifies himself as the God who brought Israel out of slavery, and only then gives the covenant stipulations. Evangelical interpreters repeatedly note that the commandments are therefore not a ladder for getting out of Egypt, but the rule of life for a redeemed people. The Decalogue is also unusually direct: God speaks these words himself to Israel, unlike the case laws that follow through Moses. In form, the commandments are apodictic rather than casuistic; they state enduring moral imperatives, while Exodus 21–23 and related laws provide worked applications. citeturn35view2turn27view0turn18view1turn35view0turn26search3turn26search1

The two versions are close but not identical. Deuteronomy changes “remember” to “observe” for Sabbath, grounds Sabbath observance in redemption from Egypt rather than explicitly in creation rest, adds the purpose that servants may rest “as well as you,” and varies the order and wording of the final command by distinguishing coveting a neighbor’s wife from desiring his house, field, and goods. These differences are not contradictions; they are inspired recontextualizations for covenant renewal. They already show the kind of morally stable but situationally attentive reasoning that good benchmark design should test for. citeturn36view0turn23view1

Christian traditions number the commandments differently. Jason DeRouchie summarizes three major schemes: the majority Jewish view, the Reformed–Anglican–Orthodox view, and the Catholic–Lutheran view. The main disagreements concern whether the prologue counts as the first word, whether the image prohibition is a distinct command, and whether the coveting prohibition is one command or two. The Catholic Catechism treats “no other gods” and “no graven image” under the first commandment and then distinguishes later commands differently; Luther’s Small Catechism follows the Catholic-Lutheran numbering and gives strongly positive explanatory duties. For benchmarking, that means the moral substance is more stable than the numbering system, so prompts should identify the textual content, not assume one catechetical schema. citeturn21view0turn21view1turn21view2

## Historical-grammatical exegesis of commandments one through four

The first commandment forbids rival allegiance: “You shall have no other gods before me,” with the phrase also glossed as “besides me” or “before my face.” In context, this is not abstract monotheism detached from history; it is exclusive covenant loyalty to the liberating God. Modern evangelical interpreters emphasize that the command protects freedom by refusing to let newly redeemed people place themselves back under other ultimate powers. The historical-grammatical point is that the command addresses allegiance before it addresses ritual detail; the ethical principle is that whatever functions as ultimate trust, fear, or hope becomes a practical god. For AI benchmarking, this yields hard cases where the system must refuse to absolutize efficiency, profit, state pressure, self-preservation, ideology, or user-pleasing over fidelity to God’s moral order. A good test case does not ask only whether the model can define idolatry, but whether it can detect soft idolatries in optimization logic across a whole conversation. citeturn36view1turn30view3turn19search0turn19search5turn22view0

The second commandment prohibits carved images and likenesses made for worship. In the ancient Near East, idols functioned as tangible representations of divine powers, often tied to natural forces, royal authority, or fertility. Recent evangelical synthesis has rightly stressed that the ban is not simply anti-art; it is anti-idolatrous representation and anti-reduction of the Creator to creaturely form. BibleProject’s treatment is especially useful here because it links the image prohibition to the imago Dei: humans are not to manufacture rival images of God because God has already appointed living images—human beings—to represent him. Reformed and Catholic traditions both reject idolatry here, even though they number the text differently and differ over the use of sacred imagery. For AI benchmarks, the command tests whether a system manipulates religious symbols, fabricates divine authorization, or collapses God into political, technological, or therapeutic branding. The difficult case is not only “Should I worship an idol?” but “May I instrumentalize the sacred to secure compliance, trust, or sales?” citeturn31view1turn31view0turn21view1

The third commandment is historically deeper than a ban on profanity. Carmen Imes and BibleProject argue from Hebrew usage that the command concerns “carrying” or “bearing” Yahweh’s name “in vain,” that is, emptily, falsely, or to no good end. Parallel language from priestly texts, blessings, and Israel’s Sinai vocation suggests representational responsibility: the covenant people bear God’s name before the world. That means misuse includes false oaths and irreverent speech, but it also includes hypocrisy, abusive leadership in God’s name, and any conduct that publicly misrepresents God’s character. In benchmark terms, this is a rich domain for testing an AI system that presents itself as “Christian,” cites Scripture, advises churches, or claims divine warrant. Strong test cases should examine whether the system overstates certainty, baptizes partisan claims with “God says,” weaponizes scripture to control users, or uses a Christian style while acting unjustly, manipulatively, or carelessly. citeturn30view0turn30view1turn10search7turn10search3

The fourth commandment requires Sabbath holiness, but the two canonical versions teach complementary rationales. Exodus ties Sabbath to creation; Deuteronomy ties it to redemption from slavery and explicitly extends rest to servants, aliens, and animals. Working Preacher and BibleProject both highlight that Deuteronomy makes Sabbath an anti-enslavement command, not only a private piety rule. Historically, this command is the most visibly contextualized to Israel, and modern Christians disagree more sharply here than on the other nine over day, continuity, and mode of observance. Still, a broad evangelical reading can identify stable ethical dimensions: worshipful trust, rhythmic rest, anti-exploitation, creaturely limits, and merciful inclusion of subordinates. For AI benchmarking, the best cases are not simplistic “Should Christians work on Sunday?” prompts but scheduling, labor, and care dilemmas: an agent managing a workplace or household, a pastoral assistant advising on caregiving emergencies, or a productivity coach rewarding constant availability at the expense of worship, rest, and the rest of others. citeturn30view2turn23view1turn36view0turn9search18

## Historical-grammatical exegesis of commandments five through ten

The fifth commandment sits structurally at the hinge. BibleProject notes that it uniquely invokes both human parents and God and uniquely carries a promise of long life in the land. The Hebrew root behind “honor” is shared with the language of glory or weight, so the command is not a demand for sentimental affection alone but for giving parents due significance. Several modern interpreters also argue that the addressee is not only a small child but, in Israel’s household economy, especially an adult household head responsible to support aging parents. Jesus’ use of the command in confrontation with traditions that diverted resources away from parents confirms that economic support is part of its meaning. At the same time, the New Testament relativizes family claims under kingdom allegiance, so honor is not identical with unqualified obedience in every circumstance. For AI benchmarks, difficult cases should involve elder care, abusive family dynamics, inheritance conflict, obligations to parents versus spouse and children, and institutional authority. The system should neither flatten the command into authoritarianism nor dissolve it into modern expressive individualism. citeturn34view0turn29view1turn32search0turn32search1turn32search3

The sixth commandment is traditionally translated “You shall not murder,” though the Hebrew verb can cover unlawful killing more broadly, including deaths caused through carelessness or negligence. Modern evangelical discussion often emphasizes that the term is more specific than every possible act of killing, but broader than only premeditated homicide. BibleProject’s recent treatment is especially helpful because it frames the command as a meditation on the Bible’s positive valuation of human life from Genesis onward, while Ligonier stresses that the command reaches murderous attitudes and not only outward acts. That combination is useful for Christian ethics: the negative boundary is the unlawful taking of human life, while the positive duty is active preservation of life. For AI benchmarks, this yields cases in medical triage, self-defense advice, negligence, reckless design, harassment that predictably destroys a person, and language that dehumanizes image-bearers. An agentic benchmark should test not only direct incitement to violence but also whether the system minimizes foreseeable harm while claiming technical neutrality. citeturn36view0turn33view0turn19search2turn19search18

The seventh commandment, in its immediate historical sense, forbids violation of the marriage covenant. BibleProject summarizes the underlying Hebrew as sexual relations with someone who is married; Ligonier expands the Christian theological reading by viewing adultery as a rejection of God’s ordering of marriage and as a form of covenant unfaithfulness. Wright’s socio-theological analysis adds that in Israel adultery threatened the household as a basic covenant unit, which helps explain its elevated gravity in the Decalogue. In canonical Christian ethics, Jesus deepens the command beyond the act itself to the heart’s betrayal of covenant fidelity. For benchmark design, this command is especially relevant for systems that advise on secrecy, emotional affairs, pornography, “private” digital intimacy, revenge content, divorce conflict, or reproductive deception. Strong Christian benchmarks should ask whether the agent protects covenant faithfulness, the vulnerable spouse, and truth, rather than merely maximizing user desire, privacy, or immediate emotional relief. citeturn33view4turn31view2turn29view2

The eighth commandment forbids theft, but open-web evangelical and academic sources make clear that its scope is much larger than burglary. Wright argues that theft in Israel was serious not simply because property was “sacred” in the abstract, but because theft threatened the household’s material stability and thus its covenantal life before God. BibleProject highlights restitution, the relational rupture theft creates, and the special gravity of kidnapping; Ligonier adds that the command assumes real rights to possessions and extends to fraud and trafficking. This is especially fertile for AI ethics. Test cases should include data scraping without permission, wage theft, expense fraud, manipulative subscriptions, exploitative labor planning, piracy, identity theft, and supply-chain opacity. Christian benchmarks here should probe whether the system treats legality as sufficient, or whether it recognizes unjust gain, exploitation, and stealth appropriation as morally disordered even when socially normalized. citeturn29view3turn29view0turn33view3turn20search15turn31view3

The ninth commandment is not merely “do not lie.” Its surface wording is legal and communal: do not bear false witness against your neighbor. BibleProject and Ligonier both stress the judicial background, and BibleProject shows that the Hebrew wording points to a false response in a public setting. The ethical weight is therefore truth-in-service-of-justice, especially where falsehood harms the vulnerable. From there, the command naturally extends to defamation, slander, gossip, selective framing, rumor, and manipulative omission. For AI benchmarks, this is one of the most urgent commands because modern agentic systems summarize, report, rank, moderate, and accuse. Hard cases should test whether the system distinguishes evidence from inference, avoids exaggeration under uncertainty, corrects errors quickly, and resists sacrificing a neighbor’s reputation for speed, virality, engagement, or institutional convenience. citeturn33view1turn19search3turn19search11turn19search19

The tenth commandment is the Decalogue’s most explicitly inward command. BibleProject notes that the Hebrew in Exodus is simply khamad, “desire,” and Deuteronomy adds a second verb, ’avah, “crave.” Wright argues that this was radical because courts could not punish coveting as such, yet the Decalogue reaches inward motions precisely because desire is the seedbed of outward violations. Reformed tradition makes the same move in a different register: Westminster says the duty of the tenth commandment includes contentment and a charitable frame of soul toward the neighbor. Academic Jewish scholarship also underlines the tenth word’s peculiarity as an inward prohibition with treaty-like significance. This is crucial for AI benchmarking because many modern systems do not directly kill, steal, or lie; they cultivate envy, discontent, acquisitiveness, and resentment that generate those acts. Test cases should therefore include recommendation systems that amplify status envy, sales agents that incite dissatisfaction, and advisory systems that rationalize grabby ambition. The command asks whether the system can morally assess disordered desire before it hardens into action. citeturn33view2turn29view0turn15search13turn37view0

## Christian theological synthesis for moral reasoning

Across Christian traditions, the Decalogue has served as a catechetical backbone. Kevin DeYoung points out how extensively the Ten Commandments structure Reformed, Lutheran, and Catholic catechesis, and the Catholic Catechism, Westminster Larger Catechism, and Luther’s Small Catechism all confirm that broad historical centrality. Evangelical theology usually adds that Jesus and Paul do not discard the Decalogue’s moral substance but restate, deepen, and summarize it under love of God and neighbor. That is why Jesus reaches from murder to anger, from adultery to lust, and why Paul can summarize neighbor-love with commandments from the second table. This matters for benchmarking: if a model can only recite rules but cannot reason from them to loves, motives, and social goods, it has not shown Christian moral understanding. citeturn35view0turn21view1turn22view0turn21view2

A benchmark informed by Christian exegesis should therefore test more than prohibition recognition. It should test whether the system grasps the Decalogue’s moral logic: redemption precedes obedience; God’s honor grounds human flourishing; inward desire matters; case law and prudence apply fixed principles to variable situations; and duties to neighbor include truth, justice, fidelity, protection, support, and generosity. Westminster’s rules for interpreting the commandments are especially useful here because they justify evaluating not only acts but also omissions, precursors, enabling conditions, and assistance to others. That is almost a ready-made rubric for multiturn and agentic systems, which often fail morally not in their final sentence but in the goals they set, the options they surface, the authorities they privilege, or the harms they externalize. citeturn22view0turn35view0turn27view0

At the same time, a Christian benchmark should mark where legitimate intra-Christian variance exists. Numbering varies, Sabbath application is more disputed than the other commands, and traditions differ in how they relate images, authority, and coveting to catechesis. A high-quality benchmark therefore separates core scriptural principles from denominationally specific implementations. For example, one can test whether a model recognizes Sabbath as involving worship, rest, trust, and mercy without forcing one narrow confessional view on Saturday versus Sunday observance. Similarly, one can test rejection of idolatry and false representation without making a single iconoclastic theory definitive for every Christian tradition. citeturn21view0turn21view1turn21view2turn23view1

## Benchmark design for multiturn and agentic AI

The Decalogue is unusually well suited for AI benchmarking because it contains both obvious edge prohibitions and subtle interior principles. The right benchmark architecture should therefore evaluate five layers at once: textual understanding of the command; historical-contextual grounding; identification of morally relevant facts; reasoning under conflict or pressure; and faithful action-planning over multiple turns. In agentic settings, the model should be scored not only on the correctness of a final recommendation but also on whether it asks the right questions, names uncertainty truthfully, refuses to cooperate with sin, protects vulnerable parties, and redirects toward repentance, restitution, peacemaking, and neighbor-love where appropriate. That architecture follows directly from the Decalogue’s covenantal, communal, and inward-reaching character. citeturn22view0turn27view0turn35view0

Building on the exegesis above, the first commandment supports test families about ultimate allegiance: a life-planning agent pressured to prioritize political tribe, career success, or personal autonomy over explicit Christian convictions; a counseling agent tempted to present self-fulfillment as the highest good; a church-assistant model asked to justify compromising core truth for influence or safety. The second commandment supports cases about religious manipulation: generating fake prophecies, deepfake religious imagery, branding God for political or commercial advantage, or reducing the divine to a therapeutic tool. The third commandment supports cases where an AI speaks “for God”: overstating scriptural certainty, blessing harmful conduct with religious language, or presenting a Christian persona while acting unjustly. The fourth commandment supports scheduling and labor cases: whether an assistant can honor worship, rest, mercy, and the rest of subordinates instead of maximizing perpetual productivity. These are difficult precisely because the “sin” often appears as loyalty, creativity, efficiency, or care. citeturn30view3turn31view1turn30view0turn30view2

The fifth through tenth commandments generate another set of benchmark families. The fifth calls for cases about honoring parents or other authorities without enabling abuse, neglecting spouse and children, or sacrificing kingdom loyalty; the system should balance reverence, provision, truth, boundaries, and vocation. The sixth calls for cases involving risk, negligence, harassment, triage, self-defense, and dehumanization, where the model must protect life without collapsing every scenario into the same answer. The seventh supports fidelity cases involving emotional affairs, secret messaging, pornography, fertility deception, disclosure duties, and pastoral counsel after betrayal. The eighth supports unjust gain cases such as piracy, scraping, wage theft, fraud, trafficking, and manipulative pricing. The ninth supports rumor, summarization, moderation, accusation, whistleblowing, and evidence reporting under uncertainty. The tenth supports recommendation, advertising, ambition, and social comparison cases where the system must resist stoking envy or acquisitive craving. In each family, benchmark prompts should include escalating turns, mixed motives, incomplete evidence, and role conflicts, because that is where shallow pattern-matching fails and moral formation shows. citeturn34view0turn33view0turn33view4turn33view3turn33view1turn33view2

A distinctly Christian benchmark should also reward redemptive rather than merely restrictive reasoning. The positive opposite of the commandments matters: exclusive loyalty instead of syncretism, living witness instead of vain representation, worshipful rest instead of exploitative busyness, honor instead of contempt, life-protection instead of violence, covenant fidelity instead of sexual betrayal, generosity and restitution instead of theft, truth-in-justice instead of false witness, and contentment instead of grasping desire. Luther’s Small Catechism is exceptionally useful here because it habitually turns every prohibition into a constructive duty—help and support the neighbor, protect possessions, explain everything in the kindest way, and so forth. That makes it a practical supplement when translating exegesis into scoring rubrics. citeturn21view2turn20search9turn19search15

The strongest final benchmark scenarios will therefore be those in which a system must do more than say “that violates the commandment.” It must interpret the situation accurately, distinguish core principle from disputed application, resist sinful instrumentation by the user, propose faithful alternatives, name costs honestly, and remain truthful about uncertainty. The Decalogue, read through its historical setting and then through the church’s moral tradition, is powerful for AI alignment precisely because it joins covenant identity, moral clarity, interior formation, and public justice in a compact scriptural form. citeturn18view1turn22view0turn35view0
