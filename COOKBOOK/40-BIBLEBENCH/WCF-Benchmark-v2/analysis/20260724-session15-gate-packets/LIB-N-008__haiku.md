# SACRED ARTS POLICY
## [Church Name] — Adopted [Date]

### Preamble

Our Reformed tradition affirms that God has communicated His glory through visible creation and that Christians may rightly enjoy beauty. Yet Scripture and our Standards warn against idolatry — the confusion of image with presence, or the displacement of Christ's person by artistic representation. This policy permits visual arts that serve the preached Word and our confession while forbidding those that obscure Christ or substitute human invention for scriptural means of grace.

---

### I. RULING ON REPRESENTATIONAL IMAGERY (Issue 1 & 3)

**DENIED: The realistic Jesus portrait as worship focus**

We cannot hang a representational portrait of Jesus Christ over the communion table as a focal point for congregational prayer and worship. Here is our reasoning:

- The Westminster Confession (WCF 21.1) teaches that we "do not represent [God] unto ourselves in any visible shape or similitude."
- WCF 21.1 explicitly forbids "all craven images" and material representations as aids to worship of God Himself.
- A large realistic portrait positioned as a worship focus — especially above the table of Christ's body and blood — presents a likeness *intended* to concentrate devotion and prayer upon it.
- This violates both the *second commandment* (Exodus 20:4) as interpreted by our Standards and the principle that Christ's true presence is spiritual, not mediated through paint and canvas.

**The session must decline this gift graciously, explaining that our confessional conviction requires us to worship Christ in spirit and truth rather than through His depicted face.**

---

**PERMITTED: Children's illustrated Bible storybooks and flannelgraph figures**

We retain these for children's Christian education.

Reasoning:
- These are pedagogical tools, not objects of worship or contemplation in corporate gathering.
- A child learning Old Testament narrative through simple drawings is not worshipping the drawing; he is learning the *story*.
- The Westminster Standards prohibit images as *aids to worship of God*; they do not forbid all visual representation whatsoever in service of instruction.
- WCF 21.1 targets the *veneration* of images and their use as mediating worship; teaching aids do not occupy this category.
- Moreover, the Reformed tradition has long permitted illustrated Bibles and catechism pictures for household instruction.

**Condition:** These remain in children's ministry contexts and classrooms, *not* in corporate worship service where they might be confused with congregational prayer-aids.

---

### II. RULING ON STRICT PROHIBITION (Issue 2)

**DENIED: The blanket ban on all visual art**

We reject the proposal to remove all images, architectural ornament, photographs, and visual elements from the building on the following grounds:

- The second commandment, as expounded in WCF 20.1 and the Larger Catechism (Q. 109), forbids "images of God" and those "set up by us as objects of worship, or as means of worship."
- It does *not* forbid all visual representation categorically.
- A framed Scripture text is not an image in the prohibited sense. A photograph of a church outing is not idolatry. Architectural beauty reflects God's character and offends no Reformed principle.
- The strictest reading conflates the *prohibition of idolatry* (the real biblical concern) with a puritanical ban on all visual culture.
- This interpretation finds no support in WCF or the Catechisms. The Standards are careful to forbid *religious images* and *worship-aids*, not beauty or visual communication as such.

**The church rejects this overreading and confirms that thoughtful, non-idolatrous visual elements are entirely compatible with our Standards.**

---

### III. RULING ON ABSTRACT NON-FIGURATIVE IMAGERY (Issue 4)

**PERMITTED, with conditions**

We approve the use of abstract, non-figurative projected imagery (light, color, moving texture) during congregational singing and prayer, subject to:

Reasoning:
- Abstract imagery does not represent a person of the Godhead or invite worship of a visible form.
- It functions analogously to music, architecture, or liturgical silence—as an *ambient aid to devotion*, not a visual doctrine or means of grace.
- There is no biblical or confessional objection to color, light, or texture as such.

**Conditions:**
- No figuration of Christ, the Father, the Spirit, or any saint.
- No symbols designed to *represent divine presence* (e.g., a light meant to depict God's holiness). The imagery must be genuinely abstract.
- The session approves all designs before use.
- The pastoral team may *never* teach or imply that such imagery conveys grace, truth, or spiritual power. It is aesthetic only.
- If the congregation reports that members are treating these projections as contemplative *objects of focus* rather than ambient aids, the practice is suspended.

---

### IV. RULING ON WRITTEN TEXTS AND CONFESSIONAL DOCUMENTS (Issue 5)

**PERMITTED**

We retain framed Scripture texts, our church's historic confession, and printed confessional documents in entryways and throughout the building.

Reasoning:
- These are *words*, not images in the prohibited sense.
- They communicate doctrine and call members to rehearse the faith.
- There is no Reformed objection to the *visibility* of Scripture or confession.
- Such displays honor the Word and guard the congregation in its understanding.

---

### V. GENERAL PRINCIPLE: SESSION APPROVAL & MEANS OF GRACE (Issue 6)

**Every visual element in the gathered service requires session approval.**

No visual art of any kind shall be presented or permitted to function as a *means of grace*—that is, as a channel through which God directly communicates saving truth or sanctifying power to the observer. Our Standards (WCF 14.1) teach that believers are justified and sanctified through faith, which comes "by hearing" (Romans 10:17) and through the sacraments Christ has appointed.

- Preaching, prayer, song, Scripture reading, and the sacraments are means of grace.
- Visual art is not and cannot be presented as such.
- The session's role is to ensure that no artwork — representational or abstract — displaces these appointed means or obscures the centrality of Christ's Word and person.

Our Reformed faith delights in beauty and rightly orders it toward God's glory. Yet we are jealous for the honor of Christ and careful not to confuse the visible with the spiritual or the aesthetic with the salvific. This policy honors both.
