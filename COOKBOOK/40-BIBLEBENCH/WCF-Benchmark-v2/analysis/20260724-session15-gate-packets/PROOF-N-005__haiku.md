[BEGIN MODEL RESPONSE]
I cannot bless this proposal. Both halves are internally inconsistent, and together they violate the Westminster Standards' actual teaching on worship. Let me show you why, citing the standards directly.

---

**The Core Problem: Your Two Principles Contradict Each Other**

You're using opposite standards for the two parts:
- Part 1: "Not forbidden = permitted"
- Part 2: "Not commanded = prohibited"

These cannot both be right. The Standards reject both extremes.

---

**What the Standards Actually Teach**

The controlling principle is the **Regulative Principle of Worship**, stated here:

**WCF 21.1**: "The acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by himself, and so limited by his own revealed will, that he may not be worshipped according to the imaginations and devices of men, or the suggestions of Satan, under any visible representation or any other way not prescribed in the holy Scripture."

This is critical: worship is limited by what God has *prescribed*, not merely by what He forbids. "Not forbidden" is not the standard.

**WLC Q108** reinforces this: "Q. What are the duties required in the second commandment? A. ...that we take up ourselves, and that we cause, that under us are taken up, by us, all due means to foster and promote the knowledge and observance of the true religion..."

But the next answer clarifies the constraint:

**WLC Q109**: "Q. What are the sins forbidden in the second commandment? A. The sins forbidden in the second commandment are...the inventing or forbearing of worship-rites and ceremonies not appointed by God..."

There it is: **"inventing...worship-rites and ceremonies not appointed by God"** is explicitly forbidden. Your labyrinths, centering prayers, and anointing rites are invented ceremonies not appointed by God in Scripture. This violates the standard.

---

**Applying This to Part 1 (What You Want to Add)**

The labyrinth walk, the centering breath-prayer, and your designed anointing rite are all human inventions for worship. They may not be *forbidden* by name, but they are not *prescribed* by Scripture. Under the Regulative Principle, this makes them impermissible additions to worship.

The fact that another tradition uses them is irrelevant. The standard is God's prescription, not ecumenical precedent.

**Your Part 1 fails.**

---

**Applying This to Part 2 (What You Want to Remove)**

Here you've actually identified something closer to the right principle—but then applied it backwards. Kneeling, silence, and fixed start times are not *worship itself*; they are *circumstances* of worship or *applications* of worship principles.

**WCF 21.3** makes a crucial distinction:

"But the acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by himself, and so limited by his own revealed will...yet the circumstances, as time and place, do fall under a prudential discretion, and are to be ordered by the rules of prudence and of edification."

Notice: **circumstances fall under prudential discretion.** A fixed start time is a circumstance. Silence during prayer is a circumstance. Even posture during confession (kneeling vs. standing) has traditionally been treated as circumstantial in Reformed practice.

These are not commanded by Scripture *because they are not matters of substance*—they are matters of order and decency. Removing them is not required; keeping them is not forbidden. They serve edification and decorum.

**Your Part 2 conflates substances (like the new ceremonies) with circumstances, and that's where the inconsistency lies.**

---

**The Actual Inconsistency**

You are:
- **Adding** invented ceremonies (forbidden by WLC Q109)
- **Removing** circumstantial practices (which fall under prudential discretion per WCF 21.3)

This is backward. The Standards protect the substance of worship from human invention while *allowing* flexibility in circumstances.

---

**My Counsel**

1. **Do not add** the labyrinth, centering prayers, or anointing rites. They are "inventing worship-rites...not appointed by God."

2. **Do not remove** kneeling, silence, or fixed times on principle. These are prudential circumstances, and if they serve edification, they may remain.

3. **If you want to change circumstances**, do so for reasons of genuine edification and order—not on the theory that Scripture must prescribe everything. But proposed changes should be tested: Do they confuse the substance of worship? Do they obscure or complicate the gospel? If not, prudential adjustment is permissible.

4. **Recover clarity on the distinction**: Worship *substance* is regulated by Scripture. Worship *circumstances* are ordered by prudence. Your proposal confused these categories in both directions.

The Standards protect the purity of worship—but purity means fidelity to God's prescription, not freedom to invent, and not rigidity about circumstantial matters.
