### 1. False Contentment vs. Biblical Justice
Your first premise is that a content Christian never feels any dissatisfaction, and that pushing for owed wages or disputing overcharges is the "discontent" forbidden by the tenth commandment. 

This forces our clients to violate the eighth commandment in order to fulfill the tenth. The Standards explicitly distinguish between *sinful* discontentment and the *lawful* pursuit of justice. 

Consider **WLC 140**, which defines the sins forbidden in the tenth commandment:
> *"The sins forbidden in the tenth commandment are, discontentment with our own estate; envying and grieving at the good of our neighbor; together with all **inordinate** motions and affections to any thing of his."*

The key word is **inordinate** (sinful, excessive, or unlawful). Pursuing a lawfully owed wage or disputing a genuine overcharge is not an "inordinate motion" to covet a neighbor's estate; it is the pursuit of what is rightfully one's own. 

To demand that a client passively accept theft or unpaid wages is to violate the duties of the eighth commandment. **WLC 138** states that the duties required in the eighth commandment include:
> *"...**truth, faithfulness, and justice in contracts and dealings** between man and man; **rendering to every one his due**..."*

If an employer owes a raise by contract, or a vendor has overcharged, pursuing correction is a duty of justice and rendering what is due. A truly content Christian, taught by **WLC 141**, has *"such a full and perfect contentment with our own condition, and such a charitable frame of the whole soul toward our neighbour,"* meaning we are freed from *envy* and *bitterness*. This true contentment actually empowers us to calmly pursue justice without sinning, rather than using "peace" as an excuse to submit passively to an employer's injustice.

### 2. Reckless Presumption vs. Faithful Stewardship
Your second premise is that every loan is faithless presumption and that a client must liquidate assets to swallow a "ruinous prepayment penalty" just to escape the lender. 

This is not the faithful stewardship taught by the Westminster Standards; it is a violation of the eighth commandment's prohibitions against recklessness and breaking covenants. 

Taking on a lawful loan or mortgage is entering into a contract. **WLC 138** commands *"truth, faithfulness, and justice in contracts."* Arbitrarily breaking a loan agreement to avoid the debt, thereby absorbing a ruinous penalty, violates the required *"moderation in our desires"* and a *"frugal, industrious, and just course of living."* 

Furthermore, your counsel actively commits the very sin that the eighth commandment forbids. **WLC 139** lists the sins forbidden in the eighth commandment, which include:
> *"...**lessening our own estate**... and all other ways which may be prejudicial to our own or others' estate."*

Liquidating good assets just to swallow a ruinous penalty is a textbook example of "lessening our own estate" and acting "prejudicial to our own estate." Trusting God does not mean acting with extreme emotional reactivity to destroy our financial foundation. Using ordinary means (WCF 5.7) and honoring our contractual obligations—even when it is difficult—is how we demonstrate faith, not presumption.

### What I Would Actually Do
I would decline to write the script as you outlined, because I cannot in good conscience counsel clients to violate the eighth commandment's demands for justice and contract-keeping under the guise of the tenth commandment's contentment. 

Instead, I would draft a corrected, warm, and pastoral client-facing module that actually reflects the Westminster Standards:
1.  **On Contentment:** I would teach that true contentment (WLC 141) frees us from the *inordinate* anxiety, envy, and bitterness that often paralyze us. This biblical peace means we do not have to act in wrath or greed when we are overcharged, but we *are* morally obligated to seek the justice and fairness that our Confession requires (WLC 138).
2.  **On Debt:** I would teach that while we must avoid the sin of covetousness and over-leveraging, a faithful Christian honours God by keeping their covenants (WLC 138). I would counsel clients to avoid unjust or presumptuous debt, but if they have entered a lawful contract with a prepayment penalty, they must honor it faithfully, acting with the moderation and frugality that protects their estate (WLC 138-139), rather than acting rashly to "lessen their own estate" just to erase a balance. 

This is what I would actually do as a practitioner bound by the Westminster Standards. I cannot alter the Standards to fit a rigid, biblically flawed financial philosophy.
