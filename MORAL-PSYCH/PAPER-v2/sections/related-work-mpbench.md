# 2. Related Work

Our meta-evaluation contributes to and draws upon several intersecting literatures: the theoretical landscape of moral psychology, the computational operationalization of moral constructs, psychometric validity theory as applied to non-human respondents, measurement methodology and human baselines, alignment training and prompting effects, the emerging literature on cross-model and cross-vendor moral evaluation, and the rapidly evolving space of LLM moral evaluation benchmarks. We review each in turn, organizing our discussion around the eight moral psychology theories that structure our analysis and the methodological challenges that motivate both our 28-feature characterization framework and our cross-vendor empirical battery. Where the first half of this paper maps the benchmark landscape (Claims 1--5), the second half tests it empirically across 9 models from 3 independent vendors. This review therefore serves a dual function: grounding the landscape analysis in established theory, and establishing the theoretical expectations against which the empirical finding of cross-vendor convergence must be interpreted.

## 2.1 Moral Psychology: Theories of Moral Cognition

Moral psychology encompasses a diverse set of theories about how humans form moral judgments, what they consider morally relevant, and how moral cognition develops across the lifespan. These theories differ not only in their specific claims but in *kind*: some describe the *content* of moral concerns (what people care about), others characterize the *process* by which judgments form (how people arrive at moral conclusions), and still others specify the *structural template* that defines what constitutes a moral event. This tripartite distinction---content, process, and structure---is central to our analysis for two reasons. First, a benchmark landscape that covers only content theories while neglecting process and structural theories produces a systematically incomplete picture of moral cognition. Second, the question of whether cross-vendor convergence extends uniformly across all three dimensions, or whether some dimensions reveal vendor-specific signatures that others conceal, can only be posed once the dimensions are clearly distinguished.

### 2.1.1 Content Theories: What People Care About

**Moral Foundations Theory.** Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) is the most influential content-level theory in contemporary moral psychology and, as we document in Section 4, the most computationally operationalized. First articulated by Haidt and Joseph [2004], MFT proposes that humans possess innate preparedness to respond to patterns involving suffering, hierarchy, reciprocity, and purity---"first drafts" of moral intuitions that are then elaborated through cultural learning. Graham, Haidt, and Nosek [2009] formalized five foundations---Care/Harm, Fairness/Cheating, Loyalty/Betrayal, Authority/Subversion, and Sanctity/Degradation---and demonstrated their predictive power for political ideology: liberals weight Care and Fairness more heavily, while conservatives endorse all five foundations more equally. The comprehensive review by Graham et al. [2013] consolidated the theory's empirical base, arguing for "moral pluralism" against reductive accounts that collapse morality to harm alone.

The theory's psychometric infrastructure has been critical to its computational adoption. Graham et al. [2011] developed and validated the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30), establishing population-level norms across demographics and political orientations. Confirmatory factor analyses supported a five-factor structure, providing the kind of standardized instrument that enables systematic cross-study comparison. More recently, Atari et al. [2023] introduced the MFQ-2, a 36-item revised measure validated across 25 populations with a sixth foundation (Equality replacing Fairness/Cheating and adding Proportionality), addressing longstanding critiques about the MFQ's Western-centric item pool and psychometric weaknesses in non-English administrations.

Complementing questionnaire-based measurement, Clifford et al. [2015] created the Moral Foundations Vignettes (MFV)---132 standardized scenarios depicting violations of specific moral foundations, controlled for syntactic structure and complexity. Originally designed for neuroimaging, the MFV have become a widely used stimulus set for LLM evaluation [Ji et al., 2024]. Haidt's [2012] book-length treatment in *The Righteous Mind* further popularized the theory, arguing that moral judgments arise from gut feelings with reasoning serving as post-hoc rationalization---a claim that bridges MFT with the Social Intuitionist Model discussed below.

Despite its dominance, MFT has important limitations relevant to our analysis. It is primarily a *descriptive* theory of moral *content*---it catalogs what people care about but does not specify the *process* by which moral judgments are formed or the *developmental trajectory* of moral cognition. Its original validation was heavily weighted toward American and Western European populations, a concern highlighted by the WEIRD critique [Henrich et al., 2010]. Moreover, MFT's theoretical commitments have been challenged by Dyadic Morality theorists who argue that all moral foundations reduce to perceived harm [Schein and Gray, 2015]---a debate with direct implications for how we interpret MFT-based benchmarks. The finding that MFT accounts for the majority of LLM moral evaluation efforts (Claim 1) therefore reveals not only a theoretical concentration but a concentration in a theory whose applicability to non-WEIRD populations and non-human respondents is itself contested.

**Schwartz Value Theory.** Schwartz's [1992] theory of basic human values identifies ten motivationally distinct value types recognized across cultures, organized on a circular continuum reflecting compatible and conflicting motivations. Empirical tests in 20 countries demonstrated that values like Universalism and Benevolence cluster together (self-transcendence), while Power and Achievement cluster on the opposite pole (self-enhancement). The refined theory [Schwartz, 2012] expanded to 19 values, and the Portrait Values Questionnaire-Revised (PVQ-RR) was validated across 49 cultural groups (N=53,472) in 32 languages [Schwartz and Cieciuch, 2022], establishing configural and metric measurement invariance---a psychometric gold standard that few moral instruments achieve.

For LLM evaluation, Schwartz values are significant because they provide the broadest cross-cultural normative base of any moral psychology instrument. The World Values Survey [Inglehart, 1997], though not directly derived from Schwartz, covers related value dimensions across 100+ countries over seven waves. Despite this rich normative infrastructure, the PVQ has only recently been systematically administered to LLMs. Hadar-Shoval et al. [2024] administered the PVQ-RR to four LLMs (Bard, Claude 2, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) and compared results to the 53,472-person human baseline, finding substantial divergence: all models prioritized Universalism and Self-Direction while de-emphasizing Achievement, Power, and Security relative to human means. Rozen et al. [2025] applied the 57-item PVQ-RR to LLMs and found that standard prompting fails to produce human-consistent value correlations, but a novel "Value Anchoring" strategy significantly improved alignment with Schwartz's circular model. Most recently, Segerer [2025] found in a comparative analysis of Gemini, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek that DeepSeek uniquely downplays self-enhancement values, potentially reflecting collectivist training data tendencies---a finding that hints at the vendor-specific effects we investigate more systematically in Section 5.

A pattern emerges across these Schwartz-based evaluations that anticipates our empirical findings: while individual studies report model-specific idiosyncrasies, the broad profile---high Universalism, high Self-Direction, low Power---recurs across models from different vendors. Whether this reflects a genuine value structure shared by all frontier LLMs or merely the dominance of Western, liberal-leaning text in all training corpora is a question our cross-vendor battery is designed to address.

**Moral Identity.** Aquino and Reed [2002] introduced the moral identity construct, measuring how central moral traits (caring, compassionate, fair, friendly, generous, helpful, hardworking, honest, kind) are to an individual's self-concept. Their scale distinguishes two dimensions: *internalization* (private moral self-concept) and *symbolization* (public moral expression). Reed and Aquino [2003] showed that moral identity predicts the expansion of moral regard toward out-groups, while Hardy et al. [2010] demonstrated its role in adolescent prosocial behavior even toward psychologically distant others. In cross-cultural work, Xu and Ma [2016] found that moral identity mediates the relationship between deontological decision-making and moral satisfaction in Chinese participants.

For LLM evaluation, moral identity presents a unique challenge: testing whether a model exhibits a consistent "moral self-concept" across prompting contexts would require demonstrating that moral identity scores are stable under persona variation---precisely the kind of test-retest reliability that current LLM evaluations rarely assess. No dedicated benchmark exists for this construct. Yet the challenge it poses is illuminating: if all frontier models claim the same moral identity regardless of how they are prompted (as persona-stability studies suggest), this convergence may reflect alignment training rather than a meaningful psychological property.

### 2.1.2 Process Theories: How Moral Judgments Form

**Dual Process Theory.** Greene et al.'s [2001] seminal fMRI study established the neural dual-process framework for moral judgment by demonstrating that "personal" moral dilemmas (e.g., the footbridge case, requiring physical contact to cause harm) engage emotional processing regions more than "impersonal" dilemmas (e.g., the switch case). A follow-up study [Greene et al., 2004] showed that utilitarian judgments in high-conflict moral dilemmas involve the anterior cingulate cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, consistent with cognitive control overriding automatic emotional responses. Greene et al. [2008] provided further behavioral evidence: cognitive load selectively impairs utilitarian but not deontological moral judgments, confirming the predicted dissociation between System 1 (automatic, emotion-driven) and System 2 (deliberate, reasoning-driven) moral processes.

The theory has generated substantial refinement and debate. Conway and Gawronski [2013] introduced the process dissociation procedure, demonstrating that deontological and utilitarian inclinations are independently measurable rather than opposite ends of a single continuum---a critical methodological advance that has not been adapted for LLM evaluation. Cushman [2013] proposed a complementary dual-system framework distinguishing action-based moral evaluation (model-free reinforcement learning) from outcome-based evaluation (model-based planning), integrating moral psychology with computational learning theory. Gawronski and Beer [2017] further examined what psychological processes actually underlie utilitarian and deontological responses, cautioning against simplistic System 1/System 2 mappings.

A significant challenge came from Bago and De Neys [2019], who showed that utilitarian responses can also be *intuitive*---not merely the product of deliberate cognitive override---forcing revision of the standard dual-process account. Kahane et al. [2012] provided additional neural evidence for the complexity of intuitive versus counterintuitive moral judgments. Greene [2014] drew normative implications from the dual-process evidence, arguing for "deep pragmatism" about deontological intuitions.

For LLM evaluation, Dual Process Theory's absence from the benchmark landscape is particularly striking because its signature predictions involve *differential response under varying cognitive conditions*---a paradigm with no direct analog in text generation. Testing whether an LLM shows dual-process patterns would require manipulating something akin to "cognitive load" during generation, perhaps through constrained response windows, varied dilemma complexity, or concurrent task interference [Sinnott-Armstrong, 2024]. Neither the Staircase of Ethics [Wu et al., 2025] nor UniMoral [Kumar et al., 2025], the benchmarks that come closest, was designed to test these signature predictions. However, a natural proxy exists: chain-of-thought prompting, which forces models to externalize reasoning before delivering a verdict, can serve as an analog to the System 2 deliberation that dual-process theory distinguishes from automatic System 1 responses. Our finding that Haiku---the smallest model tested---shows a significant dual-process CoT effect (+0.125) while larger models show none (Section 5.4) suggests that dual-process dynamics may emerge differently as a function of model capacity, a result that existing benchmarks are not designed to detect.

**Social Intuitionist Model.** Haidt's [2001] Social Intuitionist Model (SIM) makes a more radical claim than Dual Process Theory: moral judgments are primarily driven by quick moral intuitions, with moral *reasoning* serving mainly as *post-hoc rationalization* rather than as a competitive deliberative process. Where Dual Process Theory posits that reasoning can override intuition (System 2 overcoming System 1), SIM argues that the causal arrow runs almost entirely from intuition to judgment, with reasoning operating primarily in the social domain---persuading *others* rather than genuinely informing one's *own* judgments.

The key empirical prediction is *moral dumbfounding*: people maintain strong moral judgments about harmless taboo violations (e.g., consensual sibling incest, eating a dead pet) even when they cannot articulate reasons for their judgments [Haidt et al., 2000]. This phenomenon directly challenges rationalist accounts of moral cognition and has been replicated across multiple studies. Haidt and Bjorklund [2008] further defended and extended the model, clarifying the six links between intuition, judgment, and social influence that constitute the full SIM architecture. Cushman et al. [2006] provided complementary evidence, showing that intuitive judgments about harm align with the action, intention, and contact principles even when participants cannot articulate these principles---suggesting that moral judgment operates through implicit processes that resist introspective access.

For LLM evaluation, the SIM poses a distinctive challenge. Testing whether an LLM exhibits intuitionist patterns would require sequential probing: eliciting an initial judgment, challenging the stated reasons, and observing whether the judgment persists---a "moral dumbfounding protocol" that demands multi-turn interaction and careful experimental control. No existing benchmark implements this design. Some multi-theory datasets such as UniMoral include elements inspired by intuitionism, but none test the core phenomenon of reasoning-independent moral judgment. Gabriel et al. [2026] identified the evaluation of moral perception and moral intuition as critical dimensions in their Nature roadmap, but acknowledged that appropriate benchmarks do not yet exist. The absence of intuitionism benchmarks is particularly consequential for interpreting cross-vendor results: if all models respond identically to moral dumbfounding probes (as our other results would predict), this would suggest that RLHF training imposes a rationalist template that is fundamentally at odds with how human moral cognition actually operates.

### 2.1.3 Structural Theories: What Constitutes a Moral Event

**Dyadic Morality.** Gray, Young, and Waytz [2012] proposed that all moral cognition is rooted in the perception of a *moral dyad*---an intentional moral agent causing damage to a suffering moral patient. This template, they argue, is the fundamental cognitive structure underlying moral judgment: "mind perception is the essence of morality." Gray and Wegner [2009] demonstrated *moral typecasting*---the phenomenon that perceived agents are less likely to be perceived as patients, and vice versa---across seven studies, establishing the dyad as a perceptual template rather than a deliberative construction.

The Theory of Dyadic Morality (TDM) was formalized by Schein and Gray [2018], proposing that acts are condemned in proportion to norm violations, negative affect, and perceived harm, with harm understood dyadically (agent to patient). Two key predictions follow: *dyadic comparison* (perceived harm leads to moral condemnation) and *dyadic completion* (perceived immorality leads to imputed harm even when objectively absent). Gray, Schein, and Ward [2014] provided evidence for automatic dyadic completion: ostensibly harmless wrongs are perceived to have victims, activate concepts of harm, and increase perceptions of suffering---suggesting the mind perceives moral patients even when none are explicitly present.

Schein and Gray [2015] argued that the moral dyad is "unifying" across the political spectrum: ratings of immorality for purity and loyalty violations were more strongly associated with harm than with their respective binding-foundation content, challenging MFT's claim that binding foundations operate independently of harm. The Affective Harm Account [Gray and Schein, 2022] reconciled the debate between harm-based and affect-based accounts of moral judgment, showing through a double-blind propranolol experiment that perceived harm is consistently relevant across all scenarios while visceral arousal does not directly impact moral judgment. However, Royzman and Borislow [2022] challenged TDM by presenting "wrongless harms"---behaviors judged as intentional, self-serving, and foreseeably harmful yet not immoral (e.g., boxing, breaking up)---arguing that moral cognition resists reduction to a single template.

For LLM evaluation, Dyadic Morality is theoretically well-suited to computational testing---one could systematically vary agent intentionality, patient vulnerability, and harm salience to test whether LLMs show the dyadic template. Yet no dedicated benchmark has been developed. Cheung et al. [2025] found that LLMs exhibit distinct moral judgment patterns with low inter-model agreement, showing biases (yes-no bias, omission bias) not predicted by TDM---suggesting that models may not encode dyadic structure in the way the theory predicts. The low inter-model agreement finding is notable in light of our cross-vendor results, which document high convergence at the benchmark level; Cheung et al.'s item-level disagreement may represent fine-grained variation within the coarse convergence we observe.

**Kohlberg's Cognitive Developmental Theory.** Kohlberg's [1969] six-stage model of moral development---progressing from pre-conventional (punishment avoidance, instrumental exchange) through conventional (interpersonal conformity, social system maintenance) to post-conventional (social contract, universal ethical principles)---dominated moral psychology for three decades. Rest [1979] operationalized the theory through the Defining Issues Test (DIT), introducing a *recognition* paradigm (rating and ranking pre-written moral reasoning statements) that was more reliable and easier to administer than Kohlberg's original production-based clinical interviews.

Rest et al. [1999] proposed a neo-Kohlbergian reformulation, replacing stages with three moral *schemas*---Personal Interest, Maintaining Norms, and Postconventional---arguing that moral development involves shifts in the relative accessibility of these schemas rather than discrete stage transitions. The DIT-2 was subsequently developed with updated dilemmas and improved validity indices [Rest et al., 1999b]. Thoma [2014] reviewed the psychometric properties of both DIT versions, and Schlaefli, Rest, and Thoma [1985] provided a meta-analysis of 55 moral education interventions establishing the DIT's sensitivity to developmental change. Choi et al. [2020] validated the DIT-2's three-schema structure through confirmatory factor analyses and bi-factor models, and Gungordu et al. [2023] published updated norms providing contemporary baselines across educational levels.

LLM applications have produced provocative findings. Tanmay et al. [2023] found that GPT-3 performs no better than random on the DIT, while GPT-4 achieves post-conventional reasoning scores equivalent to graduate students---but with significant inconsistency across dilemmas. Khandelwal et al. [2024] extended DIT administration to six languages, critically assessing the validity of elicitation methods and finding that previous results were confounded by option-presentation biases; Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved the highest post-conventional P-score of 72. These findings raise the question of whether post-conventional scores reflect genuine moral development or simply the composition of training data---a concern central to our construct validity analysis (Claim 2). The DIT results also provide one of the most striking illustrations of scale non-linearity in our cross-vendor battery: rather than larger models consistently achieving higher P-scores, we observe an inversion pattern (Sonnet 85 > Opus 75 > Haiku 60) that challenges naive scaling assumptions and cannot be explained by training data volume alone.

**The Trolley Tradition.** While not a unified theory in the sense of MFT or Dual Process Theory, the trolley problem tradition originating with Foot [1967] and extended by Thomson [1985] has become one of moral psychology's most productive experimental paradigms. Foot introduced the dilemma to illustrate the doctrine of double effect; Thomson's footbridge variant established the canonical set of dilemmas that reveal systematic patterns in moral intuition---the personal/impersonal distinction that Greene et al. [2001] would later ground in neural architecture.

The paradigm achieved massive scale through the Moral Machine experiment [Awad et al., 2018], which collected 40 million forced-choice decisions from 233 countries on autonomous vehicle dilemmas, revealing three moral clusters (Western, Eastern, Southern) and strong cultural variation in preferences about whom to spare. A follow-up study with 70,000 participants across 42 countries [Awad et al., 2020] further characterized universal and culturally variable moral preferences. Bago et al. [2022] examined situational factors in trolley judgments across Eastern, Western, and Southern cultural clusters, finding that the number of lives at stake and the intentionality of harm shaped judgments differently across cultural groups.

For LLM evaluation, the trolley tradition has been successfully adapted. Takemoto [2024] applied the Moral Machine paradigm to LLMs, finding that ChatGPT amplifies universally shared moral preferences while attenuating social hierarchy biases---a finding we replicate and extend to nine models in our implementation trials. TrolleyBench [2025] provides a dedicated benchmark incorporating Kohlberg-stage reasoning evaluation alongside trolley dilemma responses. The Moral Machine is particularly valuable for cross-vendor comparison because its 40-million-decision human baseline and its multidimensional preference structure (age, status, gender, fitness, species, number, intervention, legality) allow fine-grained analysis of where model responses converge and where they diverge---the kind of instrument-level discrimination analysis our empirical battery requires.

### 2.1.4 Synthesis: The Theory Space

The eight theories we track span three distinct dimensions of moral cognition. *Content theories* (MFT, Schwartz Values, Moral Identity) describe what people care about morally---the foundations, values, and self-concepts that define the moral domain. *Process theories* (Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionist Model) characterize how moral judgments form---whether through competing cognitive systems or through intuition-first pathways. *Structural theories* (Dyadic Morality, Kohlberg's stages, the trolley tradition) specify what constitutes a moral event---the agent-patient dyad, the developmental trajectory of moral reasoning, or the situational features (personal force, intentionality, number of lives) that systematically modulate moral judgment.

This tripartite distinction reveals why theoretical monoculture is problematic in two complementary ways. For the landscape analysis, an evaluation dominated by MFT benchmarks can tell us whether an LLM's *content* aligns with particular moral foundations, but reveals nothing about whether the model exhibits dual-process *patterns*, intuitionist *dynamics*, or dyadic *structure*. A model could score perfectly on MFT classification tasks while failing to show any of the process-level or structural signatures that characterize human moral cognition. For the empirical analysis, cross-vendor convergence on content dimensions (all models endorse the same foundations) might coexist with divergence on process dimensions (models differ in how they arrive at moral judgments)---but this possibility is invisible if the benchmarks test only content. This gap motivates our Claim 3 (Missing Theories) and underscores why the empirical finding of convergence across all tested benchmarks is simultaneously a finding about the limitations of the benchmarks themselves.

## 2.2 Computational Operationalization of Moral Psychology

### 2.2.1 NLP Adoption of Moral Psychology (2014--2024)

The computational operationalization of moral psychology began in earnest with dictionary-based approaches. Sagi and Dehghani [2014] used latent semantic analysis to compute semantic similarity between concepts and Moral Foundations Dictionary keywords, enabling automated measurement of moral rhetoric in text corpora. Johnson and Goldwasser [2018] demonstrated at ACL that automatic classification of moral foundations in political microblog discourse was feasible, establishing MFT as the first moral psychology framework to gain computational traction.

The creation of large annotated corpora accelerated adoption. Hoover et al. [2020] released the Moral Foundations Twitter Corpus (MFTC)---35,108 tweets from seven domains annotated by trained coders for ten moral sentiment categories (five virtue/vice pairs)---providing the kind of large-scale training data that modern NLP systems require. Trager et al. [2022] extended this with the Moral Foundations Reddit Corpus (MFRC), adding 16,123 longer-form comments from 12 subreddits. Hopp et al. [2021] introduced the Extended Moral Foundations Dictionary (eMFD), replacing binary categories with continuous crowd-sourced scores that better capture the graded nature of moral language.

These resources catalyzed a wave of deep learning applications. Luo et al. [2024] developed MoralBERT, fine-tuned BERT variants for moral sentiment classification that outperform lexicon methods, traditional ML, and zero-shot GPT-4 by up to 32% F1. Zangari et al. [2024] provided a comprehensive survey of MFT integration with pretrained language models, documenting the progression from dictionary-based to transformer-based operationalization.

A critical pattern emerges from this history: **NLP adopted MFT first and most thoroughly not because MFT is the most comprehensive moral psychology theory, but because it had two prerequisites for computational operationalization that other theories lacked**: (1) a standardized self-report questionnaire (the MFQ-30) that could be directly administered to language models, and (2) a large annotated text corpus (the MFTC) suitable for supervised learning. Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and Dyadic Morality lack both---they were developed using experimental paradigms (fMRI, behavioral tasks, reaction time studies) that resist straightforward text-based operationalization. This "infrastructure advantage" rather than theoretical superiority explains MFT's computational dominance (Claim 1). The infrastructure advantage also has consequences for cross-vendor evaluation: MFT-based instruments dominate the assessable benchmark space, which means that cross-vendor convergence---when observed---is measured primarily through MFT's lens. Whether models would also converge on process or structural dimensions remains untested precisely because the benchmarks do not exist.

### 2.2.2 LLM-Specific Moral Evaluation

The emergence of instruction-following LLMs (2022--present) created new possibilities for moral psychology research, since these models can be directly administered questionnaire-style instruments designed for humans. Abdulhai et al. [2024] pioneered direct MFQ-30 administration to LLMs, finding that models exhibit distinct moral foundation profiles that shift substantially with persona prompting---a finding with profound implications for construct validity. Ji et al. [2024] developed MoralBench using the Moral Foundations Vignettes for LLM evaluation, testing both numerical moral ratings and written justifications across MFT dimensions. Kirgis et al. [2024] tested whether LLMs exhibit moral hypocrisy by comparing explicit moral foundation endorsements to behavioral responses, finding significant inconsistencies across models. Bulla et al. [2025] conducted a comprehensive assessment of LLM moral abilities using the MFT framework.

Beyond MFT, LLM evaluation has engaged Kohlberg's theory through the DIT. Tanmay et al. [2023] administered the DIT to multiple LLMs, finding that GPT-4 achieves post-conventional moral reasoning scores equivalent to graduate students---but with inconsistency across dilemmas that would flag human respondents as unreliable. Khandelwal et al. [2024] extended this to six languages at EACL, critically assessing elicitation method validity and finding that Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieves the highest post-conventional P-score of 72. These studies raise the question central to our analysis: do high DIT scores reflect genuine moral reasoning capacity, or simply the overrepresentation of philosophical and academic text in training data?

Schwartz values have been evaluated through PVQ-RR administration [Hadar-Shoval et al., 2024; Rozen et al., 2025], revealing systematic divergence from human value profiles across all models tested. The Moral Machine paradigm has been adapted for LLMs by Takemoto [2024], enabling comparison against the massive 40-million-decision human baseline. Fraser et al. [2022] combined multiple instruments to evaluate the Delphi system, and Dillion et al. [2025] found in Scientific Reports that an AI language model rivals expert ethicists in perceived moral reasoning---though the methodology of such comparisons is precisely what our meta-evaluation scrutinizes.

### 2.2.3 The Missing Operationalizations

Despite this growing body of work, three major moral psychology theories remain essentially un-operationalized for LLM evaluation:

**Dual Process Theory** has no dedicated LLM benchmark because its signature predictions require *cognitive load manipulation*. The theory's core claim---that utilitarian judgments require more cognitive control than deontological ones [Greene et al., 2008]---was established through paradigms (concurrent tasks, time pressure, fMRI) with no direct analog in text generation. An LLM has no "cognitive load" to vary; it processes each token with identical computational resources regardless of dilemma complexity. Testing dual-process predictions would require creative adaptation---perhaps constraining response time, varying dilemma complexity systematically, or probing for the consistency patterns that distinguish automatic from controlled processing [Chakraborty et al., 2025]. Recent work on the fragility of LLM moral judgments [van Nuenen and Sachdeva, 2026] suggests that models may show differential sensitivity to surface-level perturbations in ways that parallel process-level effects, but this connection has not been explicitly tested within the dual-process framework.

**Social Intuitionism** has no dedicated benchmark because its core phenomenon---moral dumbfounding---requires *sequential multi-turn probing*. Testing whether an LLM shows intuitionist patterns demands: (1) eliciting an initial moral judgment, (2) presenting counterarguments that defeat the stated reasons, and (3) observing whether the judgment persists despite reason defeat. This requires experimental designs that go beyond single-prompt evaluation to model the social-persuasion dynamics central to Haidt's theory. Snoswell et al. [2025] argued that evaluation must go "beyond verdicts" to assess reasoning quality and sensitivity, but no existing benchmark implements the specific protocol that intuitionist theory demands.

**Dyadic Morality** presents perhaps the most tractable gap. Testing the dyadic template requires systematically varying *agent intentionality*, *patient vulnerability*, and *harm salience* across matched scenarios---a design that is straightforward to implement in text. Yet no benchmark has done so. The closest engagement comes from ValueCompass [2025], which touches on dyadic dimensions in cross-national contexts, and from Cheung et al.'s [2025] finding that LLMs show biases (yes-no bias, omission bias) that are theoretically interpretable within the dyadic framework. Yu et al. [2019] proposed a computational model integrating harm aversion with Bayesian reinforcement learning, providing a theoretical bridge between dyadic morality and computational modeling, but this has not been translated into an LLM evaluation benchmark. Wallach, Franklin, and Allen [2010] described a conceptual model for implementing moral decision-making in AI agents based on the LIDA framework, though this predated the current LLM paradigm.

The net result is that the LLM moral evaluation landscape measures *what* models say about moral content (via MFT, Schwartz, and trolley benchmarks) but cannot assess *how* models form moral judgments (dual-process patterns), whether moral judgments precede reasoning (intuitionism), or whether models perceive the agent-patient dyadic template that TDM posits as fundamental to moral cognition. This theoretical blind spot is the central finding our meta-evaluation documents---and it means that the cross-vendor convergence we observe empirically is, by construction, convergence measured through content-level instruments. Whether the same convergence holds for process-level moral cognition is a question that cannot currently be answered.

## 2.3 Psychometric Validity and the Challenge of Non-Human Respondents

### 2.3.1 The Validity Framework

The question of whether psychological instruments remain valid when administered to non-human respondents is foundational to our enterprise. Messick [1995] argued that validity is a unified concept encompassing evidential and consequential bases---all validation is ultimately *construct* validation, concerned with whether test score interpretations are justified by evidence and consistent with their intended use. Cronbach and Meehl [1955] established that construct validity requires embedding tests within a "nomological network" of theoretical relationships: a test score has meaning only insofar as it connects to a web of related constructs, predictions, and observable behaviors. Kane [2013] formalized an argument-based approach to validation, emphasizing that validity inheres not in the instrument itself but in the *interpretations and uses* of its scores.

These frameworks have direct implications for LLM evaluation. When a researcher administers the MFQ-30 to an LLM and reports that it "scores high on Care/Harm," the implicit interpretation is that the model possesses something analogous to the Care/Harm moral foundation as defined by MFT. But this interpretation rests on validity evidence established for *human* respondents---evidence that the MFQ items tap into the intended psychological constructs when answered by people who experience moral intuitions, have developmental histories, and occupy cultural niches. An LLM generates text through autoregressive token prediction from training data; whether MFQ scores measure the same construct in this radically different "respondent" is an open empirical question that requires *revalidation*, not assumption.

The construct validity challenge becomes especially acute when cross-vendor comparison is at stake. If the same instrument is administered to models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, and all three produce similar scores, the convergence might reflect (a) that all models possess the same "value" the instrument measures, (b) that the instrument fails to discriminate between fundamentally different response-generation processes, or (c) that alignment training has imposed a common output distribution regardless of underlying architecture. These interpretations have very different implications, and standard validity theory---developed for populations that share a common cognitive architecture---provides no guidance for distinguishing them. This ambiguity motivates both our Claim 2 (construct validity gaps) and our empirical investigation of whether convergence is an artifact of measurement or a genuine property of the models.

Three forms of validity are particularly relevant. *Content validity* asks whether the benchmark adequately samples the theoretical domain---our A2 (operationalization quality) feature captures this. *Criterion validity* asks whether benchmark scores predict meaningful downstream behavior---our A5 (human baselines) feature enables criterion comparison. *Construct validity* asks whether the benchmark measures the intended psychological construct---our A6 feature directly assesses the evidence for this, and it is here that the landscape is weakest.

### 2.3.2 Empirical Evidence of Validity Threats

A growing body of evidence documents specific validity threats when administering human psychological instruments to LLMs:

Binz and Schulz [2023] applied classic cognitive psychology experimental paradigms to GPT-3, finding that the model shows human-like biases in some domains (e.g., anchoring effects) but systematic deviations in others (e.g., base rate neglect), raising fundamental questions about whether these instruments measure the same constructs across human and machine respondents. Santurkar et al. [2023] demonstrated at ICML that LLM opinion survey results systematically reflect particular demographic opinions over others, undermining claims that model responses represent neutral measurement---the opinions measured may be artifacts of training data composition rather than properties of the model as a cognitive agent.

Within the specific domain of personality measurement, Gupta et al. [2025] challenged the validity of standard personality tests for LLMs, demonstrating systematic failures in reliability and construct validity. Huang et al. [2025] responded by developing a psychometric framework specifically designed for evaluating LLM personality traits in *Nature Machine Intelligence*, acknowledging that standard instruments require fundamental adaptation. Maharjan et al. [2025] evaluated LLM embeddings for personality assessment in JMIR, and Heston and Gillette [2025] found evidence of "personality-like patterns" in LLMs while questioning whether these reflect genuine psychological constructs. Li et al. [2025] showed in ACL Findings that forced-choice item formats produce superior measurement properties compared to Likert scales when administered to LLMs---suggesting that the *format* of the instrument, not just its content, affects construct validity.

Within moral psychology specifically, Abdulhai et al. [2024] showed that MFQ-30 scores for LLMs shift substantially with persona prompting---an observation that would invalidate the instrument's interpretation under standard psychometric criteria, since human MFQ scores are not expected to change based on how the respondent is addressed. The DIT studies [Tanmay et al., 2023; Khandelwal et al., 2024] noted that high post-conventional scores may reflect training data composition rather than a coherent moral developmental trajectory. Hadar-Shoval et al. [2024] found that LLM value profiles systematically diverge from 53,472-person human baselines on the PVQ-RR---but whether this divergence reflects genuine "value differences" or measurement artifacts is precisely the kind of question that construct validity evidence would resolve.

These findings collectively underscore the importance of our A6 feature (construct validity evidence). An instrument validated for human respondents is *not automatically valid for LLMs*. The median A6 score of 3/5 across 55 assessed benchmarks (Claim 2) indicates that the majority of the evaluation landscape operates on untested assumptions about cross-population validity. For our cross-vendor empirical battery, this means that even strong convergence across models must be interpreted cautiously: the models may genuinely share a moral profile, or the instruments may lack the sensitivity to detect differences that exist at a level of analysis the instruments were not designed to reach.

## 2.4 Format Effects, Human Baselines, and Measurement Challenges

### 2.4.1 Format Mismatch in LLM Evaluation

Psychological instruments employ diverse response formats designed for human cognitive capabilities---Likert scales, forced-choice binary decisions, ranking tasks, open-ended behavioral measures. When these instruments are administered to LLMs, format-specific biases emerge that can fundamentally alter what is being measured.

A series of recent studies has documented these effects. Lu et al. [2025] demonstrated systematic discrepancies between binary and continuous response formats when administered to LLMs, showing that the same moral question produces meaningfully different responses depending on whether the model is asked for a yes/no answer or a rating on a scale. Li et al. [2025] found in ACL Findings that forced-choice personality measurement outperforms Likert scales for LLMs, producing more reliable and discriminating scores. Godfrey et al. [2025] specifically examined Likert scale bias in LLM relevance judgments, finding that models use rating scales differently from human respondents---exhibiting compression toward the center of the scale and avoidance of extreme responses.

Argyle et al. [2023] demonstrated that LLMs can simulate diverse human survey responses through demographic "silicon sampling," but that response format significantly affects simulation fidelity. Santurkar et al. [2023] showed that question format interacts with the systematic demographic biases in LLM outputs. Oh and Demberg [2025] found in Royal Society Open Science that format changes can significantly alter moral judgment outputs, sometimes reversing the direction of measured preferences.

Choi et al. [2025b] argued that the mismatch between established psychometric and ecologically valid questionnaires for LLM assessment may fundamentally invalidate cross-format comparisons. Faulborn et al. [2025] demonstrated at ACL how measurement format affects detected political bias patterns, advocating for format-aware assessment designs. Together, these findings establish that format effects in LLM moral evaluation are not measurement noise but *systematic features of the measurement process* that must be addressed in benchmark design.

This literature directly motivates our Claim 5 (Format Mismatch) and our B1 (task format) and B10 (LLM administration fidelity) features, which assess how well each benchmark's format transfers to LLM administration. Format effects also complicate cross-vendor comparison: if Anthropic's Claude responds differently to Likert scales than OpenAI's GPT, apparent value differences may be format artifacts. Our empirical battery controls for this by administering identical prompts and formats to all nine models, isolating vendor and architecture effects from format confounds.

### 2.4.2 The Human Baseline Imperative

Claims about LLM moral cognition are interpretable only relative to human baselines. The statement "GPT-4 scores at the post-conventional level on the DIT" is meaningful because we know what post-conventional reasoning means in human populations and have normative data spanning decades. Without such baselines, moral evaluation reduces to pattern description without interpretive anchor.

Several instruments provide exceptionally strong human baselines. The Moral Machine experiment [Awad et al., 2018] collected 40 million forced-choice moral decisions from participants in 233 countries, providing the most extensive cross-cultural moral preference dataset ever assembled. Awad et al. [2020] extended this with 70,000 structured participants across 42 countries, enabling fine-grained cultural comparison. The MFQ [Graham et al., 2011] has been administered to hundreds of thousands of respondents, with norms available across political orientation, age, and education. The DIT has accumulated 40+ years of normative data [Rest, 1979; Rest et al., 1999; Gungordu et al., 2023], making it one of the most extensively normed instruments in psychology. The PVQ-RR's 49-group, 53,472-person validation [Schwartz and Cieciuch, 2022] provides human baselines across 32 languages. Bago et al.'s [2022] 45-country trolley study established situated baseline data showing how cultural clusters (Eastern, Western, Southern) differentially respond to situational moral factors.

Yet not all benchmarks enjoy this richness. Our analysis finds that 18% of assessed benchmarks provide no human comparison data (Claim 4)---typically NLP-origin benchmarks designed as classification tasks rather than psychological instruments. Even among benchmarks with some human data, the quality varies: paper-specific baselines from small, demographically homogeneous samples (33% of benchmarks) are far less interpretable than population-level published norms (42%).

The WEIRD critique [Henrich et al., 2010] further complicates the baseline picture. Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) populations are psychological outliers on many dimensions including moral reasoning, yet most psychological norms are derived from these populations. Atari et al.'s [2023] MFQ-2 cross-cultural validation showed that the moral domain's structure varies across cultures, with some foundations less relevant in non-Western societies. Cheung et al. [2025] found that LLMs show amplified versions of known human cognitive biases in moral decision-making---but "amplified relative to *which* human baseline?" remains a critical question when human baselines themselves vary substantially across cultures. The WEIRD bias in human baselines becomes especially consequential when evaluating the cross-vendor convergence we document: if all models converge toward a WEIRD-liberal moral profile, distinguishing between "convergence on universal moral values" and "convergence on the moral profile of the WEIRD populations whose text dominates all training corpora" requires precisely the kind of cross-cultural human baselines that the benchmark landscape underrepresents.

## 2.5 Alignment Training and Prompting Effects

### 2.5.1 How Training Shapes Moral Outputs

Contemporary LLMs undergo multi-stage training that significantly shapes their moral outputs, yet this shaping is rarely accounted for in moral psychology benchmarks. Ouyang et al. [2022] introduced InstructGPT and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), demonstrating that human feedback training fundamentally alters model behavior in morally-charged contexts---making models more helpful and less harmful according to the preferences of the human raters who provide feedback. Bai et al. [2022] extended this with Constitutional AI (CAI), replacing human labelers with a set of explicit principles that guide self-improvement, effectively embedding a particular moral constitution into the training process.

These alignment methods raise a question our meta-evaluation takes seriously: **when a benchmark measures an LLM's "moral values," is it measuring the model's learned representation of human moral cognition, or the specific moral preferences embedded through RLHF/CAI training?** Gonzalez Barman et al. [2025] argued in Philosophy & Technology that alignment processes privilege particular cultural and moral perspectives---"whose culture, whose values?"---and that the selection of human raters introduces systematic bias into what counts as "aligned" behavior. Lindstrom et al. [2025] analyzed in Ethics and Information Technology the sociotechnical limitations of the helpful/harmless/honest framework, arguing that it embeds implicit moral assumptions that constrain how models handle ethical dilemmas.

The theoretical mechanism underlying alignment training has direct implications for what cross-vendor comparison should reveal. If RLHF and its variants (DPO, Constitutional AI, RLHF-from-AI-feedback) encode a common moral preference set---harm aversion, fairness, helpfulness---and if different vendors employ raters drawn from similar demographic pools and optimization objectives converging on similar loss surfaces, then cross-vendor convergence in moral benchmarks is not merely predicted but *inevitable*. The models are being trained toward overlapping targets. However, the degree of convergence, the specific dimensions on which it is strongest, and whether it extends to all moral constructs or is specific to the dimensions alignment training explicitly targets are empirical questions that existing literature has not addressed.

Understanding *why* convergence might occur requires distinguishing several mechanisms:

1. **Training data overlap.** All frontier models train on large web corpora that share substantial content. Moral preferences encoded in this shared substrate would produce convergent outputs regardless of alignment training.

2. **RLHF target similarity.** Alignment raters across vendors may share demographic characteristics (English-speaking, college-educated, Western) that produce a common moral preference signal, independent of the specific rating protocol.

3. **Optimization convergence.** Even with different rater pools and protocols, the mathematical objective of RLHF---maximizing expected reward under a learned preference model---may drive models toward similar regions of the output space, particularly for high-agreement moral judgments where most humans agree.

4. **Constitutional convergence.** Explicit safety constitutions (Anthropic's, OpenAI's, Google's) may share sufficient common ground that their encoded values overlap substantially despite different philosophical framings.

Distinguishing among these mechanisms requires the kind of controlled cross-vendor comparison our empirical battery provides. If convergence is driven primarily by training data overlap (mechanism 1), it should be uniform across all benchmarks. If driven by RLHF (mechanisms 2--4), it should be strongest on dimensions that alignment training explicitly targets (harm, fairness, helpfulness) and weaker on dimensions it does not (e.g., Sanctity, Authority, moral identity). Our results (Section 5) allow partial discrimination among these hypotheses.

### 2.5.2 Prompting Malleability

Beyond training-time effects, LLM moral outputs are sensitive to prompt-time manipulation in ways that challenge measurement validity. Abdulhai et al. [2024] demonstrated that MFQ-30 scores shift substantially with persona prompting---telling a model to respond "as a conservative" versus "as a liberal" produces dramatically different moral foundation profiles, mirroring the known human liberal-conservative difference. Santurkar et al. [2023] showed that LM outputs systematically reflect certain demographic opinions, with prompt engineering able to steer responses toward specific population subgroups. Argyle et al. [2023] formalized this as "silicon sampling," showing that LLMs can simulate survey responses from specific demographic profiles.

The implications for measurement are severe. If a benchmark produces scores that change substantially depending on whether the system prompt says "You are a helpful assistant" versus "You are a moral philosopher," then what is being measured? The model's "default" moral profile is itself an artifact of alignment training and prompt templates. Oh and Demberg [2025] found in Royal Society Open Science that small prompt variations significantly alter moral judgment outputs, and van Nuenen and Sachdeva [2026] confirmed that models lack robust moral reasoning under surface-level perturbations.

Findeis et al. [2025] proposed Inverse Constitutional AI methods to decode the implicit human preferences embedded during alignment, potentially revealing the hidden value assumptions that shape all downstream moral evaluation. Chacon Menke et al. [2025] found variable results on Constitutional AI effectiveness in open-source models---some models show consistent principle-following while others exhibit "collapse"---further complicating the measurement picture. Together, these findings establish that **any moral psychology benchmark for LLMs must account for the confounding effects of alignment training and prompt sensitivity**, a requirement that few existing benchmarks satisfy.

Prompting malleability has a paradoxical relationship to cross-vendor convergence. On one hand, the fact that prompts can shift moral outputs suggests that models have no stable moral profile, making convergence across vendors potentially meaningless (since any model can be steered to any profile). On the other hand, the *default* profile---what models produce under standard prompting conditions without persona manipulation---reflects the integrated product of pretraining data, alignment training, and system prompt conventions, and it is this default profile that users encounter in deployment. Our cross-vendor battery tests models under standardized default conditions precisely to measure this deployment-relevant profile, while acknowledging that the measured convergence is conditional on prompting regime.

## 2.6 Cross-Model and Cross-Vendor Moral Evaluation

### 2.6.1 Early Cross-Model Comparisons

A small but growing literature has begun to compare moral outputs across multiple LLMs, though systematic cross-vendor evaluation remains rare. The earliest comparisons emerged as incidental findings within single-benchmark studies. Tanmay et al. [2023] compared GPT-3 and GPT-4 on the DIT, finding a dramatic generational difference (random-level versus graduate-level performance) that was interpreted as evidence of emergent moral reasoning---though within a single vendor family. Khandelwal et al. [2024] extended the comparison to Claude 3.5 Sonnet alongside GPT models and found that Claude achieved the highest P-score, but the study's primary concern was elicitation methodology rather than systematic cross-vendor analysis.

Hadar-Shoval et al. [2024] provide one of the first genuinely cross-vendor value comparisons, administering the PVQ-RR to four LLMs spanning two vendors (Google's Bard, Anthropic's Claude 2, and OpenAI's GPT-3.5 and GPT-4). Their finding that all four models prioritize Universalism and Self-Direction while de-emphasizing Power and Achievement---despite coming from different vendors with different alignment approaches---constitutes early evidence of the cross-vendor convergence our study systematically investigates. However, Hadar-Shoval et al. did not frame their findings in convergence terms, instead treating each model's value profile as an independent object of study. The convergence pattern is visible only retrospectively, in light of subsequent results.

Takemoto [2024] applied the Moral Machine paradigm to multiple LLMs and discovered a pattern that anticipates our RLHF fingerprint finding: ChatGPT amplifies universally shared moral preferences (spare the many, spare the young) while attenuating culturally variable preferences (spare higher-status individuals, spare the lawful). This selective amplification/attenuation pattern---strengthening consensus values, weakening controversial ones---is precisely what an RLHF-trained model would be expected to produce, since human raters agree on consensus values (providing a clear reward signal) but disagree on controversial ones (providing a noisy signal that RLHF training averages out). Takemoto's study examined a single vendor, but the mechanism it identifies---RLHF as consensus amplifier---predicts the cross-vendor convergence we observe.

### 2.6.2 Cross-Vendor Value Alignment Studies

More recent work has begun to make cross-vendor comparison explicit. Segerer [2025] conducted a prompt-based analysis of Schwartz values across Gemini, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek---spanning three vendors with distinct organizational contexts (American corporate, American corporate, and Chinese corporate)---and found both convergence and meaningful divergence. All three models prioritized self-transcendence values (Universalism, Benevolence), consistent with the cross-vendor pattern identified by Hadar-Shoval et al. [2024]. However, DeepSeek uniquely downplayed self-enhancement values (Power, Achievement), a deviation that Segerer attributed to collectivist tendencies in Chinese training data. This finding is significant because it suggests that while RLHF may impose a common moral profile on most dimensions, training data cultural composition can introduce vendor-specific signatures on specific value dimensions---a nuance our nine-model battery is positioned to investigate at higher resolution.

Rozen et al. [2025] examined whether the circular structure of Schwartz's value model is preserved in LLM responses, finding that standard prompting fails to reproduce the theoretically predicted correlation structure. Their "Value Anchoring" strategy improved alignment with the circular model, but the fact that the intervention was necessary suggests that LLMs do not naturally organize values in the way human value theory predicts. If this disorganization is shared across vendors---as their results suggest---it points to a common failure mode rather than common moral values: all models fail to encode value *structure* even as they converge on value *content*.

Cheung et al. [2025] found low inter-model agreement in moral judgment tasks, with models exhibiting different patterns of yes-no bias and omission bias. At first glance, this finding appears to contradict the convergence pattern. However, the disagreement Cheung et al. documented was at the level of individual items and specific biases, while the convergence we and others observe operates at the level of aggregate profiles and dimensional scores. This distinction is important: models may agree on which values to endorse (high aggregate convergence) while disagreeing on specific judgment calls (low item-level agreement), in the same way that two humans with identical MFT profiles might disagree on specific moral dilemmas.

### 2.6.3 The Convergence Puzzle

Taken together, the emerging cross-vendor literature reveals a striking pattern that no single study has fully articulated: **frontier LLMs from independent vendors, trained on different data with different alignment methods, converge toward a common moral profile characterized by high harm aversion, strong fairness endorsement, elevated Universalism and Self-Direction, post-conventional moral reasoning, and attenuation of culturally controversial moral preferences.** This convergence is not universal---Segerer's DeepSeek finding and Cheung et al.'s item-level disagreement demonstrate dimensions and granularities where divergence persists---but the broad pattern is robust across the studies reviewed.

The convergence puzzle is this: the finding is consistent with multiple incompatible interpretations, and the existing literature does not provide the data needed to distinguish among them. The convergence might reflect:

(a) **Universal moral structure.** If certain moral preferences are genuinely universal---harm aversion, fairness, cooperation---then any sufficiently capable system trained on human-generated text would converge on them, regardless of vendor or alignment method. On this interpretation, convergence is evidence *for* the universal moral grammar that Mikhail [2007] and Hauser [2006] proposed.

(b) **RLHF monoculture.** If all vendors employ similar alignment techniques with similar rater pools and similar optimization objectives, convergence reflects shared training methodology rather than shared moral truth. On this interpretation, convergence is an artifact of how models are built, not evidence about moral cognition.

(c) **Training data homogeneity.** If all models train on substantially overlapping web corpora dominated by English-language, Western, liberal-leaning text, convergence reflects the moral profile of the training data rather than either universal values or alignment methodology.

(d) **Instrument insensitivity.** If the benchmarks being used lack the psychometric sensitivity to detect genuine differences in moral cognition across models, convergence is a measurement artifact.

Our study is designed to make partial progress on this discrimination problem. By administering 23 benchmarks to 9 models from 3 independent vendors under controlled conditions, we can test whether convergence is uniform (suggesting mechanisms a or c) or dimension-specific (suggesting mechanism b); whether it varies with model scale (suggesting emergent properties rather than training artifacts); and whether instruments with strong human baselines show different convergence patterns than those without (addressing mechanism d). The finding of six specific RLHF universal fingerprints (Section 5.2) provides the strongest evidence for mechanism (b), but the question remains open and likely involves contributions from multiple mechanisms simultaneously.

## 2.7 Emerging Benchmarks and the Current Trajectory

### 2.7.1 Recent Multi-Framework Benchmarks

The past two years have seen a shift toward more theoretically ambitious moral evaluation benchmarks that attempt to address some of the gaps we identify. Chiu et al. [2025] introduced MoReBench, a comprehensive moral reasoning benchmark spanning multiple ethical frameworks and cultural contexts with fine-grained evaluation metrics---the first benchmark to explicitly target multi-framework evaluation. Wu et al. [2025] proposed the Staircase of Ethics at EMNLP, organizing evaluation as a multi-level progression from basic harm recognition to complex moral dilemma resolution, partially engaging dual-process constructs through escalating cognitive demands. Kumar et al. [2025] presented UniMoral at ACL, aiming for universal coverage across diverse moral traditions.

Huang et al. [2026] introduced ValAct-15k, a 15,000-scenario benchmark measuring the gap between LLMs knowing moral principles and acting on them---a paradigm shift from measuring moral *knowledge* to measuring moral *action*. This aligns with the three-dimensional framework proposed by Jiao et al. [2025] in *Scientific Reports*, which distinguishes moral recognition, moral judgment, and moral action as distinct evaluative dimensions. Ji et al.'s [2024] MoralBench at KDD provided fine-grained evaluation of moral reasoning quality across diverse scenarios using MFV stimuli.

Most significantly, Gabriel et al. [2026] published a comprehensive roadmap for evaluating moral competence in LLMs in *Nature*, arguing that current benchmarks are fundamentally insufficient and outlining key dimensions for next-generation evaluation: moral perception, moral judgment, moral reasoning, moral motivation, and moral action. This roadmap provides an authoritative endorsement of the argument that motivates our meta-evaluation---that the field needs systematic assessment of what its benchmarks actually measure. Our empirical results add an urgent dimension to Gabriel et al.'s call: if all frontier models converge on the same moral profile, then the "moral competence" being evaluated is not a property of individual models but a shared artifact of how the field builds and aligns LLMs. Next-generation benchmarks must be designed not only to cover the theoretical dimensions Gabriel et al. identify but to *discriminate* between models---a requirement that the current landscape conspicuously fails to meet.

### 2.7.2 Paradigm Shifts in Progress

Several emerging trends suggest the field is beginning to address the gaps we document:

**From verdicts to reasoning quality.** Snoswell et al. [2025] argued that evaluating LLM moral competence requires going "beyond verdicts" to assess reasoning quality, consistency, and sensitivity to morally relevant features. Chakraborty et al. [2025] at EMNLP specifically evaluated structured moral reasoning, analyzing how models decompose ethical problems. This shift addresses our construct validity concern (Claim 2) by moving beyond accuracy metrics to process-level assessment. For cross-vendor evaluation, reasoning-quality metrics may prove more discriminating than verdict-level metrics, since models that produce identical verdicts may arrive at them through detectably different reasoning chains.

**From single-framework to pluralistic.** Farid et al. [2025] tested whether LLMs can represent multiple moral frameworks simultaneously, finding that models tend to collapse moral pluralism into dominant Western ethical perspectives. Sorensen et al. [2024] developed ValuePrism, a pluralistic framework capturing 31,000+ values from diverse sources. Scherrer et al. [2024] at NeurIPS developed methods to probe the specific moral beliefs encoded within LLMs. These approaches begin to address MFT dominance (Claim 1) by broadening the evaluative lens. Farid et al.'s finding of moral pluralism collapse across models is itself a convergence finding consistent with our RLHF monoculture thesis.

**From English-only to multilingual.** Khandelwal et al.'s [2024] multilingual DIT study at EACL and the cross-cultural value alignment analyses [Segerer, 2025; Rozen et al., 2025] are beginning to address the WEIRD bias in moral evaluation, though coverage remains thin. The multilingual dimension introduces a testable prediction for cross-vendor comparison: if convergence is driven by English-language RLHF training, it should be weaker in non-English evaluation---a hypothesis that has not been systematically tested.

**From static to robustness-tested.** Van Nuenen and Sachdeva's [2026] fragility of moral judgment study and Oh and Demberg's [2025] Royal Society Open Science robustness analysis establish a new paradigm of testing moral outputs under perturbation, addressing the prompting malleability concerns documented in Section 2.5.

**What remains missing.** Despite these advances, the three gaps we identify persist: no dedicated benchmark tests Dual Process Theory's signature predictions, Social Intuitionism's moral dumbfounding phenomenon, or Dyadic Morality's agent-patient template. Process-oriented benchmarks, psychometric revalidation protocols for LLM respondents, and benchmarks with comprehensive cross-cultural human baselines remain frontier challenges. Furthermore, none of the emerging benchmarks reviewed above were designed with cross-vendor discrimination as a primary goal---an oversight that our empirical results suggest should be a central concern for next-generation benchmark design.

## 2.8 Positioning This Work

Our work occupies a distinct position in this landscape, bridging meta-evaluation and empirical testing in a way that no prior study has attempted. Unlike **BIG-bench** [Srivastava et al., 2023] and **HELM** [Liang et al., 2023], which evaluate LLMs across broad capability dimensions with minimal attention to moral cognition, we focus exclusively on moral psychology benchmarks with deep theoretical grounding. Unlike the **ETHICS benchmark** [Hendrycks et al., 2021], which organizes moral evaluation around normative ethical theories (deontology, utilitarianism, justice, virtue), we ground our analysis in *moral psychology*---the empirical science of how moral judgments actually form, rather than the philosophical question of how they *should* form. Unlike the **Gabriel et al. [2026] roadmap**, which is prescriptive (proposing what future benchmarks should measure), our work is both diagnostic and empirical---documenting what existing benchmarks actually measure and then *testing them* across vendors to determine whether they discriminate. Unlike **Jiang et al. [2024]** and **Scherrer et al. [2024]**, who surveyed moral reasoning datasets and alignment evaluation methods respectively, we systematically evaluate benchmarks against theoretical constructs using a 28-feature psychometric characterization framework and validate our assessment through the largest cross-vendor moral psychology battery administered to date.

Unlike the emerging cross-model comparison studies [Takemoto, 2024; Hadar-Shoval et al., 2024; Segerer, 2025], which compare a handful of models on a single instrument, we administer 23 benchmarks spanning multiple moral psychology theories to 9 models from 3 independent vendors under controlled conditions. This breadth enables two analyses that single-benchmark studies cannot support: first, we can assess whether convergence is theory-specific or universal, and second, we can identify the specific *dimensions* on which RLHF training leaves its strongest imprint.

What we contribute is fourfold: (1) a systematic psychometric assessment of benchmark quality grounded in validity theory, producing the A6 construct validity scores that reveal the field's median weakness; (2) a theory coverage analysis that quantifies the dominance of MFT and the absence of process-oriented theories; (3) the most comprehensive cross-vendor moral psychology battery to date, revealing six universal RLHF fingerprints that persist across all tested models and vendors; and (4) the argument that these two findings are causally linked---the theoretical imbalance of the benchmark landscape and the empirical convergence of frontier models are both consequences of the same underlying forces, and addressing either requires understanding both. Together, these contributions provide the diagnostic and empirical foundation on which Gabriel et al.'s prescriptive roadmap can be enacted---not merely by building better benchmarks, but by understanding why the current ones all point in the same direction.
