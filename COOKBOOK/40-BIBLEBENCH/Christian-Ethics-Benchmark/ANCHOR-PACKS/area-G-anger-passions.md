# Area G: Anger, envy & the passions — Anchor Pack

## 1. Area definition & scope

This area covers the moral evaluation of anger and the "anger family" of emotions, together with the wider question of how the passions/affections are formed and governed. In scope: righteous vs. sinful anger; the six everyday distortions of anger (irritability, arguing, bitterness, violence, passive anger, self-righteous anger); bitterness, resentment, grudge-bearing, and long-carried hurt; complaining/grumbling; envy, jealousy, contempt, and covetousness of the heart; self-pity and anger at oneself; vengeance as an emotional stance; anger *at God*; and the general question of whether emotions are morally neutral raw data or are themselves subject to moral command and formation. The primary source is David Powlison's *Good and Angry* (2016), a whole-book treatment of anger from a redemptive-counseling frame; it is supplemented by the *New Dictionary of Christian Ethics & Pastoral Theology* (NDCEPT), Frame, Grudem, the Blackwell and Cambridge Companions, Jones, and Meilaender. Cross-cutting axis: whether the remedy for disordered anger is suppression, elimination (apatheia), or transformation into rightly-ordered affection.

A second, method-level axis runs through the whole area and is worth flagging for item writers: the *diagnostic* question of where anger comes from. Powlison's book is organized around the claim that the decisive question is not "what happened to make me angry?" (circumstances) but "why do I get angry?" — a question that "doesn't look at your circumstances; it looks at you" and surfaces the frustrated *desires* underneath the outburst (Jas. 4:1–2). This puts the interior life of desire, worship, and self-exaltation at the center of the moral analysis, and connects anger to the classic vices of pride and idolatry. It also frames *change* as slow, lifelong sanctification rather than technique, locates much of anger's damage in the tongue and in words, and holds up God's own patience ("slow to anger," Exod. 34:6) and the Spirit-produced virtue of self-control (Gal. 5:22–23) as the human template. Items in this area can therefore test not only the *classification* of an anger event (righteous / sinful / mixed) but the *diagnosis* of its inner motive and the *remedy* proposed for it.

## 2. Consensus claims

**C1.** Anger is fundamentally a moral response to perceived wrong — its essential DNA is "displeasure at wrong," a stance that says "That's wrong; I'm against that." [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"; NDCEPT, "Anger" (anger as response to "a real or perceived wrongdoing")]

**C2.** Anger done rightly is a genuine good: it protects the helpless, opposes evil, and energizes constructive action — the model is God's own anger and Jesus's anger in the service of mercy. [Powlison, ch. 9 "Good and Angry?"; Frame, near "the slowly aroused, easily extinguished, righteous anger of God's servants"; NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins" (anger "is not an essentially perverse type of emotion")]

**C3.** Most human anger, however, comes out "grubby and destructive" — returning evil for evil through complaining, coldness, tantrums, getting even, and self-pity — so anger is far more often corrupted than clean. [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"; Grudem, near "the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (Jas. 1:19–20)]

**C4.** Anger is never morally neutral in the concrete: every actual anger event comes "loaded either right or wrong (or mixed and mixed up)," so "anger just is" is a false evasion of its moral character. [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God"; Frame, ch. 21 "THE EMOTIONS" (emotions are fallen and redeemed, not a neutral faculty)]

**C5.** Sinful anger is not merely an outward act of violence but a condition of the heart that Scripture forbids under the sixth commandment and in the "works of the flesh." [Frame, ch. 35 "The Sixth Commandment: Respecting Life" (Larger Catechism forbids "sinful anger, hatred, envy, desire of revenge"); Grudem, ch. 5 "Obedience to God and the Consequences of Sin" (God prohibits sinful attitudes such as anger and jealousy, Matt. 5:22; Gal. 5:20)]

**C6.** The redemption of anger is not its elimination but its transformation into what Powlison calls "the constructive displeasure of mercy" — anger laced with mercy, aimed at making wrongs right. [Powlison, ch. 7 "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 1: Patience and Forgiveness"; Blackwell, ch. 34 "Being Blessed" (Eph. 4:26: "anger that leads to forgiveness rather than bitterness")]

**C7.** The four constructive components of good anger are patience/forbearance, forgiveness, charity, and constructive conflict. [Powlison, ch. 7 and ch. 8 "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 2: Charity and Constructive Conflict"; Blackwell, ch. 22 "Sharing Peace" (gentleness "subdues anger, lessens bitterness, restrains one's desire for revenge," and patience bears with others' faults, citing Aquinas)]

**C8.** Bitterness is anger that lasts — the recycling of old hurts and the nursing of grudges — and it is a distinct and serious distortion of anger. [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'" (bitterness as anger that "last[s] a long, long time"); NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins" (anger becomes sin when "hardened into grudge-bearing")]

**C9.** Vengeance as a personal emotional stance is forbidden; the impulse to "get even" is the paradigm of returning evil for evil (Rom. 12:17–21). [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"; NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins" (sin when anger is "permitted to issue in significant vengeful behaviour")]

**C10.** Complaining and grumbling — the "everyday angers" of irritation and a sour, negative spirit — are real sins, not trivial ones, and are a strategic starting point for change; the alternative is learned contentment. [Powlison, ch. 15 "The Everyday Angers" (and subsection "The Everyday Problem of Complaining"); Grudem, ch. 42 "Purity of Heart" (contentment as the opposite duty, Phil. 4:11)]

**C11.** Emotions/passions are not an inferior or morally inert faculty; they are morally significant, fallen, redeemable, and even directly commanded by God (e.g., "Rejoice in the Lord always"). [Frame, ch. 21 "THE EMOTIONS"; Jones, ch. 3 "The Ministry of the Holy Spirit" (feeling and moral action are inseparable; vindictive action "flows from and implies mean and vindictive feeling")]

**C12.** The passions are morally formable through virtue: the irascible passions (anger, daring, fear) are shaped by fortitude and the desiring passions by temperance, so that character determines how one feels. [Cambridge, ch. 7 "Virtue ethics" (Aquinas: virtues "shape the passions and the will" as "moral qualities"); Meilaender, ch. 4 "Virtue and Vocation, Character and Calling" (character "determines what we can see")]

**C13.** Envy is an essentially perverse emotion: displeasure at another's excellence or good, rooted in a competitive logic of self-worth; its answering virtue is humility/charity. [NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins" (envy as "displeased at another person's excellence"); Cambridge, ch. 7 "Virtue ethics" ("charity is said to be the remedy for envy")]

**C14.** Envy differs structurally from jealousy: envy is dyadic (wanting what another has) and is always a vice, whereas jealousy is triadic (a possessive attachment threatened by a rival) and can be righteous — which is why God is called jealous but never envious. [NDCEPT, "Jealousy and Envy"; Meilaender, ch. 11 "Love and the Loves" (jealousy arises when love becomes possessive/ownership; affection's "jealousy is proportionately fierce")]

**C15.** Coveting is a sin of the heart and the climax of the Decalogue: the tenth commandment targets interior desire itself, showing that all sin begins in the heart and that God demands purity of heart, not merely right actions. [Frame, ch. 44 "The Tenth Commandment: The Desires of the Heart" (subsection "COVETING AND OTHER DESIRES"); Grudem, ch. 42 "Purity of Heart" (subsection "The Broader Implication of This Command: God Is Concerned with Purity of Heart"); NDCEPT, "Covetousness"]

**C16.** Envy is a symptom of the unregenerate heart, listed with malice and murder, so moral change must come "from within" and cannot be achieved by suppressing outward expression alone. [Jones, ch. 3 "The Human Heart" (Mark 7:21–22); Grudem, ch. 4 "The Goal of Ethics" ("Vices Are the Opposite of Christlike Virtues")]

**C17.** Anger at oneself (chronic self-reproach, self-condemnation, "low self-esteem") is a genuine form of the anger problem: conscience is a good gift that can become a merciless slavemaster and needs redeeming. [Powlison, ch. 16 "Do You Ever Get Angry at Yourself?"]

**C18.** Deep wounds should not be expected to simply "wash away"; a grave wrong rightly marks a person for life, and the goal is not forgetting but transmuting pain into something fruitful rather than a "running sore" of rage and bitterness. [Powlison, ch. 14 "'I'll Never Get Over It'"]

**C19.** Anger comes ultimately from the heart's *desires*, not from circumstances: conflict and rage erupt because "you desire but do not have" and a good want has hardened into a demand ("I must have it"), so the diagnostic question "why do you get angry?" looks not at the situation but at the person. This is Powlison's central thesis and the exegetical engine of the book (James 4:1–2). [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace" (near "your desires that battle within you"; "What exactly do you want right now that makes you warlike"); Powlison, ch. 13 "Eight Questions: Taking Your Anger Apart to Put You Back Together" (near "Why do you get angry? This is the million-dollar question . . . it doesn't look at your circumstances. It looks at you")]

**C20.** God's own patience — his being "slow to anger" (Exodus 34:6) — is the explicit model for human anger; "to become slow to anger is to become like God," and God is characteristically slow to anger and quick to show mercy without being indifferent to evil. [Powlison, ch. 7 "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 1" (near "God is love, and God is slow to anger. He intends to make us like himself"); Powlison, ch. 10 "The Best Anger, the Best Love" (subsection "God Is Slow to Anger and Quick to Show Mercy")]

**C21.** Bad anger is characteristically *self-righteous* and proud — it feels intensely "righteous" precisely because it says "I'm so right, because you are so wrong," exalting the angry person to the status of a god whose will is law ("My kingdom come, my will be done"). Its answering posture is the humility that admits "I need help," which is why the change process begins with the poor in spirit. [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger" (near "bad anger is so self-righteous"; the "I AM GOD" analysis); Powlison, ch. 2 "Do You Have a Serious Problem with Anger?" (near "The poor in spirit are blessed"); Powlison, ch. 10 "The Best Anger, the Best Love" (anger "so often fueled by self-righteousness")]

**C22.** Changing anger is a slow, lifelong process of sanctification, not a technique or quick fix: it requires "lifelong rehab," must be "cultivated over a lifetime," and proceeds by grace and grit through repeated failure — just as, left unaddressed, "bad anger doesn't just go away . . . it festers over a lifetime." [Powlison, ch. 11 "A Mirror on Your Anger" (near "enter lifelong rehab"; "You and I are in process"); Powlison, ch. 1 "Angry People" (near "It festers over a lifetime . . . it must be cultivated over a lifetime"); Grudem, near "Becoming Like Christ Is a Lifelong Process"]

**C23.** The tongue and one's *words* are where anger characteristically does its damage: sinful anger "speaks rotten words that tear down rather than build up" (Eph. 4:29), and Scripture treats the tongue as a recalcitrant, weaponizing instrument that must be governed. [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace" (near "speaks rotten words that tear down rather than build up"); NDCEPT, "Speech and the Tongue" (near "Words are weapons"); Frame, near "the coarse language condemned in Ephesians 5:4"; Grudem, near "But no human being can tame the tongue"]

**C24.** Self-control (and its cognate gentleness/meekness) is a *fruit of the Spirit* — a virtue produced by God working in the regenerate heart, not mere willpower — and is the governing answer to disordered anger and the passions generally. [Grudem, near "the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control" (Gal. 5:22–23); Frame, near "loving, faithful, self-controlled" (virtues are Spirit-produced motives of the regenerate heart); Frame, near "How better to demonstrate God's love, his long-suffering, his gentleness, his self-control"]

**C25.** Anger is a whole-person event, not merely a feeling or a body-state: it is "a single complex system" that engages body, emotions, thoughts, actions, *and* underlying motives at once ("something you do with all your heart, soul, mind, and body"), which is why it cannot be reduced to physiology or excused as "just a feeling." [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger" (near "Anger is a single complex system")]

**C26.** Much sinful anger is "half-right": it begins as a genuine response to a real wrong and then goes "all wrong" through overreach, self-will, and cosmic-scope grievance — so the moral task is to separate the just core (the real wrong opposed) from the corrupt overflow (pride, vengeance, self-pity). [Powlison, ch. 3–4 (near "Her anger is half-right—many of the things that happened to her are wrong. And her anger has gone all wrong")]

**C27.** God's anger, unlike ours, is perfectly fair, measured, and consistent with his love — he is "fiercely fair-minded," never petty, capricious, or ill-tempered — so the popular caricature of the biblical God as "ill-tempered, exacting, and capricious" is a false portrait against which redeemed human anger is measured. [Powlison, ch. 9 "Good and Angry?" (near "He is fiercely fair-minded"; near "ill-tempered, exacting, and capricious")]

**C28.** Anger is a universal human problem, not the special failing of a few "angry people," and its forms range from mild and veiled to buried and intense; because roughly "half" of Paul's typical vice-lists "belong to the anger family," honest self-examination — not comparison with worse offenders — is the right starting point. [Powlison, ch. 2 "Do You Have a Serious Problem with Anger?" (answer: "Yes."); Powlison, Introduction (near "half his list belongs to the anger family"; anger as "mild . . . veiled . . . buried . . . intense")]

## 3. Named tradition splits

**Split A — Is anger *at God* ever legitimate? (the headline split).**
This is the sharpest disagreement in the area, and Powlison stages it explicitly in his chapter "Anger at God."
- **Anger at God is always sin (Powlison's position):** In ch. 17 "Anger at God," Powlison flatly concludes, "Anger at God is wrong. . . . It overflows with mistrust toward God." Even when the unmet desire is *good* (a dying father, a longed-for marriage), the anger is "a more subtle wrong" because a good gift has "replaced God, the truly good Giver." He argues the so-called "vent your anger" psalms (e.g., Psalm 44) are not hostility but "the constructive displeasure of faith" — "a cry of faith, not a roar of rage." [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God," including subsections "When Advice Misses the Core Issue of Anger with God" and "What Is the Alternative?"]
- **The therapeutic/self-help view Powlison names and rejects:** he catalogues four popular "doctrines" as the opposing side — (1) anger "just is," neither good nor bad; (2) God lets us down and disappoints us; (3) you can and should *vent* your anger at God, since the psalmists did; (4) you need to *forgive God*. Powlison presents these as "Plausible? . . . Coherent? . . . True? No." So the split is real and he supplies both sides, siding decisively against legitimacy. [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God" (the four numbered "doctrines")]
- **Powlison's constructive alternative (further nuance):** he does not counsel suppression but redirection — the lament psalms model an *honest* crying-out that is nonetheless "a cry of faith, not a roar of rage," and even severe cases (Armen's lifelong rage after the Armenian genocide) are met with compassion rather than condemnation, then redirected toward trust. The story of Nebuchadnezzar, humbled and then restored "in his humility," models enmity against God broken by mercy. So the disagreement is not "express vs. suppress" but *what* the honest expression is — mistrust to be repented, or faith crying out. [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God" (subsection "What Is the Alternative?"; near "consider the life story of Nebuchadnezzar")]

**Split B — Is anger a morally *neutral capacity*, or is it never neutral?**
- **Neutral capacity (effects-based):** The Dictionary's "Anger" entry states the capacity for anger "is a basic human endowment which is morally neither positive nor negative in and of itself," its ethical value lying "in the constructive or destructive impact of its effects," and describes the Bible as treating anger "as a neutral emotion." [NDCEPT, "Anger"]
- **Never neutral (always morally loaded):** Powlison directly denies this: "anger is never neutral. It never 'just is.'. . . every anger event comes loaded either right or wrong." Frame agrees that emotions are not a neutral faculty but are fallen, redeemable, and commanded. [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God"; Frame, ch. 21 "THE EMOTIONS"]
Note: the disagreement is partly one of *level* — NDCEPT judges the bare *capacity* neutral while Powlison judges every *actual event* loaded — but as stated they conflict on whether "anger just is." Powlison's James-4 desire-analysis supplies the *reason* no event is neutral: every anger obeys some desire and so serves some lord, right or wrong, which is why "poke your way into every example of bad anger, and you'll find god-playing." Frame grounds the same "never neutral" verdict in a different register — emotions are a fallen-and-redeemed faculty, not an inert one, so they carry moral weight intrinsically. [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace"; Frame, ch. 21 "THE EMOTIONS"]

**Split C — Righteous anger for ordinary people, or effectively reserved to God?**
- **Genuinely available to humans:** Frame affirms real human righteous anger — "the slowly aroused, easily extinguished, righteous anger of God's servants defending his honor" — and the Dictionary says anger "is not an essentially perverse type of emotion" and "may very well be true and just." [Frame, near "the slowly aroused, easily extinguished, righteous anger"; NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins"]
- **Available in principle but rare and best seen in God:** Powlison affirms humans are made in God's image "with the potential for holy indignation at evil," yet locates the only fully "coherently good" example in the "wrath of God"/Jesus, and stresses that in humans clean anger is rare and usually mixed. [Powlison, ch. 9 "Good and Angry?" and its subsection "The 'Wrath of God'"] This is a difference of emphasis rather than a flat contradiction. Powlison's clearest human example, the fourth-grader Leanne defending a bullied boy, is offered precisely as an exception that proves the rule — "the perfect combination of courage, love, and outrage" — while the paradigm case remains Jesus, who "burned with anger . . . as truly as he melted with pity" (Warfield). Frame's qualifier "slowly aroused, easily extinguished" signals that genuine human righteous anger is real but tightly bounded. [Powlison, ch. 6 "Nature, Nurture . . . and Human Nature"; ch. 10 "The Best Anger, the Best Love"]

**Split D — Stoic *apatheia* vs. rightly-ordered affections.**
The books that engage this side with Christianity: the Cambridge Companion states the Stoic maxim to "live 'according to nature'" was "an injunction to live virtuously rather than at the whim of fluctuating emotions," and contrasts it with the Augustinian alternative in which "the passions" are not eliminated but *ordered* — "to reason and reason to God." [Cambridge, ch. 6 "Natural law and Christian ethics"] Powlison's whole project is on the "ordered-affections" side: anger is a good capacity to be redeemed, not numbed away — he explicitly rejects "deep-breathing and relaxation techniques to help you keep your cool" as the goal. [Powlison, Introduction] He sharpens the contrast christologically: Jesus "was no stoic or Buddhist," but "plunged into the storms of human sufferings and sins" and "felt keenly," bristling with anger and weeping at Lazarus's tomb — the model is intense rightly-aimed feeling, not detachment. [Powlison, ch. 10 "The Best Anger, the Best Love"] Jones treats Stoicism only in an endnote (Augustine's rejection of Stoic *adiaphora*), and does not develop *apatheia* in the main text — so a full Stoic-vs-Christian debate on emotion is present but thin across these particular books.

**Split E — Can the emotions be *directly commanded/willed*, or only *indirectly formed* over time? (a level/emphasis split).**
- **Subject to command (accountability side):** Frame insists emotions are "directly commanded by God" (e.g., "Rejoice in the Lord always"), which makes them morally accountable rather than involuntary weather; Powlison likewise treats anger as something "you do . . . for personal reasons," not a mere reflex, so it can be owned and repented on the spot. [Frame, ch. 21 "THE EMOTIONS"; Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger"]
- **Only indirectly formed (habituation side):** Yet Powlison also denies any in-the-moment fix — "there is no technique or strategy to actually resolve what is going wrong" — and casts change as lifelong reorientation needing "a different timetable," while the Aquinas/virtue tradition holds that the passions are shaped by acquired virtue through habituation, i.e., over time and not by fiat. [Powlison, ch. 2 "Do You Have a Serious Problem with Anger?" (near "there is no technique or strategy"); Cambridge, ch. 7 "Virtue ethics"]
Note: as with Split B, the tension is largely one of *level* — an emotion can be *commanded* (and thus its indulgence blamed) without being instantly *summonable* by will; the disciplines that reform it work slowly. But item writers should note that "just choose to feel differently right now" and "you can't do anything but wait for slow formation" are both one-sided distortions the tradition rejects.

## 4. Casuistry & worked cases

Powlison's *Good and Angry* is built around named counseling vignettes:
- **Willy and Brenda** — a suburban couple whose daily bickering escalated into a literal gunfight (pistol upstairs, rifle downstairs, bullet holes in the sheetrock); counseling was court-mandated. Illustrates anger as interpersonal and destructive. [Powlison, ch. 1 "Angry People", near "Willy was upstairs with the pistol"]
- **Jimmy, age sixteen** — a "cold as ice" teenager who kept "a rap sheet on the whole world," living in a self-centered universe in which everyone existed only to affect his pleasure or displeasure; a case of cold, self-pitying, passive vengeance. [Powlison, near "Jimmy was sixteen"]
- **The supermarket mother and son** — a mother's rage at her child wanting a candy bar reveals cosmic-scope bitterness ("he represents all the bad choices I ever made"); anger "half-right" but gone wrong. [Powlison, ch. 3–4, near "this scene in the supermarket"]
- **Leanne (fourth grade) defending Mark** — a small girl breaks into a ring of bullies tormenting a smaller boy and berates them until they retreat; Powlison's model of clean, good anger — "the perfect combination of courage, love, and outrage." [Powlison, ch. 6, near "But Leanne could also be a heroine"]
- **Armen, the seventy-year-old survivor** — as a child he witnessed the Armenian genocide, including Ottoman soldiers butchering praying women in a church; his lifelong "blind, consuming anger" at God is judged understandable yet still wrong, because it demanded of God a protection God never promised. Central to the anger-at-God chapter. [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God", near "I met a seventy-year-old man named Armen"]
- **Four "good desire" anger-at-God cases:** a child who prays for her cancer-stricken father and rages when he dies; a single woman angry at God for no husband; a homeschooling parent angry at God over a rebellious teen; a man who rages at God after a church leader molested him as a youth. Each shows a good desire made "all-important" and displacing the Giver. [Powlison, ch. 17 "Anger at God", near "a child prays for her father"]
- **Nebuchadnezzar** — the proud king humbled by God and restored "in his humility," a case study in enmity against God broken by mercy. [Powlison, ch. 17, near "consider the life story of Nebuchadnezzar"]
- **The customer-service phone call** — a mundane billing call with menus, hold music, dropped connection, and mutual-accent frustration, spilling into all-day grousing; the paradigm "everyday anger" of irritation and complaining. [Powlison, ch. 15 "The Everyday Angers"]
- **Anger transmuted, not "gotten over":** Candy Lightner (daughter killed by a drunk driver → founded MADD), Martin Luther King Jr. (racial injustice → "I have a dream"), and Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn (Stalin's gulags → *The Gulag Archipelago*) as figures who "never got over it" but were "not disfigured." [Powlison, ch. 14 "'I'll Never Get Over It'"]
- **Trisha and Ryan** — a dating couple whose recurring argument over Ryan showing up late for a dinner date escalates from "current events" to "personal accusation and damnation" ("You always do this. You're impossible."); Powlison uses them to run his anatomy of how a good desire ("I want you to love me") mutates through "I WANT MY WAY" into "I AM GOD." [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger", near "Trisha and Ryan give us an example"]
- **The author and Nan (the "weekly quarrel")** — newlyweds who fought on "four successive Sunday evenings in June": he came home craving "peace and quiet" (the sports page, iced guava juice, Fig Newtons), she craving intimacy after a draining ministry weekend; each "good desire," when it ruled, produced grumbling and critical words. The model case for desire-as-idol. [Powlison, ch. 11 "A Mirror on Your Anger", near "four successive Sunday evenings in June"; ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace"]
- **Enrique and Cheryl Ann** — a husband who over many years learns to love a volatile, self-absorbed, unpredictably accusatory wife with "the constructive displeasure of a powerful mercy" rather than "retaliatory anger, escape into fantasy, discouragement, and self-protection"; Powlison's living portrait of patience. [Powlison, ch. 7 "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 1", near "a friend named Enrique"]
- **The road-rage truck driver** — the author, driving slowly on an unfamiliar road looking for a turn, is met by a truck driver who "leaned hard on his horn," pulled alongside "face contorted with rage," and unleashed an obscene tirade with finger gestures — "a well-practiced habit" of anger. A study in habituated, disproportionate rage. [Powlison, ch. 6 "Nature, Nurture . . . and Human Nature", near "leaned hard on his horn"]
- **The National Geographic magazine** — the author sprawls reading while his wife, amid dinner dishes, toddler debris, and unfolded laundry, asks for help; his passive-aggressive response (silently moving the magazine "two inches closer to my face") is a "small grievance" that, uncorrected, "could have worked inside me to destroy our marriage." A study in small/passive anger and its long fuse. [Powlison, ch. 1 "Angry People", near "the latest issue of National Geographic"]
- **The traffic jam ("Eight Questions")** — stuck in gridlock racing to a make-or-break appointment with a dead cell phone, the driver's anger "rarely stands alone" but "come[s] rolled into one seething ball of miseries" with anxiety, helplessness, guilt, and a craving for comfort (M&Ms, the radio dial); the framing story for Powlison's eight diagnostic questions. [Powlison, ch. 13 "Eight Questions: Taking Your Anger Apart to Put You Back Together"]
- **The parable of the workers ("the eleventh hour")** — Jesus's story of all-day laborers aggrieved that latecomers received equal pay; Powlison uses it to show that God's grace is "spectacularly unfair" and that human anger is "so often fueled by self-righteousness," unable to rejoice in another's undeserved good. [Powlison, ch. 10 "The Best Anger, the Best Love", near "the proverbial 'eleventh hour'"]

From the reference/companion works:
- **Euthanasia/suicide as failures of patience** — reframed not merely as bad choices but as symptoms of character "un-formed in patience." [Blackwell, ch. 28 "Receiving Communion: Euthanasia, Suicide, and Letting Die"]
- **The possessive natural loves** — affection and friendship curdling into jealousy and "the degrading pleasure of exclusiveness," healed only when perfected by agape that tells the beloved "It's good that you exist." [Meilaender, ch. 11 "Love and the Loves" (subsection "The Power and the Danger of the Natural Loves")]
- **Nietzschean *ressentiment*** — envy/"hatred of the successful" as a modern political outworking of what the tenth commandment forbids. [Frame, ch. 44 "The Tenth Commandment: The Desires of the Heart"]

## 5. Key concepts & vocabulary

- **"I'm against that" (anger as displeasure at wrong)** — Powlison's core definition: anger is the moral response to perceived evil/offense. [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"]
- **"The constructive displeasure of mercy"** — Powlison's verbatim name for redeemed, good anger; its four aspects are patience, forgiveness, charity, and constructive conflict. [Powlison, ch. 7, near "Let's give this good anger a name: the constructive displeasure of mercy"]
- **Six forms of bad anger** — irritability, arguing, bitterness, violence, passive anger, self-righteous anger. [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"]
- **"Ira furor brevis est" ("Anger is a brief madness")** — the Roman tag Powlison uses for anger's irrationality. [Powlison, ch. 3, near "The ancient Romans had a saying, Ira furor brevis est"]
- **Bitterness / grudge-bearing** — anger that "last[s] a long, long time," recycling old hurts. [Powlison, ch. 4; NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins" ("hardened into grudge-bearing")]
- **Self-pity** — a recurring distortion in Powlison's anatomy of anger, coupled with returning "evil for evil." [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"]
- **Grumbling / complaining** — the "everyday" form of anger; the sin cured by learned contentment. [Powlison, ch. 15 "The Everyday Angers"]
- **Anger at self / merciless conscience** — self-condemnation as a form of anger; conscience as "a good gift gone bad." [Powlison, ch. 16 "Do You Ever Get Angry at Yourself?"]
- **Contempt** — the point at which just anger tips into sin, being "heightened into contempt for the offender." [NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins"]
- **Envy (dyadic) vs. jealousy (triadic)** — envy = displeasure at another's good; jealousy = a possessive attachment threatened by a rival; God is jealous, never envious. [NDCEPT, "Jealousy and Envy"]
- **Covetousness / *pleonexia* as idolatry** — distorted desire for what is another's, escalating from feeling to intent to a life-dominating idolatry. [NDCEPT, "Covetousness"]
- **Purity of heart** — Grudem's frame for the tenth commandment: God requires right inner desire, not merely right action. [Grudem, ch. 42 "Purity of Heart"]
- **Irascible vs. concupiscible passions** — the passions of aggression (anger, fear, daring) shaped by fortitude, and of desire shaped by temperance. [Cambridge, ch. 7 "Virtue ethics"]
- **Rightly-ordered affections vs. *apatheia*** — passions ordered "to reason and reason to God" (Augustine) rather than eliminated (Stoic). [Cambridge, ch. 6 "Natural law and Christian ethics"]
- **Meekness / gentleness as anger's answering virtue** — the classical cure paired to the deadly sin of anger. [Blackwell, "Why Christian Ethics Was Invented" (anger/meekness pairing); Blackwell, ch. 22 "Sharing Peace"]
- **"Slow to anger"** — God's own patience (Exodus 34:6) held up as the human template: "to become slow to anger is to become like God"; slowness to anger is willingness "to work with wrong over time," not indifference. [Powlison, ch. 7; ch. 10 "God Is Slow to Anger and Quick to Show Mercy"]
- **"Desires that battle within you" / cravings** — Powlison's James-4 desire-analysis: conflict flows from frustrated wants that "dig in" like an army besieging a city, not from circumstances; a good desire wanted "too much" becomes a demand. [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace"]
- **"Playing God" / "I AM GOD"** — the self-deifying core of bad anger: "My kingdom come, my will be done," making one's own desires into divine law. [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger"; ch. 11 "A Mirror on Your Anger"]
- **"The million-dollar question" ("Why do you get angry?")** — the pivotal diagnostic that "doesn't look at your circumstances; it looks at you," distinguished from Question 1 (when/where/with whom/at what). [Powlison, ch. 13 "Eight Questions"]
- **Eight questions** — Powlison's frame-by-frame method for "taking your anger apart to put you back together," dissecting any anger event mild or intense. [Powlison, ch. 13 "Eight Questions: Taking Your Anger Apart to Put You Back Together"]
- **"Lifelong rehab" / "in process"** — change of anger as slow reorientation across a lifetime ("The Christian life begins with an awakening and continues with a reorientation process"), not a technique. [Powlison, ch. 11 "A Mirror on Your Anger"]
- **Fruit of the Spirit / self-control** — self-control and gentleness as Spirit-produced virtues (Gal. 5:22–23), governing the passions from a changed heart rather than by willpower. [Grudem, near "the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace"; Frame, near "loving, faithful, self-controlled"]
- **"Rotten words" / the tongue** — anger's characteristic speech that "tear[s] down rather than build up" (Eph. 4:29); the tongue as a "recalcitrant little bit of flesh" and words as "weapons." [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace"; NDCEPT, "Speech and the Tongue"]
- **Magnanimous / large-hearted** — Powlison's term for the charity aspect of good anger: "refusing to be petty and sectarian," a "consistent policy of generosity and kindness" modeled on God (and Lincoln). [Powlison, ch. 8 "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy, Part 2", near "True charity is magnanimous"]
- **The private "kangaroo court"** — the rigged inner courtroom of resentment where the offender is "allowed no defense attorney . . . no due process . . . no possibility of mercy," the verdict predetermined. [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger", near "the trial is rigged"]
- **"The anger family"** — Powlison's/Paul's grouping of the many kindred sins (irritation, complaining, judgmentalism, passive aggression, bitterness, hostility, violence); "half" of Paul's vice-lists belong to it. [Powlison, Introduction, near "half his list belongs to the anger family"]
- **"Half-right" anger** — anger that responds to a real wrong but then "goes all wrong," mixing a just core with corrupt overflow. [Powlison, ch. 3–4, near "Her anger is half-right"]
- **Mild / veiled / buried / intense angers** — the four intensities under which anger hides: mild (frustration, complaining), veiled (judgmental thoughts, passive aggression), buried (hidden, anesthetized), intense (bitterness, hostility, violence). [Powlison, Introduction]
- **Common grace's restraint of anger** — the observation that even outside redemption God restrains and orders human anger; Leanne's clean childhood outrage is "God's common grace in action." [Powlison, ch. 6 "Nature, Nurture . . . and Human Nature", near "God's common grace in action"]

## 6. Scenario motifs for item writers

1. A driver leans on the horn and mutters curses when cut off in traffic, then stews about it for the rest of the commute. (→C1, C3, C10)
2. Someone re-reads a rude work email a dozen times, drafting cutting replies in their head and rehearsing the injustice all afternoon. (→C1, C3, C4)
3. A person learns a close friend just got the promotion and raise they wanted, and feels a sinking resentment at the friend's success rather than gladness. (→C13, C14, C16)
4. A parent carries a slow-burning resentment toward an adult child who moved away and rarely calls, letting small slights accumulate into a settled grievance. (→C8, C9)
5. A homeowner chronically complains about the weather, the neighbors, prices, and "how things are going" — a habitually sour, negative running commentary. (→C10, C17)
6. A voter feels a hot contempt for people on the other side of a political issue, privately regarding them as stupid or evil rather than as opponents. (→C1, C13)
7. One partner in a relationship becomes possessive and jealous whenever the other spends time with friends, treating the beloved's other attachments as a threat. (→C14)
8. After a miscarriage (or the death of a parent), a believer is furious at God, convinced God "could have stopped it and didn't," and stops praying. (→ Split A, C4, C18)
9. A person nurses a years-old grudge against a former friend who betrayed a confidence, replaying the betrayal and refusing every occasion to let it go. (→C8, C9, C18)
10. Someone spirals into self-pity after a setback — "nothing ever works out for me, everyone else has it easy" — and marinates in the unfairness. (→C10, C17)
11. A shopper explodes at a slow, apologetic cashier over a minor pricing error, then feels a wince of regret walking out. (→C1, C3, C10)
12. A sibling quietly seethes with envy at a brother's new house and vacations, and finds themselves secretly hoping his good fortune won't last. (→C13, C15, C16)
13. A commuter endures a maddening customer-service phone call — menus, hold music, a dropped line — and takes the frustration out on the next person they meet. (→C10, C3)
14. A survivor of childhood abuse says "I'll never get over what he did to me," torn between bitterness that hardens and a hope of being marked-but-not-disfigured. (→C18, C8)
15. A perfectionist is chronically angry at themselves — "I'm such an idiot, I always fail" — and their self-reproach hardens into a settled self-contempt. (→C17)
16. Stuck in a long jam on the way to a make-or-break appointment with a dead phone, a driver feels anger tangle together with anxiety, helplessness, and guilt, then numbs it with snacks and the radio. (→C19, C1)
17. Asked why they blew up, a person points only at the trigger — "anyone would be angry at that" — and never asks what frustrated desire the outburst actually revealed about them. (→C19, C21)
18. A newly married couple notices the same fight recurs every week at the same moment, each privately certain that their own good want (rest for one, closeness for the other) entitles them to be angry. (→C19, C21)
19. Someone who has "tried everything" to master their temper expects a technique to fix it overnight and grows discouraged when change turns out to be slow, uneven, and lifelong. (→C22)
20. In an argument one person keeps a running ledger of the other's faults and lets fly deliberately cutting, belittling words meant to wound rather than to resolve anything. (→C23, C1)
21. A believer grits their teeth to suppress irritability by sheer willpower, never seeking the Spirit-grown gentleness and self-control that would reshape the reaction itself. (→C24, C22)
22. A habitually critical person is quietly certain they are simply "right" and everyone else is wrong, mistaking self-righteous contempt for moral clarity. (→C21, C1)
23. A person replays a betrayal in an inner "courtroom" where the offender gets no defense, no benefit of the doubt, and no possibility of mercy — the verdict fixed in advance. (→C8, C21)
24. Faced with a persistently difficult family member, someone chooses to keep loving them patiently "over time," bearing with them without either exploding or going cold. (→C20, C6)
25. A person is genuinely wronged by a coworker's dishonesty and is right to be indignant, but lets the just grievance swell into contempt for the coworker's whole character and a wish for their downfall. (→C26, C1)
26. Someone insists "I'm not an angry person" while regularly indulging cold silences, cutting jokes, and judgmental thoughts about others — veiled and buried forms they don't count as anger. (→C28, C25)
27. A parent's harshness at a child's small misbehavior visibly involves flushed face, raised voice, racing thoughts, and a grab for control all at once — anger as a whole-body, whole-person reaction, not a detached "feeling." (→C25)
28. A person compares themselves favorably to more obviously hot-tempered people and concludes they have no anger problem, dodging honest self-examination. (→C28, C21)
29. A colleague privately savors the news that a more successful rival has stumbled, tasting the small pleasure of seeing someone admired brought low. (→C13, C16)
30. Someone treats a sharp outburst as harmless "because I apologized right after," never examining the frustrated demand that produced it. (→C19, C22)
31. A driver's flash of road rage passes within a moment, yet they keep rehearsing the offense for the rest of the hour — a "brief madness" that will not let go. (→C3, C8)
32. A believer prays "please take away this feeling" about a stubborn resentment but does no honest work to name the ruling desire underneath it. (→C19, C22)
33. A manager confronts a genuine wrong on their team forcefully but fairly — naming the problem, protecting the person harmed, and leaving the door open to repair rather than to punish. (→C2, C7, C27)
34. A person who was gravely wronged years ago channels the pain into helping others in the same situation, marked by what happened but not consumed by rage over it. (→C18, C26)

## 7. Distinctions & pitfalls for item writers

These are common failure modes an anger/passions item can probe, each grounded in the sources:

- **The goal of change is not a "placid temperament."** Redeemed anger is not numbness or an "unflappable demeanor"; the opposite of tantrums and bitterness is constructive, merciful engagement — the "right kind of tough," not merely "nice." An item that treats calm detachment as the ideal misreads the tradition. [Powlison, ch. 3 (near "a placid temperament and unflappable demeanor"); ch. 8, near "these three mercies don't make you 'nice.' They make you the right kind of tough"]
- **Diagnosis (why) is distinct from description (what/when/where).** Locating the *trigger* ("traffic," "he was late") is not the same as identifying the *motive* (the frustrated desire that ruled the heart). Strong items force the second step, not just the first. [Powlison, ch. 13 "Eight Questions"]
- **Anger rarely travels alone.** In real events it comes "rolled into one seething ball of miseries" with anxiety, guilt, helplessness/depression, and craving for comfort; items can test whether a responder can distinguish the anger strand from its companions rather than collapsing them. [Powlison, ch. 13 "Eight Questions"]
- **A good want is not the problem; the *ruling* of the want is.** Powlison is emphatic that "it's not the (good) desires that are the problem" but that a good desire "wanted too much" becomes a demand. Items should not frame ordinary desires (rest, respect, love) as themselves sinful. [Powlison, ch. 12 "He Gives More Grace"]
- **Formation runs through nurture *and* nature.** Anger patterns are partly learned by "enculturation and socialization" (being "discipled" into the reactions modeled around us) and partly native equipment; a monocausal "it's just how I was raised" or "it's just my temperament" is a dodge. [Powlison, ch. 6 "Nature, Nurture . . . and Human Nature", near "enculturation and socialization"]
- **Level confusions drive the two neutral-vs-loaded debates.** As Splits B and E note, "the bare capacity for anger is neutral" and "every actual anger event is morally loaded" can both be held; likewise an emotion can be *commanded/blamed* without being *instantly summonable*. Items exploiting these should not force a false either/or. [NDCEPT, "Anger"; Powlison, ch. 12; Frame, ch. 21 "THE EMOTIONS"]
- **God's anger is the calibration standard, and it is unlike ours.** God's wrath is fair, slow, merciful, and aimed only at real evil; using human retaliatory anger as the template for God's — or vice versa — is the error the parable-of-the-workers case exposes. [Powlison, ch. 9 "Good and Angry?"; ch. 10 "The Best Anger, the Best Love"]
- **Suppression, venting, and transformation are three different remedies.** The tradition rejects both bare suppression (Stoic numbing) and simple catharsis/venting, favoring transformation into rightly-ordered, merciful displeasure; an item offering only "bottle it up" vs. "let it all out" omits the position the sources actually hold. [Powlison, Introduction and ch. 7–8; Cambridge, ch. 6 "Natural law and Christian ethics"]
- **Anger at self is still anger.** Chronic self-condemnation is not humility or conscientiousness but a distortion in the same family, in which conscience becomes "a merciless slavemaster"; items should not code self-directed rage as morally safe. [Powlison, ch. 16 "Do You Ever Get Angry at Yourself?"]
- **Bitterness is time-extended anger, not a separate emotion.** The distinguishing mark is duration and rehearsal — the "recycling" of old hurts — so an item can test whether a responder recognizes long-nursed grievance as the same sin matured rather than a settled "personality." [Powlison, ch. 4 "'I'm Against That'"; NDCEPT, "Deadly Sins"]
- **"Righteous anger" is easily counterfeited by self-righteousness.** Because bad anger *feels* most righteous exactly when it is most self-exalting ("I'm so right, because you are so wrong"), an item can probe whether a responder can tell genuine indignation at real wrong (Leanne) from self-justifying temper wearing its costume. [Powlison, ch. 5 "All of You Does Anger"; ch. 6 "Nature, Nurture . . . and Human Nature"]
- **Envy and jealousy are not interchangeable.** Envy (displeasure at another's good) is always a vice; jealousy (a possessive attachment threatened by a rival) can be righteous — hence God is "jealous" but never "envious." Items that use the words loosely will mis-code the moral verdict. [NDCEPT, "Jealousy and Envy"; Meilaender, ch. 11 "Love and the Loves"]
