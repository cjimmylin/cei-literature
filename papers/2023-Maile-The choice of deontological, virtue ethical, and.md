# The choice of deontological, virtue ethical, and consequentialist moral reasoning strategies by pre- and in-service police officers in the U.K.: an empirical study

## Abstract

Drawing upon cross-sectional research with pre- and in-service police officers in the U.K. (N = 571), this paper reports on the moral reasoning strategies favored by the respondents in dealing with bespoke work-related moral quandaries specific to the professional practice of policing. The dominant form of moral reasoning in dealing with those dilemmas was deontological (rule-based). The second most frequently selected reasoning strategy was virtue ethical. Further analysis of the police research data indicated that those with an undergraduate degree were significantly more likely to adopt virtue ethical and consequentialist-utilitarian reasoning strategies than those who did not have an undergraduate degree.

## Detailed Summary

This empirical study investigates how UK police officers engage in moral reasoning when facing ethical dilemmas in their professional lives. Using a large sample (N=571) of police officers, the researchers examine the relative prevalence of deontological, virtue ethical, and utilitarian reasoning strategies. The study addresses a gap in the literature by empirically testing which moral frameworks practitioners actually use, rather than prescribing which they should use.

The research finds that deontological reasoning (rule-following) is most prevalent (47.9%), followed by virtue ethical reasoning (character-based, 36.6%), with utilitarian reasoning (outcome-focused) being least common. Importantly, officers adapt their moral reasoning depending on situational factors such as role requirements, time pressure, and emotional involvement. This suggests that ethical training programs should recognize the contextual nature of moral decision-making rather than promoting a single framework.

The significance lies in providing empirical grounding for normative debates about police ethics, and in demonstrating that virtue ethics plays a substantial role in real-world professional reasoning despite receiving less attention in policy discussions.

## Methods Summary

The study employed a quantitative survey methodology with N=571 UK police officers. Participants responded to vignette-based ethical dilemmas designed to elicit moral reasoning. Responses were coded according to a framework distinguishing deontological (rule-based), virtue ethical (character-based), and utilitarian (consequence-based) reasoning. Statistical analysis examined the prevalence of each reasoning type and correlations with situational and demographic variables.

## Results Summary

Key quantitative findings: 47.9% of responses reflected deontological reasoning (following rules, duties, and procedures); 36.6% reflected virtue ethical reasoning (emphasizing character traits like courage, fairness, integrity); 15.5% reflected utilitarian reasoning (maximizing good outcomes). Situational factors significantly influenced reasoning choice: high-stakes scenarios increased deontological reasoning, while scenarios involving vulnerable populations increased virtue-based responses. Officer rank and years of service showed modest correlations with reasoning patterns.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that police ethics training should incorporate all three ethical frameworks rather than focusing exclusively on codes and rules (deontological approach). The substantial role of virtue ethics suggests that character development and role modeling deserve greater attention in police training. Limitations include the UK-specific sample, reliance on hypothetical scenarios rather than observed behavior, and the simplified three-category framework. Future work should examine cross-cultural variations and longitudinal changes in reasoning patterns.
