## Abstract

When do people find it acceptable to sacrifice one life to save many? Cross-cultural studies suggested a complex pattern of universals and variations in the way people approach this question, but data were often based on small samples from a small number of countries outside of the Western world. Here we analyze responses to three sacrificial dilemmas by 70,000 participants in 10 languages and 42 countries. In every country, the three dilemmas displayed the same qualitative ordering of sacrifice acceptability, suggesting that this ordering is best explained by basic cognitive processes rather than cultural norms. The quantitative acceptability of each sacrifice, however, showed substantial country-level variations. We show that low relational mobility (where people are more cautious about not alienating their current social partners) is strongly associated with the rejection of sacrifices for the greater good (especially for Eastern countries), which may be explained by the signaling value of this rejection. We make our dataset fully available as a public resource for researchers studying universals and variations in human morality.

## Detailed Summary

This study presents the largest cross-cultural investigation of moral preferences in sacrificial dilemmas, analyzing responses from 70,000 participants across 42 countries in 10 languages. Using the "Classic" mode of the Moral Machine website, the researchers examined how people respond to three variants of the trolley problem: Switch (redirecting a trolley to kill one instead of five), Loop (redirecting where the one person's body stops the trolley), and Footbridge (pushing a large person to stop the trolley). The research addresses a fundamental question in moral psychology: when do people find it acceptable to sacrifice one life to save many?

The study documents both universal patterns and significant cultural variations. Universally, all 42 countries showed the same qualitative ordering of sacrifice acceptability: Switch > Loop > Footbridge, suggesting this pattern reflects basic cognitive processes rather than cultural norms. However, quantitative acceptance levels varied substantially across countries. A key finding is the strong association between "relational mobility" (the fluidity with which people can develop new relationships) and endorsement of utilitarian sacrifice. In societies with low relational mobility (particularly Eastern countries), people are more cautious about expressing attitudes that might alienate social partners, and endorsing sacrifice in moral dilemmas is perceived as socially risky since such individuals are seen as less trustworthy.

## Methods Summary

Data was collected through the Moral Machine website's Classic mode from June 2017 to March 2019. Participants were presented with three trolley problem scenarios (Switch, Loop, Footbridge) in random order, with decisions recorded for users who completed all three scenarios. The sample was restricted to countries with at least 200 responses per scenario. Scenarios were presented with graphical illustrations and text descriptions in 10 languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, French, German, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish). Country of access was geolocalized via IP address. An optional demographic survey captured age, gender, education, political beliefs, and religiosity. The sample skewed toward young (75% under 32), male (75%), college-educated participants with progressive and secular leanings. Country-level analyses examined correlations with individualism, religiosity, GDP, and relational mobility scores.

## Results Summary

The Switch-Loop-Footbridge pattern was universal across all 42 countries, with country-level averages of 81% endorsing sacrifice in Switch, 72% in Loop, and 51% in Footbridge. The difference between Switch and Loop (effect size h = 0.10) showed convergence across countries with increasing sample size, suggesting psychological homogeneity. In contrast, the Loop-Footbridge difference showed increasing heterogeneity with larger samples, indicating strong cultural influences on Footbridge responses. Relational mobility showed robust positive correlations with sacrifice endorsement: r = 0.67 for Switch, r = 0.56 for Loop, and r = 0.64 for Footbridge (all p < 0.01). These effects were especially pronounced among Asian countries (correlations 0.83-0.95), which span the lower half of relational mobility scores. Previous East-West differences (e.g., US vs. China) were confirmed but contextualized as potentially overestimated due to comparing countries at extremes within their regions.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that the qualitative Switch-Loop-Footbridge pattern reflects basic cognitive processes that are universal across cultures, while quantitative variations in sacrifice acceptability are culturally influenced. Relational mobility emerges as a key explanatory variable: in societies where social relationships are more fixed and difficult to replace, people are more reluctant to endorse utilitarian sacrifice, possibly because such endorsement sends a negative social signal marking one as untrustworthy. The study acknowledges sample limitations (skewed toward young, educated, male participants) but represents the largest and most diverse dataset on trolley problem responses to date. The dataset is made publicly available as a resource for researchers studying universals and variations in human morality.
