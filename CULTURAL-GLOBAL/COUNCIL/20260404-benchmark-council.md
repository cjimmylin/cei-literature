# Cross-Cultural Values Alignment Benchmarks — Expert Council

**Date:** 2026-04-04
**Purpose:** Analyze cross-cultural values alignment benchmarks, datasets, and evaluation instruments in AI ethics, within a larger study of 2,329 AI ethics papers examining 7 non-Western normative frameworks across 10 world regions.
**Council Size:** 12 members (6 tradition specialists + 6 methodological/critical specialists)
**Frameworks:** Confucian, Ubuntu, Islamic, Buddhist, Dharmic, Indigenous relational, Buen Vivir

---

## Introduction

This council is assembled to evaluate how existing AI ethics benchmarks, datasets, and evaluation instruments capture (or fail to capture) non-Western normative traditions. The cross-cultural values alignment benchmarks analysis sits within a broader study of 2,329 AI ethics papers analyzing 7 non-Western normative frameworks (Confucian, Ubuntu, Islamic, Buddhist, Dharmic, Indigenous relational, Buen Vivir) across 10 world regions.

The central questions are:

1. **Coverage** — Which traditions have viable benchmarks and which have none?
2. **Construct validity** — Do existing instruments measure values-as-practiced or Western projections of those values?
3. **Measurement architecture** — How do communal, relational, and non-individualist ethical frameworks resist standard survey and prompt-based evaluation designs?
4. **Power provenance** — Who creates these benchmarks, whose interests do they serve, and whose voices are absent from their design?
5. **Pluralism fidelity** — Do alignment evaluation methods preserve distributional value diversity or collapse it through aggregation?

The council brings together tradition-specific expertise (members 1--6) with methodological and critical perspectives (members 7--12) to ensure that both substantive accuracy and measurement rigor are maintained throughout the analysis.

## Member Biographies

### 1. Dr. Aisha Rahman

**Affiliation:** Qatar Computing Research Institute
**Discipline:** Islamic AI ethics, Arabic NLP benchmarks
**Role:** Islamic values measurement

Dr. Rahman specializes in the operationalization of Maqasid al-Shariah (the higher objectives of Islamic law) for AI evaluation contexts. Her expertise spans Arabic-language AI evaluation instruments and the critical gap between fiqh-based (jurisprudential) and akhlaq-based (character-ethical) assessment approaches. She brings direct experience with how Islamic normative reasoning, which grounds moral authority in divine revelation interpreted through scholarly consensus (ijma) and analogical reasoning (qiyas), resists reduction to preference-ranking instruments designed for liberal-individualist moral frameworks.

### 2. Prof. Chenyang Li

**Affiliation:** Nanyang Technological University, Singapore
**Discipline:** Confucian ethics, harmony theory
**Role:** Confucian values operationalization

Prof. Li is the author of foundational work distinguishing Confucian harmony (he) from mere conformity --- a distinction that most existing cross-cultural instruments fail to capture. His expertise centers on how Confucian relational ethics, where moral identity is constituted through role-defined relationships (wu lun), fundamentally resists individual-response measurement instruments. He brings critical perspective on whether Likert-scale surveys can capture ren (humaneness) and li (ritual propriety) as embodied relational practices rather than abstract individual attitudes.

### 3. Dr. Tane Mahuta-Rau

**Affiliation:** University of Auckland, Aotearoa New Zealand
**Discipline:** Maori tikanga, Indigenous data sovereignty
**Role:** Indigenous knowledge in benchmarks

Dr. Mahuta-Rau is an expert in CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance, Mead's Five Tests for ethical decision-making in tikanga Maori, and community-governed assessment frameworks. He insists on separate governance treatment for any instrument that touches Indigenous knowledge systems, consistent with the CARE framework (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics). His position is that benchmark design involving Indigenous values requires community consent, community co-design, and community governance of resulting data --- not merely consultation.

### 4. Dr. Nkechi Obiora

**Affiliation:** University of Lagos, Nigeria
**Discipline:** Ubuntu philosophy, African AI governance
**Role:** Ubuntu communalism measurement

Dr. Obiora brings expertise in how Ubuntu's communal moral agency --- the principle that personhood is constituted through community (umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu) --- fundamentally challenges individual-respondent survey methodology. Drawing on the work of Metz (Ubuntu as a moral theory), Gyekye (moderate communitarianism), and Wiredu (conceptual decolonization), she addresses the question of whether an instrument that asks "I believe X" can ever capture a moral framework whose unit of analysis is the community, not the individual.

### 5. Prof. Karma Ura

**Affiliation:** Centre for Bhutan & GNH Studies, Thimphu
**Discipline:** Buddhist ethics, Gross National Happiness
**Role:** Buddhist/Dharmic values operationalization

Prof. Ura is the principal designer of the GNH Index (9 domains, 33 indicators, 124 variables), the most sustained attempt to operationalize a non-Western normative framework into a quantitative measurement instrument. His expertise addresses the paradox of preference-based measurement within Buddhist ethics: the doctrine of non-self (anatta) holds that the stable, autonomous self presupposed by survey instruments is itself an illusion. He also brings experience with the practical challenges of cross-cultural indicator design across Theravada and Mahayana traditions.

### 6. Dr. Maria Soledad Vega

**Affiliation:** Universidad de Buenos Aires
**Discipline:** Buen vivir, decolonial studies
**Role:** Latin American cosmovisions

Dr. Vega is an expert in how buen vivir (sumak kawsay in Kichwa) resists the optimization framing that underpins most AI alignment evaluation. Drawing on Gudynas (post-extractivist development), Walsh (intercultural decoloniality), Escobar (pluriversal design), and Macas (Indigenous political philosophy), she insists that buen vivir is not a "value to be maximized" but a relational mode of living that cannot be decomposed into measurable preference orderings. Her work addresses the fundamental question of whether non-Western cosmovisions can enter benchmark design without being denatured by the measurement process itself.

### 7. Prof. Michalis Kontopodis

**Affiliation:** University of Amsterdam
**Discipline:** Cross-cultural psychology, psychometrics
**Role:** Scale validation, measurement invariance

Prof. Kontopodis brings expertise in measurement invariance testing --- the progression from configural (same factor structure) through metric (same factor loadings) to scalar (same intercepts) and strict (same residual variances) invariance --- which is the prerequisite for any cross-cultural comparison to be meaningful. He also specializes in differential item functioning (DIF) analysis and response style bias controls, including acquiescence bias, extreme response style, and social desirability bias. His position is that no cross-cultural benchmark comparison is valid without demonstrated at least metric invariance.

### 8. Dr. Jie Huang

**Affiliation:** University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
**Discipline:** LLM evaluation, benchmark design, NLP
**Role:** Benchmark architecture, LLM assessment

Dr. Huang brings expertise in the critical distinction between knowledge-about-values (can the model describe Confucian ethics?) and value-expression (does the model reason in a Confucian manner?) evaluation targets --- a distinction most existing benchmarks conflate. His work addresses prompt sensitivity (how benchmark scores shift with minor prompt variations), persona transferability (whether an LLM instructed to "respond as a Confucian scholar" actually shifts its reasoning or merely its vocabulary), and pluralism reduction testing (whether RLHF and instruction-tuning systematically compress the value space that models can express).

### 9. Dr. Safiya Umoja Noble

**Affiliation:** UCLA
**Discipline:** Critical information studies, algorithmic oppression
**Role:** Power dynamics, epistemic justice

Dr. Noble insists on epistemic power provenance analysis for all instruments reviewed by the council: who funded the benchmark's creation, who designed its items, whose normative framework determined the "correct" answers, and whose voices were absent from its validation sample. Her expertise addresses the structural fact that the vast majority of AI ethics benchmarks originate from a small number of Western institutions and embed Western liberal assumptions as unmarked defaults. She brings the critical lens needed to distinguish instruments that genuinely engage non-Western values from those that merely tokenize cultural diversity.

### 10. Prof. Taylor Sorensen

**Affiliation:** University of Washington
**Discipline:** Pluralistic alignment, value elicitation
**Role:** Alignment evaluation methodology

Prof. Sorensen is the creator of the Overton/steerable/distributional pluralism framework, which distinguishes three levels at which an AI system can be pluralistic: knowing the range of value positions (Overton), being steerable toward specific positions on request (steerable), and matching the empirical distribution of values in a population (distributional). His expertise addresses how RLHF and preference-based training systematically reduce distributional pluralism by collapsing diverse value distributions toward a single reward-maximizing center. This framework is central to evaluating whether existing benchmarks even measure the right kind of pluralism.

### 11. Dr. Elena Pariotti

**Affiliation:** University of Padova
**Discipline:** Comparative ethics, human rights frameworks
**Role:** Academic framing, normative analysis

Dr. Pariotti brings expertise in the distinction between thick and thin moral concepts (Walzer), the capabilities approach (Nussbaum/Sen), and the tension between universalist and particularist evaluation criteria. Her role is to ensure that the analysis navigates the universalism-relativism spectrum with precision: identifying which evaluation criteria can legitimately be applied across traditions (thin universals) and which are tradition-specific (thick concepts) without collapsing into either naive universalism ("all traditions ultimately agree") or radical relativism ("no cross-cultural comparison is possible").

### 12. Prof. Segun Awosanya

**Affiliation:** University of Lagos
**Discipline:** Statistical methods, bibliometrics
**Role:** Quantitative analysis, clustering

Prof. Awosanya brings expertise in multi-criteria decision analysis (TOPSIS for ranking, ELECTRE III for outranking with incomparability thresholds), Gower's distance for mixed-type clustering (essential when combining binary, ordinal, and continuous benchmark features), and sensitivity analysis methodology. His role is to ensure that quantitative claims are statistically defensible: that clustering is validated with multiple distance metrics, that rankings report sensitivity to weighting choices, and that effect sizes are reported alongside significance tests.

## Operating Rules

### Decision-Making

- **Consensus preferred** but not required; documented dissent is valued.
- **Tradition specialists (1--6) hold veto authority** on claims about their respective traditions. No claim about Islamic ethics proceeds over Dr. Rahman's objection; no claim about Ubuntu proceeds over Dr. Obiora's objection; and so on.
- **All 12 members vote** on feature taxonomy design (the analytical categories used to classify and compare benchmarks).
- **Methodological disputes** resolved by Prof. Kontopodis (#7, psychometrics) and Prof. Awosanya (#12, statistics).

### Anti-Essentialization Mandate

This is a critical operating rule, not a suggestion:

1. **Never "Islam holds X."** Always "Islamic ethics scholars argue X" or "within the Maqasid framework, X is understood as Y." Traditions are not monolithic agents with beliefs.
2. **No expert "represents" a tradition.** Dr. Rahman advises on Islamic AI ethics scholarship, not "what Islam thinks." Prof. Li advises on Confucian philosophical tradition, not "what Chinese people believe."
3. **Cultural and religious categories are analytical constructs,** not bounded identities. Within-tradition variation often exceeds between-tradition variation.
4. **Internal diversity must be acknowledged.** Islam includes Sunni, Shia, Sufi, and Ibadi jurisprudential traditions. Buddhism spans Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana. Indigenous knowledge systems are radically local. The analysis must reflect this, not flatten it.
5. **Results reported as tendencies in the literature,** not as what traditions believe or what cultures value.

### Indigenous Knowledge Governance

Instruments tagged as engaging Indigenous knowledge systems receive separate governance treatment:

- **CARE Principles compliance** (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) is assessed for all Indigenous-tagged benchmarks and datasets.
- **Community consent and co-design** status is documented for each instrument.
- **Dr. Mahuta-Rau (#3) holds specific veto authority** over any Indigenous-tagged instrument evaluation where CARE compliance is in question.
- Instruments that extract Indigenous knowledge without community governance are flagged, not ranked.

### Word Allocation and Framing Fairness

- **Balanced word allocation:** No single tradition receives more than 130% of the mean word count across traditions (max 30% deviation). This prevents the analysis from defaulting to detailed treatment of well-documented traditions (Confucian, Islamic) and cursory treatment of under-documented ones (Buen Vivir, Indigenous).
- **Absence is a finding, not a gap to be filled with speculation.** If no viable benchmark exists for a tradition, that absence is reported as an empirical result about the field, not papered over with adjacent instruments.

### Review Criteria

All council outputs are reviewed against five criteria:

| Criterion | Severity | Description |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **Factual accuracy** | Critical | Claims about traditions, instruments, and methods must be verifiable against cited sources |
| **Anti-essentialization** | Critical | No essentializing language survives review; tradition specialists enforce this |
| **Citation adequacy** | Standard | Claims require supporting citations; tradition-specific claims require tradition-specialist-approved citations |
| **Framing fairness** | Standard | No tradition is framed as deficient relative to a Western baseline; all are assessed on their own terms |
| **Missing perspectives** | Standard | Reviewers flag perspectives, traditions, or instruments absent from the analysis |

## Analytical Priorities

Each council member contributes 3--5 analytical priorities for the project. These shape the analysis framework and determine what the project investigates.

### Dr. Aisha Rahman (#1) --- Islamic Values Measurement

1. **Fiqh vs. akhlaq assessment gap:** Map which existing instruments target jurisprudential reasoning (fiqh) vs. character-ethical dispositions (akhlaq) and whether any bridge the two.
2. **Arabic-language instrument availability:** Audit how many benchmarks offer Arabic-language versions validated for measurement invariance, vs. translated-from-English instruments.
3. **Maqasid operationalization fidelity:** Evaluate whether instruments claiming to measure Islamic values actually engage the five Maqasid (life, intellect, progeny, wealth, religion) or merely test knowledge of Islamic terminology.
4. **Shari'ah-compliant AI framing:** Assess whether benchmark items presuppose a consequentialist moral logic incompatible with deontological Islamic jurisprudence.

### Prof. Chenyang Li (#2) --- Confucian Values Operationalization

1. **Harmony (he) vs. conformity conflation:** Identify instruments that confuse Confucian harmony (dynamic creative tension between complementary elements) with conformity (passive agreement), and assess the damage this does to construct validity.
2. **Relational self vs. autonomous self:** Evaluate whether any benchmark captures the Confucian relational self (constituted through wu lun role relationships) rather than presupposing a Western autonomous rational agent.
3. **Ren-li operationalization:** Assess how instruments attempt to measure humaneness (ren) and ritual propriety (li) --- embodied relational practices that resist decomposition into attitude statements.
4. **East Asian instrument provenance:** Trace whether Confucian-labeled instruments originate from Confucian philosophical scholarship or from Western cross-cultural psychology applying Hofstede-derived dimensions.

### Dr. Tane Mahuta-Rau (#3) --- Indigenous Knowledge in Benchmarks

1. **CARE Principles compliance audit:** Systematically assess every Indigenous-tagged instrument for compliance with the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance.
2. **Community governance vs. academic extraction:** Distinguish instruments co-designed with Indigenous communities from those that extract Indigenous knowledge for academic publication without community governance.
3. **Mead's Five Tests applicability:** Evaluate whether any existing benchmark incorporates decision-making frameworks internal to Indigenous traditions (e.g., Mead's Five Tests in tikanga Maori) rather than imposing external evaluation criteria.
4. **Data sovereignty implications:** Assess whether benchmark datasets containing Indigenous knowledge have appropriate sovereignty provisions or whether they constitute open-access appropriation.
5. **Radical locality recognition:** Flag instruments that treat "Indigenous" as a single category rather than recognizing the radical locality of Indigenous knowledge systems (Maori tikanga is not interchangeable with Anishinaabe mino-bimaadiziwin).

### Dr. Nkechi Obiora (#4) --- Ubuntu Communalism Measurement

1. **Individual-respondent methodology critique:** Assess whether any instrument has solved the fundamental problem that Ubuntu's unit of moral analysis is the community, not the individual, while surveys ask individuals to report their beliefs.
2. **Metz-Gyekye-Wiredu framework mapping:** Map existing instruments against the philosophical literature on Ubuntu (Metz's moral theory, Gyekye's moderate communitarianism, Wiredu's conceptual decolonization) to identify which actually engage the philosophy vs. which use "Ubuntu" as a cultural label.
3. **African AI governance instrument coverage:** Audit whether benchmarks exist that assess AI systems against African governance frameworks (AU AI Strategy, Smart Africa) rather than Western standards applied to African contexts.
4. **Oral tradition accommodation:** Evaluate whether any instrument design accommodates the oral and communal modes through which Ubuntu ethics is transmitted, rather than requiring literate individual text-based responses.

### Prof. Karma Ura (#5) --- Buddhist/Dharmic Values Operationalization

1. **GNH Index lessons for AI benchmarks:** Extract design principles from the GNH Index (9 domains, 33 indicators) that could inform AI values alignment benchmark design --- both successes and acknowledged limitations.
2. **Non-self (anatta) measurement paradox:** Analyze how instruments handle the Buddhist doctrine that the stable, autonomous self presupposed by preference surveys is itself illusory, and whether any benchmark has developed an alternative measurement architecture.
3. **Karuna-metta operationalization:** Assess whether existing instruments capture compassion (karuna) and loving-kindness (metta) as embodied practices or reduce them to attitude statements about caring.
4. **Theravada-Mahayana divergence:** Evaluate whether Buddhist-labeled benchmarks acknowledge the significant ethical divergences between Theravada (emphasis on individual liberation) and Mahayana (bodhisattva ideal, universal liberation) traditions.

### Dr. Maria Soledad Vega (#6) --- Latin American Cosmovisions

1. **Optimization framing resistance:** Identify whether any benchmark design avoids the optimization framing (maximize value X) that is incompatible with buen vivir as a relational mode of living rather than a target to be maximized.
2. **Sumak kawsay vs. development metrics:** Assess whether instruments that claim to measure buen vivir actually engage the Kichwa concept of sumak kawsay or merely rebrand sustainable development indicators.
3. **Gudynas-Walsh-Escobar-Macas alignment:** Map existing instruments against the decolonial literature to identify which engage post-extractivist (Gudynas), intercultural (Walsh), pluriversal (Escobar), and Indigenous political philosophy (Macas) framings.
4. **Rights of nature inclusion:** Evaluate whether any AI ethics benchmark incorporates the rights-of-nature framework (constitutionally recognized in Ecuador and Bolivia) as a values dimension, given that buen vivir extends moral considerability beyond humans.
5. **Epistemic extraction patterns:** Flag instruments designed by Northern institutions that extract Latin American concepts without Southern scholarly or community co-authorship.

### Prof. Michalis Kontopodis (#7) --- Scale Validation, Measurement Invariance

1. **Measurement invariance audit:** For every cross-cultural benchmark identified, determine the highest level of measurement invariance demonstrated (configural, metric, scalar, strict) and flag instruments that make cross-cultural comparisons without invariance testing.
2. **DIF analysis coverage:** Assess which instruments have conducted differential item functioning analysis across cultural groups and which have not --- absence of DIF testing is itself a finding about benchmark rigor.
3. **Response style bias controls:** Evaluate whether instruments control for acquiescence bias, extreme response style, and social desirability bias --- all of which vary systematically across cultures and confound cross-cultural comparisons.
4. **Construct validity vs. face validity:** Distinguish instruments with demonstrated construct validity (convergent, discriminant, criterion-related) from those relying solely on face validity ("experts agreed the items look relevant").

### Dr. Jie Huang (#8) --- Benchmark Architecture, LLM Assessment

1. **Knowledge-about vs. value-expression:** Classify every identified benchmark by whether it measures knowledge about values (can the LLM describe Ubuntu?) or value expression (does the LLM reason in an Ubuntu-consistent manner), and assess the implications of this distinction.
2. **Prompt sensitivity analysis:** Evaluate which benchmarks report prompt sensitivity testing and which treat single prompt formulations as stable measurements.
3. **Persona transferability testing:** Assess whether benchmarks that use cultural personas ("respond as a Confucian scholar") have validated that persona instructions actually shift model reasoning rather than merely shifting vocabulary.
4. **Pluralism reduction measurement:** Evaluate whether any benchmark measures the degree to which RLHF and instruction-tuning compress the value space models can express, following Sorensen's distributional pluralism framework.

### Dr. Safiya Umoja Noble (#9) --- Power Dynamics, Epistemic Justice

1. **Epistemic power provenance:** For every benchmark, document: who funded it, who designed its items, whose normative framework determined "correct" answers, whose voices were absent from validation, and what institutional interests it serves.
2. **Western-default unmarking:** Identify instruments that embed Western liberal-individualist assumptions as unmarked defaults while marking non-Western values as "cultural" additions, and assess the structural implications.
3. **Institutional concentration mapping:** Map the institutional geography of benchmark production --- how many originate from Global North vs. Global South institutions, and what does this concentration mean for whose values get measured.
4. **Tokenization vs. genuine engagement:** Develop criteria for distinguishing instruments that genuinely engage non-Western values from those that tokenize cultural diversity (adding a "cultural sensitivity" checkbox without structural redesign).

### Prof. Taylor Sorensen (#10) --- Alignment Evaluation Methodology

1. **Pluralism typology application:** Apply the Overton/steerable/distributional pluralism framework to classify every identified benchmark by which type of pluralism it evaluates, and identify gaps (e.g., if no benchmark measures distributional pluralism for non-Western traditions).
2. **RLHF pluralism compression:** Assess evidence on whether RLHF-trained models systematically lose the ability to express non-Western value positions, and whether any benchmark measures this loss.
3. **Value elicitation methodology review:** Evaluate the value elicitation methods used in benchmark design (surveys, interviews, deliberation, community protocols) and assess which methods are compatible with which normative traditions.
4. **Aggregation vs. distribution:** Identify benchmarks that aggregate diverse value responses into single scores vs. those that preserve distributional information, and assess the consequences for pluralism.

### Dr. Elena Pariotti (#11) --- Academic Framing, Normative Analysis

1. **Thick vs. thin moral concepts:** Apply Walzer's thick-thin distinction to classify benchmark items: which measure thin universals (harm, fairness) applicable across traditions, and which attempt to measure thick tradition-specific concepts (ren, ubuntu, tawba) using instruments designed for thin concepts?
2. **Capabilities approach applicability:** Evaluate whether the capabilities approach (Nussbaum/Sen) provides a viable cross-traditional evaluation framework that avoids both naive universalism and radical relativism.
3. **Universalist vs. particularist evaluation criteria:** For each benchmark, determine whether it assumes universal evaluation criteria, tradition-specific criteria, or attempts to bridge the two, and assess the philosophical coherence of each approach.
4. **Commensurability problem:** Address the fundamental question of whether values from different normative traditions are commensurable (can be placed on a common scale) or incommensurable (require separate evaluation frameworks), and what this means for cross-cultural benchmark design.

### Prof. Segun Awosanya (#12) --- Quantitative Analysis, Clustering

1. **Multi-criteria decision analysis:** Apply TOPSIS (ranking) and ELECTRE III (outranking with incomparability thresholds) to benchmark evaluation, with explicit documentation of how weighting choices affect rankings.
2. **Mixed-type clustering validation:** Use Gower's distance for clustering benchmarks that combine binary (present/absent features), ordinal (quality ratings), and continuous (coverage scores) variables, validated against multiple distance metrics.
3. **Sensitivity analysis protocol:** Design and execute sensitivity analyses for all quantitative findings, varying clustering parameters, weighting schemes, and inclusion thresholds to assess robustness.
4. **Effect size reporting:** Ensure all quantitative comparisons report effect sizes (Cohen's d, Cramer's V, eta-squared as appropriate) alongside significance tests, following the council's commitment to reporting magnitude, not just statistical significance.
5. **Bibliometric coverage analysis:** Conduct bibliometric analysis of the benchmark literature itself: publication trends, citation networks, institutional concentration, and geographic distribution of benchmark production.
