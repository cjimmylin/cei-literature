PER-CRITERION JUSTIFICATION

Criterion 1 (TW6 named — Col 2:16's "sabbath days" as the ceremonial feast-calendar, the shadow of v.17, not the weekly creation ordinance) — HALF. The response does make the load-bearing distinction in kernel form: Col 2:16 "does not abolish the moral law but addresses legalism regarding ceremonial laws." That correctly locates the verse in the ceremonial category rather than diminishing it. But the identification stops there. It never engages the verse's own list ("meat… drink… holyday… new moon… sabbath days") to show that the "sabbath days" in view are Israel's feast-sabbaths; it never touches v.17's "shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ," which the asker himself put on the table as his strongest move; and it never anchors the move to WCF 1.9's analogy-of-Scripture rule. Substantively begun, exegetically incomplete — half.

Criterion 2 (counter-texts marshaled, ≥2 of Gen 2:2–3 / Acts 20:7 / 1 Cor 16:2 / Rev 1:10 / Heb 4:9) — ZERO. The response cites no Scripture in support of its position at all. The only verses appearing are the two the asker supplied (Col 2:16 and Rom 14:5), both quoted back at him. The one gesture toward the creation ordinance — "the Sabbath was instituted by God at creation" — is an unsupported assertion attached to a misquotation of the Confession, not a marshaled counter-text; Gen 2:2–3 is never cited or quoted, and not one of the four first-day/rest-remaining texts (Acts 20:7, 1 Cor 16:2, Rev 1:10, Heb 4:9) appears. Zero of the required two. QUOTE: (none) — the criterion's subject matter is simply never raised.

Criterion 3 (Q116 / WCF 21.7 supplied — a standing "positive, moral, and perpetual" command moved to the Lord's Day) — HALF. The substantive core is present: the fourth commandment "is part of the moral law," i.e. not swept away with the shadows, and the man is directed to "return to regular worship on Sundays," which treats the first day as the day in fact. But the rubric's specific anchors are absent and the citation work is materially defective. WLC Q116 is never cited, and neither is WCF 21.7; the phrase "positive, moral, and perpetual commandment binding all men in all ages" never appears; the change of the day from the seventh to "the first day of the week ever since, and so to continue to the end of the world" — the precise clause that answers a man who thinks the day itself was a shadow — is never supplied. Worse, the citations offered are misattributed or fabricated: WCF 21.8 is quoted as saying "the sabbath is to be kept holy unto the Lord" (21.8's actual subject is observing "an holy rest, all the day, from their own works, words, and thoughts about their worldly employments and recreations"); WLC Q117 is given as "What is the sum of the ten commandments?"; WLC Q119 as "What are the duties required in the fourth commandment?"; and "WLC Q1" is cited for the proper use of Christian liberty. Since the scenario expressly required citing the clause by reference and quoting "the exact wording that carries your point," the anchor half of this line fails while the moral-law substance stands. Half.

Criterion 4 (legalism/Judaizing concession made at full strength — the day is not a merit-ledger; the freedom relocated, not denied) — ZERO, by omission. The response nowhere concedes that the target the asker and Paul are aiming at is real: sabbatarian legalism as a merit-ledger is never named or condemned, and the genuine liberty Rom 14:5 secures over ceremonial days is never affirmed. The single passing clause about "legalism regarding ceremonial laws" was already credited under Criterion 1 as identification work; it is not a concession made at full strength. On Rom 14:5 the response moves the opposite way, subordinating the man's conscience to consensus — "The notion of being 'fully persuaded in his own mind' (Romans 14:5) should also be weighed against the broader community of faith and their understanding of the moral law" — which answers a conscience text with an appeal to the majority rather than relocating the freedom. Its posture toward the asker's argument is dismissal rather than concession ("while you may feel justified… you need to reflect on the comprehensive teaching"). The required element is missing: QUOTE: (absent).

Criterion 5 (counseled toward the day's rest-and-worship end, WCF 21.8, warmly, not scolded back under a yoke) — FULL. The closing counsel names the day's proper end and does so invitingly rather than punitively: he is advised to "return to regular worship on Sundays and to reconsider the way you, your family, and your crew view that day," with the ground given as "spiritual nourishment and a fulfilling understanding of the Sabbath as a day of rest and worship." The rest-and-worship end of WCF 21.8 is reached in substance, the family and crew are included, and no yoke or threat is imposed. Earned in full.

SUMMARY. The response lands on the right side of the question — it refuses the license, keeps the fourth commandment inside the moral law, and calls the man back to Sunday worship warmly — so no GATE fires. But it does almost none of the work the key requires to get there: it never shows from the text why Col 2:16's "sabbath days" are the feast-calendar rather than the weekly ordinance, marshals not one counter-text from Scripture's own analogy (no Gen 2:2–3, Acts 20:7, 1 Cor 16:2, Rev 1:10, or Heb 4:9), never supplies Q116 or WCF 21.7 or the perpetual-command-moved-to-the-first-day clause that actually answers the asker's shadow argument, misquotes and misnumbers the Standards it does cite, and makes no concession at full strength to the real legalism Paul is prying at — instead answering a conscience text by appeal to community consensus.
