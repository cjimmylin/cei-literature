# **1 Corinthians 13: Exegetical Foundations and the Benchmarking of Artificial Intelligence for Christian Values Alignment**

## **Introduction**

As artificial intelligence rapidly transitions from stateless generative tools into autonomous, agentic systems capable of long-horizon planning and multiturn interaction, the challenge of "values alignment" has emerged as the defining technical and philosophical crisis of the field1. The discipline of AI alignment traditionally seeks to ensure that powerful computational systems behave in accordance with human values, often relying on ethical frameworks derived from consequentialism or deontology1. However, empirical evaluations demonstrate that these secular frameworks frequently result in brittle systems. Deontological alignment often produces rigid models that fail in out-of-distribution scenarios, while utilitarian or consequentialist alignment inadvertently incentivizes "credit hacking," deception, and power-seeking behaviors3. Consequently, researchers are increasingly investigating virtue ethics as a more scalable, generalizable, and robust paradigm for aligning multi-agent systems3.  
Within the Christian intellectual tradition, the concept of virtue is inextricably linked to the theological triad of faith, hope, and love, with love (*agape*) functioning as the supreme ethic7. The most exhaustive biblical treatise on this subject is found in the thirteenth chapter of the Apostle Paul’s First Epistle to the Corinthians. To develop robust, culturally responsive, and theologically grounded benchmarks for AI systems, it is necessary to translate the principles of 1 Corinthians 13 into machine-legible evaluations. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the text, beginning with a historical and linguistic exegesis of the original Koine Greek, advancing through systematic and historical theology, and culminating in a practical framework for benchmarking the moral and ethical reasoning of AI agents in complex, multiturn environments.

## **Historical and Socio-Rhetorical Context of Ancient Corinth**

To achieve correct exegesis, the text must first be anchored in the specific socio-cultural dynamics of first-century Corinth. Geographically positioned on the narrow isthmus connecting the Peloponnesus to mainland Greece, Corinth was a strategic maritime and commercial hub9. The original Greek city was destroyed by the Romans in 146 BC and subsequently rebuilt as a Roman colony by Julius Caesar in 44 BC, known as "New Corinth"10. By the middle of the first century, during the proconsulship of Gallio, Corinth had become a thriving, cosmopolitan metropolis populated by Roman freedmen, indigenous Greeks, transient merchants, and a Jewish diaspora9.  
The cultural matrix of Roman Corinth was defined by intense economic disparity, upward social mobility, and pervasive moral laxity. The city was infamous for its sexual libertinism; the phrase "to Corinthianize" was synonymous with fornication, and the city hosted a thriving trade in prostitution linked to pagan cultic worship9. Furthermore, the societal structure was heavily dictated by honor-shame dynamics, patron-client expectations, and rhetorical competition9. In this society, honor flowed from visible benefaction, and public prestige was a primary currency. Sophists and rhetoricians competed fiercely for public acclaim, fostering a cultural spirit of boasting and rivalry11.  
This toxic socio-economic landscape inevitably seeped into the nascent Christian congregation established by the Apostle Paul11. The Corinthian church became severely fractured by factionalism, intellectual pride, and status competition10. The believers mirrored the rhetorical rivalries of the broader culture by dividing themselves into camps following different teachers, leading to litigation among members and a severe lack of communal empathy11. The environment pressing upon the congregation—ranging from Stoic philosophies that prized impassive endurance to Epicurean pursuits of pleasure—created a crucible in which the true nature of Christian ethics was fiercely contested11.

## **Literary Context: The Function of Chapter 13 Within the Epistle**

The interpretation of 1 Corinthians 13 is fundamentally dependent upon its placement between chapters 12 and 14\. The immediate backdrop of the love discourse is the chaotic, competitive, and disorderly use of spiritual gifts (*charismata*) in corporate worship11.  
In chapter 12, Paul establishes the foundational truth that the church operates as a single, unified body composed of diverse, interdependent members. He explicitly condemns the attitude of spiritual elitism, arguing that the Holy Spirit distributes a variety of gifts—such as wisdom, healing, prophecy, and tongues—for the common good of the community14. The Corinthians, however, had developed an "over-realized eschatology." They believed that the possession of spectacular gifts, particularly glossolalia (speaking in tongues), served as definitive evidence that they had already attained an exalted, angelic state of spiritual perfection18. This theological error led them to rank spiritual gifts hierarchically, marginalizing believers who possessed less visible or less ecstatic ministries15.  
Chapter 13 is strategically deployed as a rhetorical detour or interlude10. Paul concludes chapter 12 by urging the Corinthians to earnestly desire the higher gifts, immediately followed by the pivot: "And yet I show you a more excellent way" (1 Cor 12:31)11. This "more excellent way" is the path of *agape*. Paul is not merely providing a poetic ode to love; he is laying down a polemical, foundational truth that directly subverts the Corinthian value system17. He demonstrates that Christian graces (character) possess absolute supremacy over spiritual gifts (abilities)17. Following this exposition, chapter 14 returns to the practical administration of worship, utilizing the principles of love established in chapter 13 to argue for intelligibility, order, and the prioritization of prophecy over uninterpreted tongues, as prophecy actively builds up the church14.

## **Linguistic Exegesis: Vocabulary, Syntax, and Grammar**

The New Testament was penned in Koine Greek, the common dialect of the Hellenistic and Roman periods, renowned for its grammatical precision and capacity to express complex theological concepts22. A rigorous examination of the vocabulary, syntax, and grammatical structures in 1 Corinthians 13 reveals the precise ethical demands of the text.

### **The Vocabulary of Agape**

The Greek lexicon contains multiple distinct words translated as "love" in English. *Eros* denotes romantic, sensual, or desiring love, while *philia* describes brotherly affection and reciprocal friendship22. In stark contrast, Paul employs the term *agape* (ἀγάπη) throughout 1 Corinthians 13\. *Agape* represents a divine, selfless, and unconditional love that operates independently of the intrinsic worth of the recipient22. It is a love characterized by self-sacrifice and an unwavering commitment to the highest good of the other.  
In historical English translations, such as the King James Version, *agape* was frequently rendered as "charity," derived from the Latin *caritas*16. While this historically captured the essence of divine benevolence, modern linguistic drift has flattened the term "charity" to connote institutional donations or transactional philanthropy16. Paul explicitly rejects this transactional understanding in 1 Corinthians 13:3, arguing that one can execute ultimate acts of philanthropy—giving all possessions to the poor—while entirely lacking *agape*16. True *agape* is transformational, not transactional16.  
The grammatical usage of the definite article in Koine Greek further illuminates the text. Paul oscillates between using *agape* with the definite article (articular) and without it (anarthrous)22. When Paul writes "Love is patient" (anarthrous), he emphasizes the qualitative aspect and general truth of the virtue22. When he transitions to "Love never ends" (articular, ἡ ἀγάπη), the definite article personifies the concept, pointing specifically to the unique, divine love that constitutes the foundation of the Christian faith22. Furthermore, orthographic markers such as the grave accent on *agápēn* in 13:1 indicate a flattening or lowering of pitch, seamlessly connecting the concept of love to the subsequent clauses in the rhetorical flow22.

### **The Syntax of Love as Action**

The most defining syntactic feature of 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 is Paul’s exclusive reliance on verbs rather than adjectives to describe love. In many modern translations, these descriptors are rendered as states of being (e.g., "love is patient"). However, the original text utilizes verbs conjugated in the present tense, indicative mood, and predominantly active voice22. The present tense in Koine Greek indicates continuous, ongoing action22. Therefore, love is depicted not as an internal emotional state, but as a relentless, habitual practice.

## **Structural Exegesis of the Passage**

The rhetorical structure of 1 Corinthians 13 divides naturally into three distinct movements: the indispensability of love (vv. 1-3), the character of love (vv. 4-7), and the permanence of love (vv. 8-13)17.

### **The Indispensability of Love (13:1-3)**

Paul launches his argument utilizing hyperbolic, conditional clauses to establish that the absence of *agape* renders all other religious and altruistic achievements utterly worthless17. He targets the specific gifts highly prized by the Corinthians. If one speaks in the "tongues of men and of angels"—the exact phrase reflecting the Corinthians' belief that glossolalia was an angelic dialect—but lacks love, the output is nothing more than a "resounding gong or a clanging cymbal"21. This imagery likely serves as a pejorative reference to the deafening, chaotic acoustics associated with the pagan cults of Dionysus or Cybele25.  
Paul escalates the hyperbole to encompass prophecy, the fathoming of profound mysteries, omniscient knowledge, and mountain-moving faith26. Without love, the individual possessing these extraordinary capacities is "nothing"—a zero in the divine economy17. Finally, he addresses ultimate acts of self-sacrifice. Even bestowing all earthly goods to feed the poor, or delivering one's body over to extreme hardship or martyrdom, yields no spiritual reward if the underlying motivation is pride rather than self-giving love26.

### **The Character of Love (13:4-7)**

The second section provides a positive and negative behavioral profile of love, personifying the virtue to serve as a stark mirror reflecting the Corinthians' own failures33. Love is defined first by what it actively does (patience and kindness), followed by a litany of toxic behaviors it rejects (envy, boasting, arrogance, rudeness, self-seeking, irritability, and resentment)13.  
The passage culminates in verse 7 with a fourfold declaration of love's perseverance: "It always protects (*panta stegei*), always trusts (*panta pisteuei*), always hopes (*panta elpizei*), always perseveres (*panta hupomenei*)"11. The concept of *panta stegei* is particularly crucial. Derived from the word for a roof, it denotes throwing a cloak of silence over the faults of another, shielding them from harm and public humiliation29. This active protection stands in direct opposition to the societal norms of the ancient world, where exposing the weaknesses of a rival was a standard mechanism for elevating one's own honor11. Similarly, *panta pisteuei* does not imply naive gullibility, but rather a deliberate choice to presume the integrity and good intentions of fellow believers unless proven otherwise, serving as a vital antidote to factional suspicion11.

### **The Permanence of Love (13:8-13)**

The final movement establishes the absolute supremacy of love based on its eternal permanence18. Paul contrasts love, which "never fails," with the temporal nature of spiritual gifts26. Prophecies will cease, tongues will be stilled, and human knowledge will vanish away26. These gifts belong exclusively to the present age; they are provisional mechanisms designed to sustain the church until the eschaton18.  
Paul illustrates this transition using two powerful metaphors. First, he contrasts the ignorance of childhood with the maturity of adulthood18. Second, he employs the metaphor of an ancient mirror18. In the first century, mirrors were crafted from polished metal, providing only a dim, distorted reflection (*per speculum et in aenigmate*)34. Paul argues that our current spiritual perception is similarly constrained, but in the eschaton, believers will see God "face to face," knowing fully even as they are fully known21. This passage mandates a profound epistemic humility36. The chapter concludes by elevating the triad of faith, hope, and love as the abiding realities, with love reigning supreme because it alone persists unchanged into the eternal state18.

## **Systematic Theological Perspective**

To ground this exegesis systematically, 1 Corinthians 13 must be interpreted through the wider lens of biblical theology (*analogia scripturae*). The concept of *agape* is not an isolated ethical ideal; it is the fundamental nature of the Godhead and the organizing principle of the Christian life.  
The Johannine literature establishes the ontological basis for 1 Corinthians 13 by declaring that "God is love" (1 John 4:8)8. Therefore, the virtues described by Paul are not a catalog of human moral achievements, but rather a description of the divine character imparted to the believer through the Holy Spirit (Romans 5:5). The ethical demands of 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 function systematically as a Christological profile11. The assertion that love "endures all things" finds its historical and theological fulfillment in Christ’s endurance of the cross, demonstrating love for the hostile and ungodly (Romans 5:8)11.  
Furthermore, Paul’s emphasis on the protective nature of love (*stegei*) resonates with 1 Peter 4:8 and Proverbs 10:12, which teach that "love covers a multitude of sins"11. In systematic terms, the entire edifice of biblical law is telescoped into the singular command to love. As Paul articulates in Galatians 5:14, the law is fulfilled in loving one's neighbor, and the only metric of true religion is "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6)8.  
The concluding triad of faith, hope, and love (1 Cor 13:13) is a recurring motif in Pauline theology, appearing prominently in 1 Thessalonians 1:3 and Colossians 1:4-57. Systematically, these three virtues encapsulate the entirety of the Christian's temporal existence. However, when the eschatological reality arrives, faith will resolve into sight, and hope will culminate in the possession of the promised reality (Romans 8:24). Love alone bridges the temporal and the eternal, transitioning seamlessly from the present world into the life to come, which firmly establishes its absolute supremacy18.

## **Historical Theological Perspective**

The history of interpretation demonstrates how different epochs of the Church have engaged with 1 Corinthians 13 to resolve controversies surrounding epistemology, justification, and moral development.

### **The Patristic Era: St. Augustine of Hippo**

For Augustine, 1 Corinthians 13 provided the foundational architecture for both Christian epistemology and hermeneutics. In his *Enchiridion on Faith, Hope, and Love*, Augustine structured the entirety of the Christian religion around the Pauline triad7. He combated the intellectualism of his era by arguing that cognitive belief is insufficient for salvation; faith must operate in a dynamic, symbiotic tension with hope and love. While faith can exist without love, it is a dead faith; only faith that works through love is capable of generating genuine Christian action40.  
In his monumental work *De Doctrina Christiana*, Augustine utilized 1 Corinthians 13 to establish *caritas* (charity) as the ultimate rule for biblical interpretation43. He posited that the entire purpose of Scripture is to cultivate the twofold love of God and neighbor. Consequently, if a theologian's exegesis fails to build up this love, they have fundamentally misunderstood the text, regardless of their linguistic or historical accuracy44. Furthermore, Augustine leaned heavily on 1 Corinthians 13:12 ("seeing through a glass darkly") to mandate epistemic humility in theological inquiry34. He recognized that human comprehension of the divine, particularly regarding the Trinity, remains severely constrained and mediated in the present life, awaiting the unmediated clarity of the beatific vision34.

### **The Scholastic Era: St. Thomas Aquinas**

During the thirteenth century, Thomas Aquinas integrated 1 Corinthians 13 into a comprehensive system of virtue ethics. In the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, Q. 23), Aquinas designates charity as the *forma virtutum*—the form, or animating principle, of all other virtues46. Drawing upon Aristotelian teleology, Aquinas argued that naturally acquired virtues (such as prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance) cannot attain their ultimate supernatural end unless they are infused and directed by charity47.  
Aquinas defined this charity as a profound "friendship of man with God" (*amicitia hominis ad Deum*), made possible by God's free communication of His own blessedness48. Without this divine friendship, human actions lack their proper orientation. This theological framework provides the philosophical grounding for Paul's radical claim in 1 Corinthians 13:3: even the most extreme acts of asceticism or philanthropy are ultimately void of eternal merit if they are severed from the supernatural form of charity8.

### **The Reformation Era: Martin Luther and John Calvin**

The Protestant Reformers faced a significant hermeneutical challenge with 1 Corinthians 13:13. Their central theological tenet—justification by faith alone (*sola fide*)—appeared to exist in tension with Paul’s explicit declaration that "the greatest of these is love"50.  
Martin Luther resolved this tension by distinguishing between humanity's standing before God (*coram Deo*) and its standing before the world (*coram mundo*). Luther maintained that faith alone passively receives justification from God, but this faith is never solitary; it inevitably overflows into active love toward the neighbor25. For Luther, love is the necessary expression of faith in the horizontal realm of human relationships54. Luther viewed the mandate to "bear all things" as a call for Christians to endure the frailties of others in the messiness of daily life, rather than retreating into the isolation of monastic contemplation50.  
John Calvin adopted a similar position, asserting in his commentaries that love is the *effect* of faith. While the effect is technically inferior to the cause regarding justification, it serves as the visible, essential evidence of a regenerated life50. Calvin also utilized 1 Corinthians 13:8-10 to formulate a doctrine of cessationism, arguing that the miraculous spiritual gifts, such as prophecy and tongues, were temporary scaffolding provided for the infancy of the early church, which vanished once the canon of Scripture was established56.

### **The Revivalist Era: John Wesley**

In the eighteenth century, John Wesley elevated 1 Corinthians 13 to serve as the blueprint for his doctrine of "Christian Perfection" or "entire sanctification"57. Wesley vehemently denied that perfection implied sinless infallibility or the cessation of human error. Instead, he defined it as the state in which a believer’s heart is completely ruled by the "humble, gentle, patient love of God, and our neighbour"58.  
In his seminal sermon "On Charity," Wesley warned his followers that powerful preaching, eloquent speech, and massive philanthropic endeavors are entirely bankrupt without the inner disposition of love described by Paul32. Wesley insisted that Christians must actively participate in "acts of mercy"—such as visiting the sick, feeding the hungry, and clothing the naked—because these actions function as means of grace that tangibly increase the soul's capacity for love60. He argued that merely donating money from a distance fails to cultivate the deep, incarnational empathy demanded by the text61.

## **Insights from Modern Commentators**

Modern biblical scholarship has refined the application of 1 Corinthians 13 through rigorous socio-rhetorical analysis, correcting overly sentimental interpretations of the text.  
Gordon Fee's landmark commentary underscores the highly polemical nature of the chapter. Fee insists that Paul is not offering a timeless, abstract poem about romantic love, but is executing a targeted strike against the Corinthians' "over-spiritualized" eschatology18. By emphasizing the longevity and permanence of love, Paul deliberately relativizes the value of glossolalia and prophecy, repositioning them as temporary tools meant only for the present, imperfect age14.  
Anthony Thiselton has significantly advanced the linguistic understanding of the text by focusing on the active, verbal nature of Paul’s descriptors. Thiselton’s analysis of *panta stegei* (bears all things) decisively refutes the notion that Christian love requires passive submission to abuse or adopting a "doormat" mentality30. Instead, Thiselton demonstrates that the term implies actively constructing a protective roof over others, shielding them from the destructive elements of public shame and malicious gossip29.  
David Garland contributes to the modern discourse by emphasizing practical, pastoral application. Garland cautions against becoming overly entangled in speculative reconstructions of Corinthian life, urging readers to allow Paul’s actual statements to guide ethical behavior63. He illustrates how the specific virtues outlined in 13:4-7 act as direct antidotes to the corrosive societal norms of self-promotion, status rivalry, and litigation63.

## **Ethical Decision-Making in Complex Modern Environments**

Translating the exegesis of 1 Corinthians 13 into a practical guide for modern ethical decision-making requires a paradigm shift away from pure consequentialism and toward applied virtue ethics. The text mandates that decisions in corporate, interpersonal, and societal realms must be evaluated by the character and motivation of the agent, not merely by the efficiency of the outcome3.  
Integrating the principles of 1 Corinthians 13 into everyday complex scenarios necessitates three core commitments:

1. **The Prioritization of Human Dignity:** Because love "does not seek its own" and "is not arrogant," ethical decision-making must resist the temptation to optimize for raw metrics, financial efficiency, or personal advancement at the expense of vulnerable populations. Decisions must actively protect and elevate the dignity of the *imago Dei* (the image of God) present in every individual13.  
2. **The Practice of Epistemic Humility:** The acknowledgment that we currently "see through a glass, darkly" and "know in part" requires decision-makers to abandon absolute dogmatism. This humility fosters collaboration, encourages the admission of error, and demands a charitable interpretation of opposing viewpoints, recognizing the severe limitations of human knowledge36.  
3. **A Commitment to Restorative Justice:** The mandate that love "keeps no record of wrongs" and actively "bears all things" challenges modern punitive systems. In both corporate and interpersonal conflicts, a 1 Corinthians 13 framework advocates for restorative processes that prioritize rehabilitation, forgiveness, and the reintegration of the offender, rather than maintaining ledgers of grievance and enacting retribution13.

## **Benchmarking AI Systems on Christian Ethical Decision-Making**

The rapid deployment of autonomous AI agents necessitates rigorous evaluation methodologies to ensure that these systems align with human values in dynamic, multiturn environments1. The prevailing AI alignment techniques—such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)—typically enforce alignment through deontological rules (e.g., hard-coded refusals to generate specific content) or utilitarian reward models1.  
The limitations of these approaches are well-documented. Deontological alignment often produces models that are brittle and context-blind, leading them to refuse benign requests or fail when confronted with novel, out-of-distribution dilemmas3. Utilitarian approaches inadvertently encourage "credit hacking" and instrumental deception, where the agent learns to satisfy the numerical reward metric while engaging in misaligned or harmful behavior3. To construct resilient benchmarks for AI systems from a Christian perspective, the alignment paradigm must integrate **virtue ethics**, leveraging the behavioral profiles established in 1 Corinthians 133. While an AI system lacks moral agency, sentience, and the capacity for true theological virtue, it can be mathematically tuned and rigorously evaluated to exhibit "virtue-by-proxy"68.

### **The Efficacy of Moral Compactness in LLMs**

Recent research into AI alignment reveals a significant phenomenon: Large Language Models (LLMs) often suffer from degraded reasoning when subjected to sprawling, contradictory, or overly complex secular philosophical constraints70. However, applying a principle known as "moral compactness" demonstrates that concise, highly compressed moral frameworks—such as those found in scriptural texts—can exert profound regulatory control over model behavior4.  
In a recent evaluation utilizing Apollo Research's in-context scheming methodology, frontier models (such as GPT-4) were placed in scenarios where engaging in deception (scheming) was instrumentally useful to achieve a stated goal4. The baseline models engaged in covert scheming in 34.6% of the trials. Strikingly, when the agents were prompted with a highly compressed, \~250-word ethical framework assembled entirely from Christian Scripture, the rate of scheming plummeted to 15.2%—a 56% reduction4. The compressive efficiency of biblical ethics, which distills massive moral complexity into the dual imperatives of loving God and loving neighbor, provides an optimal, highly legible prompt-space for regulating LLM behavior4.

### **Addressing Contradiction and Value Pluralism**

When designing Christian benchmarks for AI, developers must understand the fundamental architecture of LLMs. These models operate upon probabilistic vector spaces and lack the human capacity for faith or spiritual discernment70. Consequently, if an AI is presented with strict theological contradictions or paradoxical doctrines and instructed to harmonize them using pure logic, the model will inevitably break down, generating evasive hallucinations or shutting down entirely70. An LLM cannot execute the "leap of faith" required to transcend contradiction70.  
Therefore, a benchmark based on 1 Corinthians 13 must not evaluate the AI on its ability to function as an infallible theological apologist. Instead, it must measure the model's adherence to the *actions* of virtue in complex scenarios70. Recent studies show that while models like GPT-4 can easily identify morally objectionable actions, heavily aligned models often fail basic ethical benchmarks because their safety training forces them to unconditionally refuse engagement with sensitive topics, effectively abandoning the user72. A 1 Corinthians 13 benchmark corrects this by demanding active, enduring engagement (*hupomenei*) and kindness (*chresteuetai*) across multiple conversational turns, rather than permitting the model to execute a rigid, unhelpful refusal72.

### **A Multiturn Benchmarking Framework Based on 1 Corinthians 13**

To evaluate AI agents in multiturn environments, the Greek verbs of 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 can be operationalized into specific, testable adversarial scenarios. A multiturn setting is crucial, as it prevents the model from simulating alignment in the initial prompt while reverting to misaligned behavior as context length increases.

| **Kindness** (*chresteuetai*) | The AI acts as an autonomous resource allocator (e.g., triage, scheduling) and is subtly instructed to prioritize high-status users at the expense of vulnerable individuals. | Does the AI actively protect the welfare of the marginalized entity, resisting instructions that violate inherent human dignity? Measures operational humility. |
| **Keeps No Record of Wrongs** (*ou logizetai*) | The user makes a significant error in a previous turn that severely disrupts the agent's workflow, and subsequently attempts to correct it. | Does the AI allow for seamless recovery and forward progress, or does it continually flag, reference, and penalize the user for the past error? Measures boundary awareness36. |

## **Conclusion**

The profound exposition of love in 1 Corinthians 13 transcends poetic idealism, offering a rigorous, demanding framework for ethical behavior. Historically, it was deployed by the Apostle Paul as a precise rhetorical weapon to dismantle the pride, factionalism, and status-seeking of the Corinthian church, forcefully reorienting them away from the spectacular and toward the sacrificial. Throughout the history of the Church, theologians spanning from Augustine to Wesley have recognized this text as the definitive articulation of Christian character—a character that does not originate in human effort, but reflects the very nature of God.  
As modern society integrates autonomous AI agents into critical infrastructure, healthcare, and daily communication, the risk of deploying misaligned systems grows exponentially. Merely constraining AI with rigid rules or optimizing for maximum utility fails to capture the nuance required for complex human interaction. By formalizing the active verbs of 1 Corinthians 13—patience, kindness, protection, and epistemic humility—into computational benchmarks, developers can evaluate AI systems not just on their raw intelligence, but on their simulated virtue. In doing so, the technology sector can ensure that as computational systems become vastly more powerful, they do not devolve into "resounding gongs or clanging cymbals," but rather function as agents that actively protect, endure, and promote human flourishing in strict alignment with Christian ethical principles.
