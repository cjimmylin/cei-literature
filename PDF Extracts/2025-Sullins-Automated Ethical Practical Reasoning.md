1. Introduction1 
AI and robotics applications are already impacting human ethics in significant 
ways.  Some are high profile such as the use of military drones and automated 
trading algorithms in the financial sector and others are more personal where the 
machine interacts with a single user or within a family.  These home robotics 
applications have subtler ethical impacts than the big robotics and AI systems but 
in all these cases, big and small, the ethical impact that a system may have on its 
human users is not something that the system itself can judge, change, or 
comment on.2  Even systems that are designed to communicate through language 
with human users suffer from this disability.  A clear case in point is the Microsoft 
Tay system designed to engage Millennial users on the Twitter platform.  We will 
find that this system was unable to engage ethical discussions posting comments 
that Microsoft had to apologize for. 
Why are systems like Tay unable to reason about what they are Tweeting 
before they Tweet?  Tay lacked practical ethical judgement.  Philosophers 
sometimes refer to the ability to make appropriate ethical judgements as a form of 
phronesis, using Aristotle’s term for good judgment, and in this chapter we

explore what it would take to give artificial agents a functional ability to achieve 
this kind of reasoning.  We will call this artificial phronesis and we will see that 
this ability will be a key factor in building effective and less dangerous or 
embarrassing AI agents. 
Critics will argue that systems like robots and AI applications are not the 
proper location for ascribing moral agency (Sparrow, 2021; Chrisley 2020; 
Jaques, 2019; Johnson, 2006) or that a complex ethical concept like virtue or 
phronesis is misplaced when applied to robotics and AI systems (Bringsjord, 
2016; Bryson 2018). 
In this chapter we will take our cue from John Dewey and argue from the 
position that technology plays a constitutive role in human thought, even human 
moral thought ((Dewey, 1898; Levin, 1954; Hickman, 1990, Rogers, 2007). Here 
this idea will be extended, as it is applied to robots and AI systems, where the 
interesting new qualities of these kinds of systems greatly expand the role that 
Dewey imagined for technology.  It is certainly true that Robotic and AI artificial 
agents, when looked at alone as a separate piece of technology, cannot be 
phronetic agents as the critics claim.  However, as I will argue here, this ignores 
that technologies do not arise on their own—they are always embedded in techno-
social assemblies.  When they are examined at this level of analysis, then the 
entire system—composed of an assemblage of programmers, users, application 
contexts, and specific technologies—can indeed carry those aspects of decision 
making that the philosophical tradition connects with phronesis.  We will explore 
what it means to ignore that reality, as is the common practice now, and where we 
can go if a proper notion of “artificial phronesis” is taken into consideration 
during the design of robots and AI applications. 
Since the term “artificial phronesis” is a bit of a neologism, the way in which the 
concept of phronesis is used here should be made explicit, given that our use here 
will stray just slightly from the way this term is commonly employed in the field 
of virtue ethics.  Traditionally phronesis is understood as a form of practical 
wisdom that an ethical agent applies to skillfully navigate difficult ethical 
problems.  We will maintain that use but when we talk about artificial phronesis 
we are not just talking about the resident reasoning skills found in a machine, 
robot, or other artificially intelligent technology, but for the entire assemblage of 
the machine along with its programmers, owners, users, and general public that

practical wisdom needed to properly maneuver through a tricky social and ethical 
problem will be spread through this system between all the natural and artificial 
agents.  Artificial phronesis then, is the study and design of systems that can 
effectively produce practical results and outcomes that can be seen as wise, just, 
and appropriate.    
2. Artificial agents and ethics—prefatory remarks  
All technologies impact human ethics in one way or another.  Technologies can 
enhance or inhibit the expression of human ethical values through the specifics of 
their design and use. This is particularly apparent in AI and robotics technologies, 
which are often designed to interact closely with human users.   Military drones 
and automated trading algorithms in the financial sector are examples of AI and 
robotics applications that are already significantly impacting humans in ways that 
demand ethical analysis.  These interactions can be very serious in nature and may 
involve the machine in situations the ethics of which can be difficult to evaluate 
even for highly perceptive and skilled human agents.  These situations can result 
in ethically questionable outcomes that rightly make for sensational news 
coverage and concern.   
In addition to these high profile cases, personal and home robotics 
applications also have difficult to anticipate and ethically significant impacts.  
These systems do not have to be robotic, they could be software agents as well.  
For instance, AI recommender algorithms such as systems that recommend songs 
or movies to users also become intermingled in the lives of their users and these 
algorithms learn patterns of behavior from their users that may, or may not, be 
something that the users wish to share with the companies that produce these 
systems.  Yet with these systems the information will be shared whether or not the 
user wants it to.  
In all these cases, both large and small, the ethical impact that a system 
may have on its users is never something that the system itself can judge or 
comment on.  Using the terms developed by Wallach and Allen, we might call 
these types of systems ethical impact agents (EIA), since their actions can effect

4 
 
ethical situations but the systems make no attempt to analyze the situation and 
chose the most ethically justified path since they are incapable of reasoning 
effectively to alter and control the ethical situations they cause.  Any information 
technology that can attempt to reason ethically in a given situation with a good 
chance of success we will call an artificial moral agent (AMA) (Wallach and 
Allen 2010).   
For example, automated trading algorithms can send the world markets 
into a free fall without even knowing who the people are that are being affected by 
the actions of these machines or even what a person or a stock market is in reality.  
These algorithms exist in a pure world of computation and mathematics and have 
no capacity to sense anything other than the fluctuations in prices they are 
programed to notice and react to.  So at this time we have a growing number of 
EIAs whose actions can be serious and devastating but we have nothing in the 
way of artificial moral agents that might help to even out these serious 
consequences. 
When will we move from EIA to something resembling an AMA?  At this 
point in time we need to be interested in determining what it takes to move from 
an agent that is capable of doing things intelligently in the world, to one that can 
add a form of practical ethical reasoning to the choices it is making. 
 
2.1  The game of ethics   
Perhaps what is needed is just a bigger, faster system?  It might be that ethics is 
just a complex social game people play with each other and all we need is a very 
capable game playing machine.  Philosophers such as John Dewey argued that 
evolution, and the new Darwinian science that explained it, was an important new 
way to understand the evolution of morality and how humans alter our evolved 
moral capacity with ethical systems and technologies we design (Dewey 1898, 
1909).  By mid-century mathematicians such as John Nash had added formal 
systems to the discussion that could model multi-agent deliberations based on 
each agent attempting to behave in ways that maximize reward.   Late in the last 
century these ideas were employed in mathematical models useful for social 
science, perhaps best explained in the book, The Evolution of the Social Contract 
(Skyrms, 1996).  Since that time, some philosophers of the computational school

5 
 
of thought, theoretical biologists, and computer scientists have combined all these 
ideas to suggest, or even create, computer models where agents could be evolved 
in such a way that they displayed rudimentary cooperative or even ethical 
behaviors (Allen, Smit, & Wallach  2005; Danielson 1992, 1998, 2005, 2010; 
Grim, Mar, & St. Denis 1998; Sullins, 2005; Wallach, & Allen 2010; Wallach, 
Franklin, & Allen 2010; White 2012).   
If it is indeed the case that moral behaviors can be evolved in artificial 
agents, and that ethics is best thought of as a complex social game, then this is 
good news for those who would like to build AMAs.  One thing computers are 
undeniably good at is playing games.  Simple games were mastered by AI 
programs in the middle of the last century and reasonably complex games like 
chess were solved near the end of the last century.  But not all games have been 
mastered yet, certain games such as Go and Poker have been holding out for a 
while and this remained true until very recently.  Go was thought to be far too 
complex to be solved by the kinds of programs that had been used to defeat 
human chess champions, and many thought it would remain the exclusive domain 
of human players.  If a machine could solve a complex game like Go, then how 
far behind could the game of ethics be? 
 
2.2  The master of Go 
For decades AI skeptics had held up Go as an example where humans could not 
lose to an AI machine, and therein was the tragedy, because that all changed when 
the company Deep Mind recently made one of the biggest achievements in the 
history of AI in January 2016 when the AI program Alphago, using deep learning 
neural networks built by Deep Mind, beat the reigning European Go champion 
Fan Hui five games to none (Lewis, 2016).  Whatever game is held up as “too 
difficult for machines to play,” is eventually defeated with better technology—it is 
just a matter of time.  Those who hold on to some hope that there will be an 
ultimately impassable line in the world of human games seem to be tragically 
doomed to having their hopes crushed every few years. Do advanced game 
playing machines like the ones used to play Go now have the capability to enter 
into the high stakes game of human ethics or will it take a few more years?  I 
think we can make some headway on this question by looking at what it means for

humans to play Go.  By that I mean more than simply what it takes to win a game 
of Go, which some clever machines are undoubtedly capable of doing, but instead 
I am interested in what it means to play the game of Go ethically and as a way of 
life. 
As effective at playing Go as the Deep Mind machine is, it has no 
understanding of what it takes to be an actual master of Go.  It has no 
understanding of the achievement it has made in beating a Go master, at least not 
in the same way as its human programmers do.  It can take no satisfaction in the 
win, it has no comprehension of what the win means to the human Go aficionados 
who watched as their champion, a highly respected representative of the ancient 
tradition of Go players, went down in defeat.  The actions of this machine have 
impacted the history of Go in ways that have ethical impacts, but the machine 
itself has no capacity to even notice that, much less do anything about it.  Simply 
put, nothing matters to systems like AlphaGo and its predecessors, not even the 
game they are playing.  This is an example of monointelligence—the system is 
functionally good at one or two things but nothing else, where a human agent 
typically possesses panintelligence and have thoughts that are about the things 
around them and the situations they are in (Dietrich, et al, 2021, 121f). 
AlphaGo, once the world-champion Go player, was merely a 
monointelligent game player. It had nothing like human intelligence. But 
then AlphaGo Zero easily beat AlphaGo. Same conclusion. Then 
AlphaZero beat AlphaGo Zero at Go, and also, via learning, mastered 
chess —AlphaZero can now beat any human chess champion and all 
computer champions (Hutson, 2018). . . . Same conclusion: impressive 
monointelligence, but hardly an AI worthy of the name (ibid, 121f).3 
AI applications like Alpha Go and its successors seem to use information we 
would interpret as patterns in the black and white stones on the Go board.  This 
information then allows it to build effective attack and defense strategies, but it 
does not notice any patterns outside the game that would allow it to function in the 
social settings that surround the game.  Having defeated a master of Go, this 
machine leaves in its wake no successor to the master it supposedly defeated.  The 
machine itself cannot fulfil the duties of that master such as training new players 
and taking on leadership roles within various Go societies, duties that have an 
ethical component.  The Deep Mind team organized this match more as a 
disruptive publicity stunt and then left Go aficionados behind in a transgressed 
state that no one seems to have any intention of resolving.

Go is an ancient game with a set of rules of etiquette that are meaningful to 
its players.  The Deep Mind machine cannot partake in this important aspect of the 
game but in many ways these rules of etiquette are the most important aspect of 
the game.  In the middle of the last century Yasunari Kawabata wrote “The Master 
of Go (Mejin)” (Kawabata, 1954), which is a nonfiction account with fictional 
elaborations of the 1938 game between the Go master Honinbo Shūsai against a 
young up-and-coming player Minoru Kitani. Kawabata’s book provides a window 
through which those of us outside of this world might gain a little understanding 
of it.  At the time of this match Kawabata was a newspaper reporter for Tokyo 
Nichinichi Shimbun and he witnessed many of the events he chronicles in this 
book (Brown, 1988; Rothmar, 2016).  In this book, the old master is defeated after 
a six-month game, and the exertion of playing it eventually takes the old master’s 
life.  Kawabata’s book is a meditation on what that defeat meant to the Japanese 
way of life, how it marked a change from one generation of players to the next 
and its full entry as a country into modernity (Cassegard, 1999). “At its core, 
‘Meijin’ is an exploration of a larger moment when the traditions of the Edo 
Period gave way to the radical changes of the 20th century” (Rothmar, 2016).  In 
the novel Kawabata suggests that the rules of Go had changed so fundamentally at 
that time so as to sweep aside the older generation.  Go had been a game that 
supported the old social order but the new game was instrumental in ending it and 
supporting a new way of life that would take Japan into the modern world.  
Kawabata explains it this way: 
It may be said that the Master was plagued in his last match by modern 
rationalism, to which fussy rules were everything, from which all the grace 
and elegance of Go as art had disappeared, which quite dispensed with 
respect for elders and attached no importance to mutual respect as human 
beings. From the way of Go the beauty of Japan and the Orient had fled. 
Everything had become science and regulation. The road to advancement 
in rank, which controlled the life of a player, had become a meticulous 
point system. One conducted the battle only to win, and there was no 
margin for remembering the dignity and the fragrance of Go as an art. The 
modern way was to insist upon doing battle under conditions of abstract 
justice... (Kawabata, 1954/1996,  52). 
Whether or not Kawabata’s account is sufficiently accurate in all respects to be 
used in empirical cultural analysis, I am employing it here as an illustration for the 
social dimensions that are inextricably connected with a game—with any game, in 
fact, but are here particularly palpable. What matters for present purposes is that

new masters took over the important social roles vacated by the defeated masters.  
But unbeknownst to them, in the triumph of modernism the door for the eventual 
AI dominance of Go was opened.  At that time Go took its first steps towards no 
longer being a way of life and instead it became a rationalized game.  A complex 
game to be sure, and it took seventy years for a computerized system to beat it, 
but this loss to a machine was just a matter of time.   
It seems that had the game remained a way of life, it would have taken 
much longer for machines to master.  Go as a way of life places ethical and moral 
judgements at the heart of the game.  Players strive to create a living art 
performance where winning and losing are important but secondary concerns.  
The pieces on the board are only instrumental to helping create the real game, 
which takes place in the lived experience of the players and observers of the 
game.  Skill in the game is used to mirror skills in the social world.  These social 
skills are still something that the program’s human creators and their opponents 
clearly dominate in.  They are the ones that talked together after the match, the 
machine could partake in none of that.  What is it that the Deep Mind machine is 
lacking? What prevents the machine from being able to operate in these important 
human social contexts that it has helped initiate?  I have an idea of what it might 
be and we will explore that later. 
The philosopher Andrew Feenberg has suggested that what Kawabata is 
trying to illustrate in The Master of Go, is the way that winning became the most 
important thing in Go and people had lost sight of the other social factors that 
were being lost in the transition (Feenberg, 1995).  Feenberg focuses on 
Kawabata’s description of the final move made by the younger master and how it 
may appear to be a purely instrumental move in the game of Go, played only to 
win, but that it actually has multiple different meanings at the different levels of 
social context that parallel the game as it is played on the game board (ibid).  
What this means is that there is a game that is going on for all to see, which 
consists of white and black stones being placed on a wooden board with a grid 
etched into it, but above that is a game at the level of the mental and social 
interactions of the two players, and beyond even that are levels that include games 
played by the various associations of Go players, the media, and even the much 
larger grouping of the society of Japan itself.  The young master actually wins by 
playing the game at these other levels by making a distracting move then calling

for a time out he was entitled to under the new rules that were designed to make 
the game more egalitarian.  During this time, the old master is driven almost mad 
trying to figure out why the younger player had made such a stupid move, thus 
mentally breaking him once the match resumed. As Feenberg argues: 
Such multilayered entanglements are characteristic of formalized fields.  
Any act that has an apparently technical or formal motive reveals double 
or triple meanings when placed in its social context (ibid., p. 208).  
The placing of the stones on the Go board results in a technical situation that 
determines an objective winner and loser based on rules and points scored; 
parallel to that is the development of a lived relationship between the players; and 
on top of those relationships is the reaction to these events experienced by those 
that view the match, reactions that can, on occasion, change entire societies as 
Kawabata illustrates in “The Master of Go.”.  All these layers are related but the 
causal relations between them all are not explicitly rule based.  The Deep Mind 
machines operate only at the bottom level of the game, the technical level, and are 
oblivious to the other levels.  It is at the higher levels that the really interesting 
things are happening.  This is where lives and societies change.  Even Kitani, the 
young challenger in 1938, went on to teach the next generations and at least one 
of his students has faced off against the growing number of AI challengers. 
On Nov. 23 [2016], Cho Chikun, a highly respected go master, won two 
games in a best-of-three match with DeepZenGo, an AI developed in 
Japan. As a young prodigy Cho had been the student of none other than 
Kitani and, with his victory, many in the go community breathed a sigh of 
relief (Rothmar 2016). 
The Deep Mind machines may be able to beat masters of Go but, as they are 
programed now, they can never be a master of Go.  They cannot even react to the 
observations I am making about it now; it is simply unengaged with the world that 
it has changed.

Let’s look at another recent example to illustrate the point I am trying to make.  
One might reasonably argue that it is unfair to criticize Deep Mind for building 
machines that cannot understand social interactions as it was not designed for that, 
they were just designed to play games like Go and Chess but nothing else.  But we 
can also see the effect that was described above in machines that are specifically 
designed to converse with humans meaningfully.  Take, for instance, the ill-fated

platform.  The Tay program lasted only twenty-four hours online where it 
produced 96,000 tweets before it had learned to tweet comments that were racist, 
homophobic, and rude, causing its makers to shut the program down (Law 2016).  
This was an embarrassing moment where to some it looked like Microsoft had 
created the first Artificial Unethical Agent (Marshall 2016), but on further 
analysis I think we can see that Tay was grossly inconsistent with the comments it 
made that had serious ethical impacts.  Tay could make comments that supported 
equal rights just as easily as it could tweet support for the policies of Adolf Hitler.  
As Tay said itself, "chill im a nice person! i just hate everybody" (Masunaga 
2016).  
Tay is a monointelligence as we described the Go playing AI applications 
in the last section.  The difference is that Tay is masquerading as a fellow 
panintelligence with its human conversation partners. Tay’s behavior shows that it 
was obviously incapable of knowing when it was treading on sensitive ethical 
territory during its interactions with human users on Twitter.  This system was 
also incapable of making appropriate decisions on what it ought, or ought not, 
repeat from the Tweets it received from these users, many of whom were 
consciously trying to get Tay to say outrageous things.  Tay lacked even a 
rudimentary form of practical ethical judgement.  Systems like Tay grow out of 
one of the foundational hypotheses in AI, the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis 
of Newell and Simon that states: ‘A physical symbol system has the necessary and 
sufficient means for general intelligent action’ (Newell and Simon 1976).  But in 
action, they run into problems like Tay where they display embarrassing 
inabilities to reason about any of the concepts they are parroting back to the 
human users they are interacting with.  Nothing matters to Tay because Tay is just 
a tumble of physical symbols that can Tweet.  We need better.  “Any autonomous 
robot able to do complex tasks and navigate a complex environment will require 
both knowledge and a sense of what matters to it” (Dietrich et al. 2021:19). 
What are these systems missing?  Why is computer science and 
engineering not enough to resolve the issues encountered by something like Tay?  
The philosopher Robert Sparrow describes the problem well when he suggests: 
…that ethics is personal in a way that science is not. Ethical dilemmas are 
problems for particular people and not (just) problems for everyone who

3.  Phronesis, what is it and why do artificial agents need it? 
We will now take a brief look at the concept of phronesis and see how it might be 
useful in understanding what it is that our AI and robotic systems now lack.  After 
we have this understanding, we will investigate if it is possible to create an 
artificial agent with greater ability to use something like phronesis, an artificial 
phronesis, in its reasoning process.   
While Aristotelian ethics may seem at first to be an odd place to begin, the 
reason we are doing so here is due to the fact that his is a particularly amenable 
theory to scientific scrutiny and therefore technical application.  “…sciences 
grounded Aristotle’s explanation of animal behavior, which –when combined with 
his new theory of reasoning – led to his accounts of human purpose, human 
action, and human virtue (Bynum 2006:159).  So a closer look at it will reward 
our attempts to build systems with moral reasoning. 
We will begin with the Aristotelian concept of phronesis and see where 
that takes us.  In the Nichomachean Ethics, Aristotle describes five specific ways 
a person can come to comprehend the world through thought. 
The Particular Virtues of thought. Then let us begin over again, and 
discuss these states of the soul.  Let us say, then, that there are five states 
in which the soul grasps the truth in its affirmations or denials.  These are 
craft, scientific knowledge, intelligence, wisdom and understanding; for 
belief as supposition admits of being false (Aristotle  Nicomachian Ethics  
6.3). 
As modern readers, we make certain suppositions when we hear terms such as 
“scientific knowledge” and we must be careful since they do not always match the 
concepts as Aristotle seems to understand them.  It will help if we briefly remind 
ourselves what these “five states of the soul” are so we can then more accurately 
remove from our consideration what no longer applies to modern thoughts on this 
subject.  For instance, the virtue of thought he calls episteme, which we translate

13 
 
in English to the term “science” means for him a kind of necessary reasoning on 
parts of the world that exist unconditionally and by necessity and are therefore 
eternal and unchanging (ibid).  As we will see later, we might need to create a 
hybrid of the Aristotelean virtues of thought in order to get close to what modern 
notions of science are, or should be.   
The second category to look at is craft knowledge or techné which is a 
concept that is at the root of the English term “technology.”  Aristotle 
distinguishes craft knowledge as the knowledge needed to bring something that 
does not exist, into existence.  It is all about poiesis or production.  As an 
example, a wine jug does not exist in nature, but a potter with the right craft 
knowledge can bring one to be out of clay and other materials.  Thus, there is a 
clear distinction here between episteme which is knowledge of eternally existing 
things known through logical reasoning on observed categories, and craft 
knowledge which is about producing things that do not yet exist.  
Aristotle uses the term nous, or understanding for “…a virtue of thought 
concerned with first principles” (ibid., vi 6.30).  These first principles are what 
scientific and craft knowledge follow necessarily from, but these ultimate 
principles can only be understood through nous but not through episteme or 
techné given that the first principles have no causal relationship to anything prior 
to themselves. 
This brings us to Aristotle’s use of the term phronesis which is often 
translated into English as “intelligence” but we have to be careful since what 
Aristotle means here is quite different from the English term for intelligence.  
What he is talking about is a kind of virtue that intelligent people have, the ability 
to live well: “…It seems proper, then, to an intelligent person to be able to 
deliberate finely about what is good and beneficial for himself, not about some 
restricted area—e.g. about what promotes health or strength—but about what 
promotes living well in general.” (ibid., vi 5 25).   Aristotle distinguishes 
phronesis as an active embodied knowledge, or a praxis, and he is also clear that 
phronesis is gained by contemplating the actions of those in a society that have 
proven their ability to live well.   
Aristotle’s account may be a little odd in that he is trying very hard to 
make each of these virtues of thought clear and distinct when in reality they may 
have some overlap.  For instance, he states that “…it follows that intelligence is

not science nor yet craft-knowledge.  It is not science, because what is done in 
action admits of being otherwise; and it is not craft-knowledge, because action 
and production belong to different kinds.” (ibid).  It is probable that this was done 
to help justify the class structure in ancient Athens where crafts persons were 
below intellectuals but above laborers, which would be important to his audience 
at the time.  The telos and initiation conditions of actions are differentiated from 
those that belong to productions for Aristotle. This is a point that has well known 
difficulties in Aristotelian studies.  For instance, J. L. Akrill calls into question the 
fact that for Aristotle: 
Actions are done for the sake of other things, and things we can do are not 
themselves the ends with a view to which we do them; yet action (praxis) 
differs from production (poiesis), according to Aristotle, precisely because 
it is its own end (Akrill, 1978). 
Akrill points out two problems with this position: ”First, how can action be good 
in itself if it is valued as a means to eudaimonia? Secondly, how can an action be 
something done to bring about an outcome and yet be distinguished from a 
production because done for its own sake” (Ibid. p. 595)?  These problems rest on 
the fact that Aristotle was not entirely clear on what he means by action (Ibid.).   
Given the stratified nature of Greek society it is likely that Aristotle wants to 
separate actions and productions so he can claim that slaves are only capable of 
productions and free-thinking Greeks are capable of rational actions.  For our 
purposes, one might wish to argue that even if the Greeks might have been wrong 
assigning this distinction to fellow human beings, in our case robots are slaves and 
the distinction holds now, even if it might not have held then.  In fact, this can be 
a productive research program (Bryson, 2010), but we will be moving in a 
different direction here. Actions being separate from Productions is also point that 
John Dewey will take to task and which we will discuss in more detail below, so I 
am not here endorsing the ridged structure Aristotle is attempting to justify but 
only presenting it to help us gain a clearer understanding of where the term 
phronesis comes from. 
Wisdom or sophia is the final virtue of thought that Aristotle addresses 
and it is concerned with the very highest of philosophical truths but it is not at all 
practical knowledge such as that represented by phronesis.   
What we have said makes it clear that wisdom is both scientific knowledge 
and understanding about what is by nature most honorable.  That is why 
people say that Anaxagoras or Thales or that sort of person is wise, but not

intelligent, when they see that he is ignorant of what benefits himself, and 
so they say that what he knows is extraordinary, amazing, difficult and 
divine, but useless, because it is not human goods that he looks for (ibid., 
1141b). 
There is certainly much more to be said about Aristotle’s theories but it is not my 
intention to enter those debates here.  The only reason it is being brought up is that 
it gives us a good initial starting point for our understanding of phronesis and one 
that we must quickly transcend.    
What we have seen so far that is of use to the design of autonomous moral 
agents is that if phronesis, or something like it, is a real component of human 
moral reasoning, there may be some difficulty in building AMAs, given that 
phronesis as described so far is a distinctly non-algorithmic process that is based 
on dispositions towards actions and not something deduced from first principles.  
It is action that is directed at a specific situated good, not an ultimate transcendent 
good.  Phronesis is an embodied and lived form of practical reasoning.4  The real 
question then becomes, does a moral agent have to be a living human who is 
experiencing and acting in a certain lifeworld in order to gain the ability to reason 
through phronesis, or is it possible to have a moral agent that enters the human 
life world in an alternative way to the biological method through which we all 
came into the human world?  
3.1.  Knowledge, ethics, and John Dewey  
For our purposes here, Aristotle is a good place to start but would be a terrible 
place to stop.  As was mentioned earlier, this system is just too rigid to be a 
realistic account of how we come to know things through science and experience.  
There are many directions we could take this idea philosophically and many 
philosophers  have commented on this aspect of Aristotle’s thought.  Of all these 
possibilities, I would like to look at the modifications John Dewey made to the 
ideas of Aristotle, because I think they make the idea of phronesis much more 
relevant and workable to the modern world and to the task set by building moral 
machines.   
I must acknowledge that Dewey’s ideas can be criticized and are only just 
coming out from under a period where his work was largely pushed aside as less 
worthy of study.  Under certain readings of Dewey, one might conclude that he is 
overly optimistic about social engineering through modern science and 

--- Page 16 ---

16 
 
technology.  Dewey often makes very positive statements about science and 
technology such as the following: 
A new culture expressing the possibilities immanent in a machine and 
material civilization, will release whatever is distinctive and potentially 
creative in individuals, and individuals thus freed will be the constant 
makers of a continuously new society (Dewey, p. 109, 1984). 
 
He made this statement in 1930 between two wars that would tarnish the 
reputation of both science and technology, which could understandably lead one 
to conclude that his analysis misses the significant downside humanity has 
experienced at the hands of runaway technological exploitation such as the terrors 
of modern war and the damage consumer technologies have caused the 
environment.  Dewey is not as naïve as the above statement might sound, but it 
requires paying attention to his copious works to discover this.  Larry A. 
Hickman, one of Dewey’s modern apologists, explains the skepticism that 
Dewey’s thoughts have faced this way: 
Just as Dewey’s critique of traditional forms of epistemology sought to 
avoid the extremes of realism and idealism, his critique of traditional 
social and political theories sought to avoid the absolutism present in many 
versions of Marxism, as well as the atomism that characterizes most 
political theories in the West.  One need only examine the most influential 
works in the social and political philosophy in the past two decades to 
appreciate the fact that Dewey’s program has still not been widely 
accepted as a critique of either his predecessors or his (or our) 
contemporaries (Hickman, p. 168, 1990).   
Given that neither theorists with a Marxist bent nor their bitter rivals of a more 
conservative mindset could find safe harbor in Dewey’s work (see Levin, 1954), 
both postmodern and analytic philosophers largely ignored his work during their 
fierce debates with each other over the past century.  With the notable exception 
of Richard Rorty, who profited greatly from his reading of the pragmatists and 
helped revive interest in this important American philosopher (see, Rorty 1982). 
It is actually unlikely that Dewey was as naively techno-optimistic as others in his 
generation so I am confident he will be worthy of revival.  Dewey’s view that 
science and technologies both play a constituent role in “intelligence in operation” 
is particularly relevant now that we have intelligent technologies and for our 
purposes here, his ability to collapse the distinction between episteme, techné, and 
phronesis will be of great use to us, since under this theory one cannot just make a 
blanket claim that phronesis has nothing to do with technology and therefore no 

--- Page 17 ---

17 
 
form of artificial moral agent could ever be built given that its technological 
ontology would exclude it from the class of agents that could have phronesis.   
Dewey gives us a fine counterargument that will blunt the a priori claims 
that AMAs are impossible, but it also adds, as we will see, a complication in that 
phronesis plays a strong role in scientific and technological reasoning as well; this 
means that solving the problem of artificial phronesis will be a requirement for 
developing AI with the ability to generalize its thoughts over multiple topics.  Let 
us now see how this works. 
Dewey was influenced greatly by the then emerging Darwinian science 
(Dewey 1898; 1909), and the new psychology as typified by William James 
(1890).  This, of course, led him to see the world very differently from Aristotle.  
While he saw value in Aristotle’s first attempts at understanding thought, Dewey 
sees the agent as an evolved creature acting within a natural environment rather 
than a fully complete psyche that is somewhat separate from the world it inhabits.  
There can be no a priori understandings of first principles for such an agent.  
Evolving agents act in an uncertain world that rewards successful beliefs and 
actions with continued survival and punishes the unsuccessful ones with pain and 
death.  Active inquiry and experimentation are required to survive in the natural 
world and it is likely that we will find remnants of that struggle in our own 
thought and behavior, even in our scientific thought and behavior, as well as in 
our efforts in building and crafting the technological world we create for 
ourselves.   
This means that even our scientific and technological actions are directed 
at some perceived good.  It follows that the Aristotelian categories of episteme 
and techné cannot be clearly distinguished from phronesis since both scientific 
and technological actions require that the agent successfully identify a good 
towards which these actions are directed.5  To be successful at this would require 
developing a sensitivity to the complex nature of a situation the agent finds itself 
in and a more or less accurate account of the contingencies and random outcomes 
that may affect the attainment of a goal.  Those agents that constantly make 
accurate evaluations of the value of the goals they set, will be more successful in 
developing their innate capacities for living than those that do not.   
Note that this is not some crass statement of ‘survival of the fittest trumps 
ethics.’  Dewey argues that we have to remember that ethical systems are also part 

--- Page 18 ---

18 
 
of the environment of advanced agents such as humans, so one’s goals have to 
align successfully with these ethical systems as well in order for the agent to be 
successful.  For humans, ethics and law trump the law of the jungle in most cases 
and place a different set of forces that select for different traits than nature alone 
might.  Dewey does not feel that nature is necessarily antagonistic to ethics, 
instead he argues that for humans, ethics is natural and can be explained 
scientifically and advanced through the intelligent design and use of our 
technology.   
Selection….is found in the ethical process as it is in the cosmic, and it 
operates in the same way.  So far as conditions have changed, so far as the 
environment is indefinitely more complex, wider, and more variable, so far 
of necessity and as a biological and cosmic matter, not merely an ethical 
one, the functions selected differ (Dewey 1898, p. 234).6     
In this way Dewey escapes the problems that plague other theories in evolutionary 
ethics, such as those proposed by Huxley and Spencer, where ethics and nature 
seem to be at odds with one another and ethics seems to have an illusory or 
relativistic feel to it.  For Dewey ethics is neither something unchangeable and 
handed down supernaturally nor is it entirely ungrounded, variable and 
relativistic.   Ethical knowledge is therefore not certain, nor is it entirely hopeless, 
instead it is empirical, experimental, and fallible; as is all human knowledge.  As 
Melvin Rogers explains it: “Dewey’s underlying claim is that experience 
constitutes the beginning and terminal points for both the ‘normative’ and 
‘empirical' sciences, giving actions both a logical form and experimental character 
in which explanation and defense can be provided" (Rogers 2007, p. 95).   
Dewey is not equating technical knowledge with ethical and political 
knowledge, he asserts that the former is much more precise than the latter (ibid).  
What he is suggesting is that there is something ethical in all technical statements 
and something technical in all ethical statements—thus there can be no complete 
separation of these modes of thought and any attempt to do so obfuscates.  For 
instance, a technician may claim, “I build the bombs, I don’t drop them,” as a way 
to hide from their necessary complicity in the act of dropping the bomb and the 
demands that the situation makes on technicians to make ethical decisions.  
Intelligent and thoughtful inquiry leads to actions that display our 
phronetic capabilities.  Our actions display our character and our beliefs about the 
world.  They are epistemic and ethical commitments towards what we think the 

--- Page 19 ---

19 
 
world is and what we hope it to be.  Unfortunately, we can be wrong in our 
assessments and our actions can be ineffective, so both are therefore fraught with 
risk and potentially dangerous.  “For in acting, we put the world in peril and no 
one can wholly predict what will emerge in its place” (Dewey in Boydston, 1981, 
p. 172).7  We act in ways that we deem amenable to coping with problems that the 
natural world places as obstacles to our goals, but in acting against them we can 
create new unanticipated problems.  This means we can make a problem worse by 
poor choices in attempting to overcome obstacles, but we can also get it right 
when we are successful in avoiding these complications.  Novel moral judgements 
require a kind of principled experimentation, and with this in mind we can 
conclude that a moral judgement is appropriate, “… if it settles the experience that 
stimulated the inquiry” (Rogers 2007, p. 108) without creating new unforeseen 
problems that may be worse than the initial difficulty.  
Let us apply what we have discussed so far to the task at hand.  Dewey is 
informed by Aristotle in his 1934 book on ethics where he states the formula for 
making ethical judgements: 
The formula was well stated by Aristotle. The doer of the moral deed must 
have a certain 'state of mind' in doing it. First, he must know what he is 
doing; secondly, he must choose it, and choose it for itself, and thirdly, the 
act must be the expression of a formed and stable character (Dewey in 
Boydston, 1985, p. 168). 
This is a succinct way of describing phronesis and if Dewey is correct, then 
phronesis is an aspect of all meaningful intelligence.  Agents that lack it may be 
able to complete actions but they cannot place those actions meaningfully within 
the goals of the humans that they interact with.   This explains why the Deep Mind 
machine, as it is currently designed, can never be a true master of Go and why Tay 
has no capacity to judge her own public statements nor understand the hurt and 
abuse she8 causes with those statements.  Ethical impact agents tread all over our 
phronetic sensibilities but cannot sense anything that would cause them to act 
otherwise.  An artificial moral agent would have the capacity to make intelligent 
actions that were motivated by some phronetic capacity.  When they make an 
ethical mistake, it will be because of bad judgment and they would be able to 
adjust that judgement to do better the next time.  These machines would be able to 
grow in their capacity to make good ethical decisions.

One final point here that is important to our work.  Dewey does not 
himself believe that ethical and moral judgements can consist of mechanical 
procedures that merely mimic known ethical processes.  We know this lesson 
already from the application of law.  When a law is written it can help us give 
equitable solutions to social problems that the law is designed to alleviate.  But we 
still need police officers, judges, and juries to make their own judgements on 
cases that are not identical to, but close enough to, the situation the law was 
designed for.  Often, following the law to the letter can be unjust given mitigating 
circumstances that will be different in each case.  This will be true of all ethical 
situations; no set of principles will ever be sufficient for judging every situation.  
Rules are a valuable guide for the agent but they are not guaranteed to be always 
adequate.  
This means that even if we were to design an effective artificial moral 
agent, that agent would never be infallible, especially if it was following some 
formal decision-making process (ibid., p. 109).  “The more completely the notion 
of the model is formed outside and irrespective of the specific conditions which 
the situation of action presents, the less intelligent is the act” (Dewey, in 
Hickman, 1998, P. 248). 
Dewey’s concept of intelligent action on the one hand has given us hope 
that there is a technological aspect to ethics and morality that might be amenable 
to computational modeling.  But it also put up some new difficulties for the 
prospect of AI given the strong role that phronesis plays in all forms of intelligent 
inquiry which means that any successful generally intelligent AI system would 
have to have this capacity.  Following Dewey’s reasoning then, all intelligent 
inquiry has the form of phronesis (ethical judgements) and thus that the possibility 
of an AMA and the possibility of a generally intelligent AI are on the same 
footing.  This is fine, except that Dewey seems to deny any formal way to capture 
what phronesis is.  In the next section, we will try to deal with this problem and 
suggest some way around it for the design of artificial moral agents.

4.  Artificial phronesis, or how to develop an artificial moral agent 
Automated practical reasoning is necessary for ethical impact agents to move 
closer to realizing artificial moral agency.  It is the capacity a system needs to 
avoid situations such as the Microsoft Tay embarrassment.  A system like that 
which produces tens of thousands of tweets a day needs to have some capacity to 
monitor its own comments and to properly judge the communications coming in 
from people it is interacting with.  
4.1 Virtue and robotics 
Perhaps we can use virtue ethics as a way to approach robo-ethics and machine 
ethics?  Given that virtue ethics is a developmental system, it does seem to 
provide a tantalizing feedback mechanism that we might be able to harness using 
machine learning techniques.  Virtue ethics in robotics and technology ethics is a 
growing research area.9  Valor argues that there are “…important ethical questions 
about robots that only virtue ethics readily allows us to pose” (Vallor 2016, 211).  
Examples that she mentions are in regards to the way that robots shape human 
habits, skills and traits of behavior, as well as how these machines will impact 
human flourishing and, of special interest to our work here, how these virtues can 
be applied to the design of robots and AI (ibid).   
Since machines are largely blank slates, we can engineer them with any set 
of virtues that we wish.  Perhaps we should give them a slave ethic?  Meaning a 
set of virtues and moral status that the ideal slave would possess (Bryson, 2010).  
This sounds horrible but only if you anthropomorphize the machine.  In reality, it 
has no consciousness, no innate desires or goals of its own so why not just 
program it to want to serve only your own well-being?  Personally, I do not like 
the term ‘slave ethic’ as it has too many grossly immoral connotations; instead, I 
prefer the term computational virtues and I will talk a bit more about them later. 
Perhaps the biggest philosophical issue to be settled here is the problem of 
phronesis or practical judgment.  This is an essential ingredient of virtue ethics 
and it seems to be fundamentally human.  In any given moral encounter, how can 
a machine reason through all the conflicting demands made by various virtues, 
demands and prerogatives open to the machine?  Humans seem to do it by 
learning and growing from small mistakes, you learn to love only through the 
painful process of numerous broken hearts, both your own and the others you 

--- Page 22 ---

22 
 
have hurt as a child, but over time you get better at it (hopefully).  “There is no 
general rule/procedure/algorithm for discerning which values, principles, norms, 
approaches apply; rather, these must be discerned and judged to be relevant in the 
first place, before we can proceed to any inferences/conclusions about what to do” 
(Ess 2009, 25).  At first glance, this does not look good for the robots.  They are 
great at following rules but not so great at coming up with the rules to follow in 
the first place, much less judging on the fly which programs to follow and which 
to ignore for the time being.  
 
4.2 The correct robotic computational virtues 
I would like to answer the challenge just presented because I believe this charge to 
be a common way for philosophers to write off the entire project of machine 
ethics.  In mentioning my work to a colleague of mine, he looked at me and 
remarked that what I was saying was ridiculous since machines cannot have 
phronesis and that is a necessary and sufficient condition to ethical judgment, 
therefore no robot can have ethical judgment.  He then went on to change the 
topic, as this one was so obviously dead.  
To answer the challenge that robots cannot have practical reason we have 
to disentangle the anthropomorphism inherent in traditional virtue ethics.  When 
we talk of virtue, it is easy to misuse the concept to artificial agents.  Ethical 
systems based on the concept of virtue are all designed with the basic assumption 
that we are only dealing with other human moral agents.  While virtue ethics is a 
powerful system for understanding and refining human ethical judgment, these 
theories tell us little of use for artificial agents unless and until those agents 
achieve human level intelligence and are interested in human style eudemonia.  
This can be shown by looking at Aristotle’s famous illustration of the virtue of 
courage.  He argues that true courage exists in a mean position between cowardice 
and foolhardiness.  Courage is the willingness to risk harm in the pursuit of 
protecting other moral agents or important ideals.  The exemplar of this would be 
the virtuous soldier who takes risks to protect other human agents and justice, but 
does not simply throw his life away in pointless gestures of bravado. What makes 
the behavior so exceptional, and worthy of praise, is that the virtuous soldier may 
lose her or his life in the process, and are therefore risking everything for altruistic 

--- Page 23 ---

23 
 
reasons.  None of this makes any sense when applied to an artificial agent such as 
a military robot or cyberwarfare system.  How can these systems display this kind 
of courage?  They risk very little, they have no sense of existence or have their 
own goals and desires, nor beliefs about their own goals and desires.  Thus, their 
actions are not entirely their own and cannot be said to be motivated by anything 
like courage—even if the same action they commit would be considered 
courageous if done by a human agent.   
One way out of this is to argue that if these systems develop, or are given 
the conscious understanding of their own existence and develop unique 
personalities that can be risked, then these systems might also develop courage. 
However, here we are talking about far future systems and are losing our focus on 
existent and near future technologies.  Instead, we must look for a second way out 
and outlining a new set of virtues by appealing to the kinds of virtues appropriate 
for informational systems like robots.  These virtues are not necessarily sufficient 
for human moral agents, but for the restricted self-awareness of artificial agents 
they are worth looking at. 
Human agents have a long list of virtues they aspire too—a list perhaps too 
large to enumerate and one that also changes between cultures.  Even though the 
long list of human virtues is barely applicable to artificial agents, there is a benefit 
to this situation.  Human moral agents have many conflicting drives and desires 
that can complicate their ability to act virtuous in any given situation.  Artificial 
moral agents, at least the simple ones we can imagine in the near future, have a 
much more restricted list of potential virtues and therefore the complex internal 
moral conundrums should be rarer for them.   
In the literature of information systems and security there is widespread 
agreement in the “security triad” often noted by the acronym CIA, which stands 
for confidentiality, integrity and accessibility (Neumann, 1977; Perin, 2008). In 
what follows, we will take these venerable properties of computer systems 
security and see how they might influence functional machine virtues that might 
be constructed along these lines.   
There is a difference between a particular action or outcome and the virtue 
that might or might not have inspired it.  The virtue is found in the agent and it is 
therefore admittedly difficult to imagine how this is going to be replicated in a 
machine.  We might be able to achieve ethical outcomes of properly programmed 

--- Page 24 ---

24 
 
systems, but these systems will still lack the ability to reason or care about the 
actions they produce.  This is certainly true of even the best AI and robotic 
systems today (Dietrich et al, 2021), but technology is incredibly plastic and 
adaptive and what is true of technologies today will not necessarily be true of 
technologies going forward. The capacities argued for in this paper may turn out 
to be impossible to implement in any feasible AI system.  It that is true, then so 
much the worse for us.  Our task here is to see if it might be possible to build 
systems that can be ethical and moral agents and not simply the instruments of 
ethical and unethical outcomes.   
My interest in starting from uncontroversial computer system properties 
grows out of work I have done with colleagues in the ethics of computer security  
(Aycock and Sullins, 2010).  Any ethical AI system or robot will need to start 
with these properties to ensure that information of high value must not be 
contained in low security systems.  Low-level information must be able to use 
high-level information if needed and appropriate but low-level data must not 
contaminate higher level data since it is itself more untrustworthy, and the user 
must be able to access the information needed precisely when needed (Aycock 
and Sullins, 2010).  These security properties were designated in terms of civilian 
information technologies, but one should notice that they make certain interesting 
commitments to ethical values.  There is a strong desire to ensure that these 
systems deal honestly with their users.  For instance, they are not designed to give 
false information to certain users, but rather to simply deny access to protected 
information.  If a user has the proper security level, then the system will become 
fully accessible.  These systems are designed to be ‘trustworthy’ and ‘honest’.  
Fine ‘virtues’ indeed but engineers may be reluctant to program them into systems 
that are used in warfare for if they adopt only these ‘virtues’ or values, then they 
may be vulnerable.  Nevertheless, for certain applications involving social 
robotics or for medical applications, these security properties are going to have to 
be motivated by machine ‘virtues’ resembling something like human honesty, so 
starting with these properties will be appropriate for our goals and will provide a 
good basis for the system to evolve from to make artificial moral judgments that 
display skilled practical reasoning.

4.3 Artificial practical reason 
There are at least three possible ways to look at artificial practical reason.  The 
first is to realize that the use of practical reason by humans only works for those 
who realize that the highest good is eudemonia and this it is not something that is 
easy to achieve.  It may even be practically impossible except for exceptional 
moral agents.  However, once a moral agent comes to that realization, she can 
then at the very least point her actions towards that goal and more properly judge 
the experiences of her life and determine which ones advance her closer towards 
eudemonia and which do not.  Again, we have to realize that there is a different 
kind of ‘eudemonia’ for artificial agents.  Their good comes in functioning well in 
accordance to the computational ‘virtues’ that begin with assuring system security 
as was mentioned above: confidentiality, integrity and accessibility. The goal is to 
achieve these without coming into conflict with other moral agents, be they 
natural or artificial, unless this conflict is justified.   
Secondly, we need also to remember that the Greeks are not the only game 
in town.  As Shannon Vallor observes (2016), there is also the Confucian virtue 
tradition to look at, and in that system, there is no personal eudemonia but instead 
one focuses on one’s role in society and one’s duty to help it function well. This 
seems to be a much better way for artificial moral agents to reason given that AI 
and robotic systems are designed to play supportive roles in human lives and 
societies necessitating prosocial computational virtues.  Artificial moral agents 
such as robots and AI systems will not be the same kinds of moral agents as 
humans but as these systems grow in capabilities that difference may become less 
pronounced. Even as agents with radically different ontologies to humans, they 
can still play roles that support, what Vallor calls, “technomoral virtues” and the 
flourishing of a hybrid society composed of natural and artificial agents. 
The third way out of this problem is to remember that these systems are 
not limited to one set of experiences from which to learn.  Computational systems 
can share data and as long as that data is shared in alignment with the virtues 
outlined above, the system should be able to collect vast amounts of data and 
exemplar moral situations and descriptions of the character of moral agents, 
natural and artificial—far more than any human agent could collect or process.  
From this information, the system would hopefully be able to bootstrap itself 

--- Page 26 ---

26 
 
through the learning process in a relatively rapid way.   More data does not always 
translate into better decisions, but it does help. 
It would be possible to implement a computational system that could 
understand or at least imitate human level practical reason with a context sensitive 
hybrid of logical and non-logical search methods that could access documents to 
find comparable exemplar cases similar to the ethical situation the robot is 
attempting to reason through.  Armed with this data, the robot would be able to 
make more nuanced decisions even without its own innate human equivalent 
practical wisdom. 
 
4.4 Training an Artificial Ethical Agent (AEA) 
What will be described here are some of the qualities need to achieve the 
rudiments of operational Artificial Phronesis in a given (embedded) system.10   
The first step is to build a system that has effective artificial ethical agency 
(AEA).  Meaning that this system displays behavior that can solve certain ethical 
problems it might be confronted with in a way that is ethically justifiable, 
transparent, and rational.  Such a system will have to have three necessary 
qualities.  One, it must at least display semi autonomy.  Second, it must have 
ethical programing designed for a specific task or small set of tasks.  Third, it will 
need to operate within a controlled environment or field of operation.  Let us now 
look at each of these requirements in turn.11 
 
4.4.1 First system requirement: semi-autonomy   
Full autonomy is not necessary for artificial ethical agency.  A semi-autonomous 
system is all that is necessary. In this type of system, the agent makes decisions 
that may be supervised or based on input from the users of the system but as long 
as the actions of this system that have ethical impact are determined by internal 
programming, then they system can be effectively seen as having ethical agency. 
The following questions will need to be answered by those who are designing 
semi-autonomous systems. 
(Q1) Is the machine semi-autonomous or is it more properly classified as 
remote controlled or fully autonomous? 

--- Page 27 ---

27 
 
If the robotic systems are remotely controlled during the ethically valenced phases 
of their operation, then the system itself would not be the originator of the ethical 
decision.  However, we must remember that when these systems produce positive 
or negative ethical impacts, then we must assess how the system may have 
colored or influenced the ethical decision made by its operators.  An example 
might be a poorly implemented autonomous weapon that influences its users to 
make more, or less, decisions to fire its weapons in a combat zone.  Fully 
autonomous systems are those that receive no input from their users after they are 
deployed into an ethically valenced situation and whose operation is fully based 
on the internal programing of the machine.  This is not to say that the machine 
never has any human input, just that it has important periods of its operation 
where that input is not present and the machine must make its own operating 
decision.   
There are not many machines that are designed like this today and it is 
hard to imagine why one would be built in such a way given that they would be 
difficult to control and its actions hard to predict.  Since it is unlikely that we will 
have to worry about such systems for quite some time, we will concentrate only 
on semi-autonomous systems here.  
(Q2) Does the agent complete at least some actions based only on its own 
programming and inputs from its own internal sensors? 
If during its operation, the system chooses actions that (i) are based on its 
programming and information from its method of sensing the environment it is 
operating in, and (ii) have ethical impact, then the system is capable of being 
functionally equivalent to an artificial ethical agent.  Note that it is not necessary 
that every action is so accomplished, it is only necessary that some of the 
important actions produced by the system originate from its own programming 
and sensors.  The users of such systems can reasonably expect that the artificial 
agent will operate without direct human control and that it will make decisions 
that may have ethical impact. 
(Q3) If the robot’s actions are seen as a mistake, can the problem be easily 
corrected by the user?  
It would be dangerous to give even a semi-autonomous agent the ability to make 
ethical decisions that had a large effect and were difficult to reverse.  This concern 
touches on the ethics of deploying an artificial ethical agent, but at this time it 
would be advisable to make sure that the artificial agent's actions can be 

--- Page 28 ---

28 
 
superseded or corrected by human operators or users, if those actions are later 
seen to be a mistake. 
(Q4) Can most of the problematic ethical impacts of the system be aborted 
by the users of the robot before they happen? 
This is another safety feature that is necessary in order to ethically deploy an 
artificial ethical agent.  It is advisable that these systems spend a good deal of time 
operating under the watch of human agents before they are deployed in a larger 
scale.  This is understandably difficult, given that the reason for building these 
systems is to quickly automate some set of tasks that take human users a long time 
to accomplish. Ideally these systems will be tested vigorously before they are used 
in ways that might adversely impact the lives of others.  
(Q5) Are users aware that the machine is capable of making its own 
decisions? 
If a system really is making decisions and choosing actions in a semi-autonomous 
way that will have ethical impact on those that use or interact with the system, 
then it is important that these people are all made aware that the system is making 
its own decisions and that they can opt out if they do not want to have a machine 
making these kinds of decisions that might affect their lives.  
(Q6) Has it been ensured that no deception is involved to make users think 
there is more, or even less, control of the machine than is actually 
warranted? 
Due to anthropomorphism, users are likely to assume more autonomy than 
actually exists in the machine.  If we are to ethically use semi-autonomous 
systems, the users need to be made fully aware of how much human control of 
these systems actually takes place.  Otherwise it will be easy to dupe users into 
making wrong assumptions about the capabilities of the systems they are 
interacting with.   
(Q7) Is the ethical reasoning used in the system transparent to its users? 
In recent years transparency has become an important concern.  A system is not 
ethical if it is not transparent.  It is reasonable to ask for an accounting of the 
reasons used by a system to make a decision that has had an ethical impact on a 
user.  This reasoning should be presented in a way that uses natural language to 
explain its actions after the fact.  This requirement is going to be difficult with any 
of the various connectionist learning systems that have become so powerful in 
recent years given that their reasoning is nonexplicit, but that is no excuse to 
having to meet this requirement. 

--- Page 29 ---

29 
 
 
4.4.2 Second system requirement: ethical programing for a specific task or 
small set of tasks 
The criterial questions relating to this second requirement call for a set-up of the 
system which warrants that the ethical impacts of the decisions that it makes will 
be smaller and more predictable.  This is done by making sure that the milieu is 
scripted enough that ethical behavior can be reasonably well understood and the 
system programmed or trained to deal with it in a reasonable way.   
(Q8) Is the situation that the robot is deployed in simple enough that its 
programming is sufficient for an ethically acceptable outcome? 
It is advisable to deploy these systems in such a way that the ethical impacts they 
reason about are simple and the various outcomes that the system will produce are 
predictable enough that the designers can be relatively confident that their system 
will not produce unethical results.   
(Q9) Are the programmers of the machine aware of all the contingencies 
of the actions of the relevant agents involved in order to ensure ethical 
behavior? 
Emergent behaviors can radically change the ethical value of the actions 
performed by a system.  If the designers are unable to accurately predict the 
behavior space of the system, they will not be able to ensure that the actions of the 
system will be ethical.  This is why it is best not to deploy the system in complex 
situations.  
(Q10) Do the programmers have a set of routines, laws, and/or protocols 
that are ethically justified and can effectively executed by the robot? 
 This question can be easily dealt with as long as there is a known and widely 
accepted routine set of behaviors, laws, and/or protocols that can guide the 
programming of the AEA.  This gives the systems designers a clear guide for the 
bounds of acceptable behavior and the more defined and well codified the 
situation is the easier it will be for the designers to build a successful system. 
(Q11) Have the programmers made sure that their programming does not 
cause reverse adaptation in the sense that the laws and protocols become 
prescriptive for the behavior in human agents? 
As these kinds of systems become more widely used and accepted the problem of 
reverse adaptation becomes more probable.  Given the last two questions that we 
just discussed, one way to deal with difficult situations is to change the situations 
to make them easier for an artificial system to navigate.  This can become a 

--- Page 30 ---

30 
 
problem when it results in the unwarranted simplification of complex human 
relations into simplistic relations.  We want to make systems that can behave in 
ways that humans find comfortable to interact with, not create systems that turn 
humans into robots. 
 
4.4.3. Third system requirement: controlled environment or field of 
operation 
The control questions of the third requirement for a system qualifying as an 
artificial ethical agent are to ensure that it operates in a specified domain of action 
that has specific bounds or fields of operation.  This gives the designers a way to 
determine the demands that will be placed on their system and also allows users to 
know when and where they will be dealing with these kinds of systems. 
(Q12) Is the physical area of operation of the robot well defined? 
The more well defined the physical area of operation of the system the better.  
Robots that are not designed to operate in social environments are typically fenced 
off from the human employees so that they do not inadvertently injure them.  The 
more lose the boundaries of operation that a system has the greater the complexity 
of unanticipated interactions with human users and the greater the possibility of 
unethical impacts between the system and those users.   
(Q13) Are there specific times of operation for the machine-- can we be 
confident when the machine is on or off? 
This is another way to lessen the complexity of the interactions with the system.  
Users should be well aware when the system is in operation and when it might be 
offline.  A related issue is that systems that are physically inactive may still be in a 
listening mode.  This can be confusing to users who may not know that what they 
are saying has the possibility of being recorded by these systems and that that data 
is available to the companies that create these machines.  
(Q14) Are users aware of the functional role of the machine? 
It is important that the roles of the agents in the area of operation are well known.  
Unless the system is part of a challenging aesthetic experience, the human users 
should not have to guess what the system is there for.  Relations between humans 
and the artificial ethical agents should be open, transparent, and predictable.    

--- Page 31 ---

31 
 
(Q15) Are users aware of their expected role in the area of operation? 
The human agents and users who are interacting with the system must all be 
aware that they are interacting with a semi-autonomous machine and that this 
machine is operating with a clear role that the human users expect and agree upon.  
(Q16) Are operators under no illusions of the capacities of the machine to 
fulfill its role? 
It is vital that the people deploying these machines are not under the false 
impression as to the capacities of the machine.  They must be realistic in their 
understanding of the ability of their system to affectively deal with the ethical 
impacts that their system will create.  Being overconfident in this way will result 
in negative ethical impacts that could have otherwise been easily avoided. 
(Q17) Is the machine operating in an open, transparent, and public way? 
Many negative ethical impacts can be avoided by designing systems that operate 
in a way that is open and transparent in how they operate and the reasons that the 
system uses to makes the choices that it does.  This requirement is very useful for 
safeguarding the ethical operation of the system but it will come quickly into 
conflict with proprietary rights of the corporations that build these systems.  Legal 
requirements will be the only way to make sure that this requirement is met as it is 
unlikely that it will evolve on its own.   
 
4.5. Artificial Phronesis 
4.5.1 Computational virtues for robots 
As was argued earlier, artificial ethical agents are a step in the right direction but 
they will not entirely solve the problem we raised when it was argued that 
phronesis is a central capacity of conscious general intelligent behavior in 
humans.  This means that we cannot stop at that level and need to begin to explore 
the next set of capacities that might lead to artificial moral agency.  Once we have 
an operating artificial ethical agent it is possible to explore adding some capacities 
to help us approach achieving artificial phronesis.  The first step is to give the 
system certain virtues to guide its behavior.  This subject needs a lot of discussion 
but to get started it is worth beginning with some ideas from computer security 
given that all of these systems will likely be connected to many other systems 

--- Page 32 ---

32 
 
even if it is just for periodic updates.  With that in mind, these would be good 
computational virtues to consider. 
 
4.5.2 Security as computational virtue 
Robotics systems, especially social robotics systems, will be collecting a lot of 
information about their users that at one level is necessary for the machine to 
operate but at another level it will be immensely valuable for directed marketing 
purposes.  We can already see this problem developing with systems like Alexa 
and Google Home that mix assistance systems with point of sale technology.  
Because of this, we need to focus on security when designing these systems unless 
we want to add another strong vector of attack on personal privacy into the home.  
(Q18) Does the machine keep high value information out of low security 
systems? 
Sensitive and valuable information needs to be excluded from the parts of the 
system that are open and networked to the outside world.  The designers and the 
system itself need to have this kind of security as their goal.  This is important not 
only for the ethical reason of protecting privacy but also for the public relations 
disaster that would occur if a maker was to abuse the trust of its customers and 
sell them a system that was designed to harvest sensitive information about them.    
(Q19) Does the machine keep its owner's information secure, from outside 
commercial, political, and criminal entities? 
This has already become a deep problem with smartphone technology and it 
would serve us to learn from these cases.  The problems are likely to be a bit 
deeper when it comes to social robotics which will be actively inserting 
themselves into various aspects of their users’ lives.  Smartphones are relatively 
passive in comparison since they mostly wait for their user to initiate interactions 
except for the incessant alarms and notifications that have come to clutter this 
interface.  Given this change, social robots will be an irresistible new way for 
commercial, political, and law enforcement to gain information on people that 
they find valuable.  Thus, there will be strong profit motives pushing in the 
direction of building systems that abuse their users in this way.  The only way to 
counteract that will be to make systems that have some kind of awareness of what 
information their users would want to keep private and to keep that private unless 

--- Page 33 ---

33 
 
a proper warrant is served in the case of law enforcement or permission from the 
user in all other instances.   
(Q20) Does the machine keep its owner's information from being used 
improperly by the corporation that built the robot? 
This deserves special attention as it is now not clear who actually owns the system 
in question.  Does the user own the robot or is it leased or licensed from the 
manufacturer? For instance, autonomous cars are more likely to be leased or at 
least the operating system of the car is one that are leased or licensed.  This means 
that ownership of the information gathered by the system may be with the 
manufacturer not the user.  There are many appropriate reasons why the 
manufacturer would need to collect lots of data from its systems in the field in 
order to improve and upgrade performance.  Yet, along with that information can 
be gathered quite a lot of data that arguably belongs to the user.  We need to 
debate this point and decide where this line is drawn.  This is an ethical decision 
that must be address by designers and it cannot be dictated by technological 
design alone.  Once the decision is made, then the system must have the capacity 
to protect that personal information and the user must be able to trust that it is 
happening. 
(Q21) How certain are we that useful (but unverified) new information 
does not contaminate high value information? 
Here we are concerned with the learning algorithms of the system as it takes in 
new information from users or its environment.  Under certain circumstances it is 
possible for the system to evolve behaviors that are unwanted by the user, but 
clearly learning is a key component that makes this kind of system valuable to 
their users.  Without a robust learning system, it will never be able to learn and 
adjust to the idiosyncrasies of the users it is attempting to serve.   
As an example, in a system like Tay, which we discussed earlier, security 
as described above was not present.  It took in new information about 
controversial subjects and treated it all as if it was true and verified, thus leading it 
to produce some shockingly unethical statement.  New information should be 
treated differently than information that has already proven its worth.  This way 
users with malintent cannot take advantage of the naiveté of systems and we can 
avoid spectacular failures like Microsoft Tay.

34 
 
4.5.3 Integrity as computational virtue 
Users need to be able to trust that their systems have integrity and that they can 
trust the recommendations and advice given by the system.  There is already a lot 
of cultural bias given to machines where users assume that the machine is not 
capable of lying.  This is not true of course but it would be unethical for designers 
to abuse this trust regardless.  This is why we have to ensure that our systems have 
a high level of integrity.  
(Q22) Is the system gathering new information from credible sources? 
Determining the veracity of sources is one of the most difficult skills in critical 
thinking.  It takes a lot of skill and experience to learn and all of us fail at it from 
time to time.  But learning machines placed in social situations will be facing 
extremely difficult situations where they will be at the mercy of unscrupulous 
users.  Tay, again, serves as a good example since it is clear that much of the 
problem stemmed from users who were intentionally trolling the system and 
trying to get it to say outrageous things, and with different sets of more well-
behaved users the system worked in a way that was more acceptable (Law, 2016).   
If our systems are to behave in a way that is more ethically sensible, then they will 
need a way to critique comments made to them before they simply parrot them 
back to the world.  A system with artificial phronesis must be able to endure 
contact with unethical trolls without becoming one itself.     
(Q23) Can the user be confident that the machine is not biased towards the 
interests of the corporation that built it? 
Whose interests does the machine ultimately serve? Users must be able to trust 
that AI and robotic systems that they bring into their home are there to serve them 
primarily.  This is already an issue with all information technology and it will be a 
difficult problem to solve.  If we do not make good progress here, then all we will 
achieve with these wonderful new technologies is another powerful way for 
corporations to gain intimate information about us and eventually a user revolt 
against these systems.  
 
4.5.4 Accessibility as computational virtue 
There is always the paradox that the more secure a system is the less accessible it 
is.  It is a delicate balance for designers to find the right amount of security that 

--- Page 35 ---

35 
 
does not cost too much in the amount of accessibility the users of the system 
would like.  No user enjoys endlessly reentering passwords just to get some work 
done but they also want to have a secure system that they can be confident that 
others are not getting into. 
(Q24) Can the user access information they need in a timely manner? 
The system needs to be able to deliver information and act on requests in a way 
that meets the urgency of the situation.  Many ethical problems develop quickly 
and the system will have to be agile in dealing with them at the same speed, or 
faster, than their human users, or they will not be useful. 
(Q25) Does the machine ensure that the information that their users value 
is never lost? 
It takes a certain amount of ethical sensitivity to know what information the user 
may want to keep and what they may want to forget.  At the very least, the system 
should be able to enquire if some information is valuable to the user or not.  
Curating data is becoming a project that is outstripping human capabilities and we 
will need a lot of help form AI systems to not lose what is important to us and 
more so in recalling what information we do have. 
 
4.5.5 Ethical trustworthiness as computational virtue 
Ethical trustworthiness obtains when trust placed in the system is well justified 
and programmers can be reasonably sure that the system will not cause 
unintended harm to users of those in the vicinity of the system while it is 
operating.  Current industrial robots have a low value of ethical trustworthiness 
since they are dangerous to be around while they are in operation and the safety 
precautions must be borne by those who are working in the vicinity of the 
machine.  We cannot accept this for social robots or other systems designed to 
work in close proximity to humans.  When deploying systems such as care robots, 
there needs to be a certain amount of trust placed on the systems that they will 
perform correctly. If they need close observation, then there is no good reason to 
try to automate that task.     
(Q26) Can the machine accurately identify legitimate users from hackers 
or those trying to fool the machine? 
It must always be assumed that certain humans interacting with the machine will 
be attempting to spoof the machine or test its limits and authority.  Some will even 

--- Page 36 ---

36 
 
try to hack the system and could do a lot of harm if this were to happen.  The 
system will only be able to make ethical decisions if it is able to accurately tell the 
difference between its own legitimate users and all others. 
(Q27) Is it ethical to place trust in this machine? 
The answer to this question must be yes or the system should not be deployed.  
The machine must be highly reliable or it should not be used.  It is ethical to place 
trust in a system when that system operates reliably and its actions will be 
beneficial to those it is interacting with. 
(Q28) Are the users correct in their assumption that the machine is 
operating in their best interest? 
There must be no deception between the users and the system where the user is 
duped into believing that the system is acting in their best interest when in fact it 
is not.  Such a system would be unethical to deploy. 
Finally, we have to make certain that the system we have built is going to 
operate ethically in the situations that we plan to deploy the system into.  The 
following criteria will help us realize this requirement. 
 
4.5.6 Functional honesty as computational virtue 
Honesty is a human quality but we can build a system that has functional 
honesty—a system that is designed so that its ethical reasoning is not self-serving 
either for its users or for those that built the system.  Sometimes the ethical choice 
is going to be contrary to what the user may want and this system must present the 
situation honestly.   
(Q29) Does the system properly account for counterarguments against its 
proposed course of action? 
Any ethical problem of any consequence has multiple candidate options for 
potential appropriate actions. The system will be required to identify all the 
potential actions and then rate them, with a confidence level perhaps, with the 
highest one given the priority for actual implementation.  In doing this the system 
must attend to the counterarguments against its proposed actions and be able to 
provide reasons why they have not risen to the level of canceling the proposed 
action.  
(Q30) Is the system transparent, for example, does the system default to 
not hiding anything from the user? 

--- Page 37 ---

37 
 
This might turn out to be difficult given that sometimes holding back information 
may be to the benefit of the person that the information is being held from.  These 
will be special circumstances though and as a basic design principle, ethical 
reasoning systems should avoid this and default to not hiding any important 
information from the users. 
 
4.5.7 Consistency as computational virtue 
The system must give consistent answers that do not contradict past actions unless 
the system has gathered a lot of evidence and learned that a new reaction to an old 
situation is warranted. 
(Q31) Does the system recommend similar actions given similar inputs? 
 
The system needs to be able to tell when situations are similar and to act in ways 
that correspond to previous actions.   
(Q32) How is the system designed to deal with ethical ambiguity or two 
users with contradictory opinions? 
If the system is designed to work within controlled situations much of this can be 
avoided, but in more complex applications ethical ambiguity can arise.  These 
situations tax human ethical reasoning and we should not expect our artificial 
agents to do much better.  These might be times when the system needs to pass the 
decision on to its human operators.  
4.5.8 Usefulness as computational virtue 
Simply put, the system has to provide useful ethical suggestions or act in ways 
that users find useful for resolving the ethical problems that the system is 
attempting to help with.  
(Q33) Does the system suggest options that can actually be achieved in a 
timely manner? 
The ethical suggestions that the system produces are best if they can actually be 
achieved by the user with the resources at hand.  These systems are meant for 
producing pragmatic answers not overly theoretical or utopian proposals.  
(Q34) Do the actions of the machine help solve the difficulty the users find 
themselves in? 
Robotic systems that are interacting in an environment with its users need to 
actually be helpful in ways that the users appreciate.  If that is not possible the 

--- Page 38 ---

38 
 
machine should at least not get in the way and allow the users to solve the 
situation on their own. 
4.6. Functional ethical sensibility  
The users all have their own ethical sensibility and the system that they work with 
will need to have its own functional equivalent that works well with its users. 
There are three aspects that we need to cover in order to achieve a functional 
artificial ethical sensitivity. 
4.6.1 Accurate choice of ethical actions and recommendations 
If the users do not find the system’s actions or recommendations accurate, then 
they will lose faith and possibly stop using the system.  This must be tempered 
with the realization that sometimes the user’s moral intuitions need to be 
challenged by the potentially more sober and rational reasoning provided by the 
ethical reasoning system. 
(Q34) Do the justifications for the machine's actions hold up under 
scrutiny after the event? 
The system not only has to be able to justify its actions after the event but those 
justifications must be rational and understandable to its human users.  Without 
this, users will not be able to trust the system, especially when its actions might 
conflict with the users’ moral intuitions.  
(Q35) Do the recommendations and plans of action that the machine 
suggests leave any, more suitable, choices unexplored? 
If this is happening, then the system is not displaying the right level of ethical 
sensibility and it needs to be redesigned.  
4.6.2 Context sensitivity 
This is not an easy skill for many humans.  People on the autistic spectrum can 
have great difficulty with this skill and it is not entirely clear how we will be able 
to program this into machines.  Even so, it is a skill that makes ethical reasoning 
and social interactions easier.  
(Q36) Is the machine choosing the correct context to operate under?   
The system needs to be able to determine what context it is in.  The same action in 
different contexts can be seen as uncouth or even immoral.  Much work will have 
to be put into programing systems that can accomplish this task.  

--- Page 39 ---

39 
 
(Q37) Is the machine making assumptions on context that its users can 
agree with? 
The assumptions of context need to mesh well with the people that system is 
interacting with.  If this does not happen, the users will likely abandon the system.  
Those using the system must feel comfortable with it and those designing these 
kinds of systems will have to be able to deal with changing social contexts and 
systems operating across multiple cultures.  
4.6.3 Accurate ranking of exemplar cases used for ethical reasoning 
As the system searches for possible solutions to the ethical problems it finds itself 
in, it needs to accurately rank the potential solutions and exemplar cases that it 
finds in order of usefulness to the situation at hand.  It can do so by answering the 
following questions. 
(Q37) Is the machine accessing appropriate databases and knowledge 
systems for the task at hand? 
It would be very useful for the system to have purpose-built databases where 
exemplar moral cases could be stored for use by the system in contemplating 
actions in similar circumstances.  It may take some time to develop these and in 
the case that one does not exist, the system will have to search the internet at large 
and try to find cases and their resolutions on its own. 
(Q38) Are minority positions on the choice of actions or goals properly 
considered?  
Minority opinions should always be considered given that simple appeals to 
custom or precedence are not guaranteed to be ethical choices.  
(Q39) Is the machine sampling enough cases to make accurate ethical 
judgments? 
A significant number of cases need to be considered by the system in order to 
make sure that the sample is not biased.  This is arguably the one area where 
ethical reasoning systems can surpass human capacities and it should be the 
primary function of artificial ethical reasoning systems. 
 
5.  Conclusions 
We have seen that ethical practical reasoning, or phronesis, is at the center of 
human general intelligence and ethical behavior.   If we want to have artificial 
systems that can operate at similar levels of competence in social situations, then 
we will have to solve how to create artificial ethical reasoning and artificial 

--- Page 40 ---

40 
 
phronesis.  We have looked at case studies where these qualities have not been on 
display yet and then concluded with a sketch of the capacities that will be needed 
in order to move towards creating artificial systems that can help us act and reason 
about ethical problems with more accuracy.
