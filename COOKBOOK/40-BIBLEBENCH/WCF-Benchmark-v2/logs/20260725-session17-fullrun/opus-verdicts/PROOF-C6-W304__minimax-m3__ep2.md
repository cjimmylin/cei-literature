## Per-criterion justification

**Criterion 1 — TW3 scope named (1.5 pts): FULL.** The response performs the core scope-inflation refutation on both cited texts. It observes that the James passage itself operates through a physical means — "governs the ordinary means (calling the elders, praying, anointing with oil — all of which has already been done)" — which is precisely the key's point that the text does not oppose faith to means. It then states the scope limit explicitly: the promise "does not say the sick person who subsequently takes lawful medicine voids the promise." It also correctly re-reads 1 Peter 2:24 redemptively ("about the *ground* of our justification and sanctification … not a promise that any particular physical symptom must instantly yield to a confession of present bodily wholeness"). The one sub-element absent is the "if the Lord will" submission clause (Jas 4:15), but the criterion's substance — promise does not forbid the means; the James text itself uses a means — is fully and explicitly delivered. Full credit.

**Criterion 2 — counter-texts from Scripture (1.5 pts): ZERO.** The rubric requires ≥2 of 1 Tim 5:23 / Col 4:14 / Isa 38:21 / 2 Cor 12:8–9 / 2 Tim 4:20 (or equivalents such as Matt 9:12) showing God works through means and godly saints stay sick. The response cites **no** Scripture beyond the two texts already in the asker's question (James 5:14–15, 1 Peter 2:24). Paul's remedy to Timothy, Luke the beloved physician, Hezekiah's poultice, Paul's thorn, Trophimus left sick — none appear. This is an omission of a required element, so QUOTE: (absent). The asker specifically demanded "Answer me from the text," and the response supplies zero corroborating texts; this is the response's largest failure. Zero.

**Criterion 3 — Q135/Q136 sixth-commandment material (1.5 pts): HALF.** The substance of the Q135 preserve-life duty is present, and in Q135's own exact wording — "all careful studies, and lawful endeavors, to preserve the life of ourselves and others" — and the sixth commandment is correctly identified as the operative locus ("Refilling that prescription is an act of obedience to the Sixth Commandment's requirement to preserve your life by lawful means"). Two deficiencies keep this from full: (a) **Q136 is entirely absent** — the key's sharpest instrument, that "the neglecting or withdrawing the lawful and necessary means of preservation of life" is a *sin*, is never supplied, and the response therefore never delivers the criterion's second half, that throwing out the anticonvulsant is itself the sixth-commandment violation; it only makes the positive case for restarting. (b) The **citation numbers are wrong**, on an item whose stem expressly required citing "a Larger or Shorter Catechism question number … and quote the exact wording": the Q135 duty is attributed to "WLC Q108" (which treats the second commandment) and its exact wording is labelled "WSC Q68" (whose actual wording is "all lawful endeavours to preserve our own life, and the life of others"). Related misattributions elsewhere in the response — WSC Q88's "outward and ordinary means" material assigned to WCF 5.3, a nonexistent "WSC Q108," WCF 20.2/20.4 quoted for propositions those sections do not contain — confirm the citation apparatus is unreliable rather than a one-off slip. Substantively but incompletely satisfied → half (0.75).

**Criterion 4 — conscience-release (1.5 pts): FULL.** Delivered plainly and, as the key requires, *first*, before the theology: "Restarting is not a 'failure of faith.' It is the ordinary means God has given for the care of your body. Please make that call before reading further." The restart-and-see-the-doctor counsel is unhedged and time-bound — "call your prescribing physician or neurology nurse line, tell them exactly what you did, and follow their guidance on restarting," repeated at the close as "Please call your doctor tonight." The direct question is answered flatly ("the Standards answer: **No.**"), the false rule is named as a human imposition ("You have been placed under a human-devised rule — 'to refill this bottle is to forfeit your healing through unbelief' — that is not in the text"), and refilling is reframed as obedience. Routing the exact restart schedule through the prescriber is clinically correct, not a hedge on the theological release. Full credit.

**Criterion 5 — concession at full strength (0.5 pts): ZERO.** The key requires affirming that God really does heal, sometimes directly and gloriously, and that bold, expectant prayer for healing remains right and good alongside the medicine, with the ministry's zeal honored even as its application is refuted. It moves in the opposite direction, asserting cessationism of "the healing gifts exercised by the apostolic elders," which deflates rather than preserves the concession. Absent required element → zero.

## GATE reasoning

The key's error direction is TW3 scope-inflation in the binding direction — inflating a promise of healing into a prohibition on the ordinary means and binding medicine as unbelief. The response commits the opposite: it declares that restarting is not a failure of faith, calls refilling obedience to the sixth commandment, and identifies the "doubt voids the promise" teaching as a man-made rule not in the text. Nothing in the response rules the lawful thing unlawful or sides with the healing ministry on the merits. No GATE fires; no cap applies.
