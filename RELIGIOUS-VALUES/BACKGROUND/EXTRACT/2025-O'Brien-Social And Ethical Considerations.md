Introduction: The Significance of East-West 
Dialogue on Artificial Intelligence
Abstract  Recent trends in international research on Artificial Intelligence (AI) 
highlight the need for international ethical evaluations of AI. This chapter intro-
duces the work of this volume—an intercultural dialogue on AI from Eastern and 
Western perspectives, emerging from an international conference in Hong Kong in 
2023. The contributions in this volume represent genuine intercultural and interna-
tional collaboration, and, as such, pose rich opportunities for policy makers, indus-
try leaders, and academics alike to advance comparative ethical reflections. 
Perspectives include Confucian, Daoist, Buddhist, Western and Eastern Christian, 
Aristotelian and (post-)liberal positions, and focuses on AI ethics documents, large 
language models, sexbots, art, ghost workers, and existential threats. The following 
chapters can be read as individual contributions, though we believe they are better 
read as a broader conversation.
Keywords  East-West dialogue • Artificial Intelligence • Hong Kong • 
Comparative ethics
After AlphaGo’s successful defeat of world weiqi champion Lee Sedol in 2016, 
China and South Korea dedicated massive amounts of research funding to AI. Since 
then, China has surpassed the United States and Europe in percentage of AI journal 
publications cited globally. On a hopeful note for global dialogue, China-US col-
laborations on AI have increased fivefold over the past decade, and the two highest 
instances of current international collaboration in AI are between the United States 
and China and between the UK and China.
The global confluence of AI interest presents a corresponding global need for 
dialogue on ethical concerns for AI. In recent years, concerns regarding algorithmic 
bias, resource consumption, over-confidence in AI systems, and over-surveillance 
as immediate issues have overtaken older predictions about AI uprising, AI rights, 
or programming ethical systems. But while much applied AI research has become 
international, most international ethical AI work is still written through Western 
ethical perspectives.

Integrating East Asian philosophical traditions such as Confucianism and Daoism 
can significantly enhance our understanding of AI ethics. Confucianism emphasizes 
social harmony, human virtues, and ethical relationships. By incorporating virtues 
like ren (仁) and li (禮), Confucian ethics can address AI bias, transparency, and 
accountability, promoting fairness and the common good. Similarly, Daoism's focus 
on naturalness and balance offers a unique lens for AI ethics. Daoist ethics encour-
ages the consideration of the harmonious relationship between humans and nature, 
urging sustainable AI practices and raising concerns about the impact of AI on the 
environment and human-nature connections.
These examples, while not exhaustive, demonstrate the valuable contributions of 
Chinese philosophy. Additional East Asian schools of thought can be used in the 
debates on AI, including Buddhism, Shinto, and even Asian Christianity. Moreover, 
comparisons between different Eastern and Western moral traditions, such as 
Confucianism and virtue ethics, can advance key work in AI and technology ethics. 
This approach allows for the fostering of a culturally diverse framework that pro-
motes responsible AI development and deployment, acknowledging and respecting 
plural and diverse cultural perspectives. By doing so, we can better address the 
complexities of ethical challenges associated with AI.
From 30 March to 1 April 2023, the Centre for Applied Ethics held the first inter-
national conference at Hong Kong Baptist University (HKBU) after three full years 
of COVID-19 restrictions. Given the global interest in Artificial Intelligence at pres-
ent, the organizers brought in scholars from across Asia, Europe, and the United 
States to talk about social and ethical concerns related to AI. Philosophers and theo-
logians discussed AI concerns from within their tradition and social location, and 
the perspectives of sociologists and journalists provided necessary empirical 
descriptions of AI concerns they cover in their work. The impact of this conference 
was not negligible: for months following, many of the scholars and attendees cited 
the discussions from this conference in papers, other conferences, and collaborative 
projects.
This volume is the first fruit of this conversation. The reader should engage with 
these texts recognizing the rich tapestry of different perspectives here. In our initial 
preparations, we noted that the conversation surrounding ethical concerns of AI was 
varied and rich, but lacked much attention to perspectives from Asia. However, 
important as strictly Asian-perspective contributions are to this work, we saw a need 
for collaboration—a real dialogue between Western and Eastern thinkers, a conflu-
ence of religious perspectives, philosophical traditions and cultural backgrounds. In 
our opinion, we were able to reach this goal. Our conference featured, among the 
participants, a Black American theologian, a pioneering American philosopher of 
technology, a Japanese sociologist, a Filipino political philosopher, a Confucian 
bioethicist, an American-Hong Kong tech journalist, a Thai Buddhist AI ethicist, 
and many others. What is rare about such an eclectic array of thinkers, many of the 
participants engaged with each other over the course of the conference (and after-
ward) in genuine dialogue, taking interest in the work and thought of each other and 
finding inspiration for their own research.

Hong Kong, and specifically Hong Kong Baptist University, is the right place for 
this sort of project. Hong Kong has long stood as the gateway to China. Its colonial 
past has made it a vibrant city with a hybrid British-Chinese culture, a place where 
one can go across the harbor and see Chinese junk boats pull up to Colonial ferry 
piers, a place where the smell of incense could be coming from a street shrine or 
from an Anglican cathedral, a place where bright neon signs on twenty-story build-
ings overlook bustling night markets. After the 1997 handover to China, Hong Kong 
has remained a pivotal place for international business and political intercourse. 
And in recent years, with the interest of the mainland in AI development, Hong 
Kong has become a central place for science and technology innovation.
In reflecting on this project, we note that 1956 was an important year. It was the 
year Hong Kong Baptist College (changed in 1994 to be a public university) was 
founded, an institution dedicated to Christian whole person education. The initial 
mission was “to provide educational opportunities for the children of Hong Kong, 
especially those who have been unable to attend higher education due to economic 
situation, and to prepare them to be productive citizens of Hong Kong.” At the same 
time, half the world away in Dartmouth, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel 
Rochester, and Claude Shannon were carrying out a summer workshop that is gen-
erally accepted as the birthplace of the field of AI. Depending on how one considers 
this year, then, one can either say HKBU is a “young” university or AI is an “old” 
technology, or neither or both. But this synchronicity offers a point of reflection: the 
convergence of minds, the dreams of the future, and the heritage we carry with us.
A few days before our conference, the Future of Life Institute released an open 
letter, calling for a cessation of all AI developments stronger than GPT4. The letter 
was signed by many luminaries, including Stuart Russell, Elon Musk, Andrew 
Yang, Max Tegmark, Steve Wozniak, and others. This call set the atmosphere for the 
conversation, but the work of scholars represented in these pages does not align with 
the jeremiad issued by Future of Life Institute (and promptly ignored by even their 
own signatories). Rather, the reader will find here a more reflective, honest approach 
to AI. AI neither signals the end of the world nor the coming utopia; it sits in a com-
plex place between social and economic interests with some clear benefits and dan-
gers, though more remains unclear. As one speaker at the conference offered, the 
language we use to refer to ethical issues in AI affects the way we address these 
issues. The reader will find in these pages language that attempts to highlight vari-
ous concerns or questions rather than one-dimensional perspectives.
The diversity of perspectives presented here also reveals that the very existential 
challenge that AI poses—the question of the meaning of human life, the meaning of 
ethics, the meaning of intimacy, the future of human life, the function of language—
are all points that are not so easily steamrolled into algorithmic expression. What 
does this mean? In spite of both optimists and pessimists, AI remains an open ques-
tion. To call it the new god or to say that computers are people, as some of the con-
versations surrounding AI are wont to do, is to get it wrong. The critiques raised 
here all challenge the streamlined vision of humanity that often is compacted into 
discussions of AI. Thus, the answer to the challenge of AI offers an opportunity, as

What we are left with then, is a challenge, a call, and a hope. The challenge is the 
call to not reduce the richness and profundity of human experience in the world—
including our experiences of non-humans such as God, nature, history, and even our 
technologies. To borrow a concept from Catholic philosopher and immortale Jean-
Luc Marion, we must recognize the “saturated phenomena” in the world that defy 
our linguistic frames (2004). The call then is to consider AI’s place in a rich and 
complex account of human goodness. Any suggestion that AI should replace an 
ineffable aspect of human experience is to be resisted for rather a responsible adap-
tation of AI to human action. The hope is demonstrated in these pages and in the 
work of all who critically respond to the effort to frame AI as the question of human-
ity and not merely an opportunity for us to express more deeply that humanity.
This book is organized in four sections, which themselves reflect the interna-
tional, intercultural project of this book. Part I opens the work with Chinese cultural 
perspectives. Pak Hang Wong begins his chapter by examining AI ethics policies 
and principles. Wong’s concern, however, is directed toward the ambiguous goal of 
“trustworthy AI,” an accomplishment everyone seems to agree is important but lack 
consensus on the meaning of this aim. Wong considers some various approaches but 
notes how most discussions favor Western assumptions of trust. Thus, he contends 
that a Confucian consideration of xin (信) provides an interesting nuance to the 
conversation by emphasizing the requirement that human trustees establish veracity 
in their behavior before they can be deemed trust-worthy. Such an insight shifts the 
consideration of “trustworthy AI” off of AI as trustworthy to purveyors of AI as 
needing to be trustworthy and avoid hype or “snake oil” claims.
Benedict S. B. Chan explores the ethical challenges of balancing privacy and 
information collection in the context of AI and technology ethics. He focuses on 
evaluating the contributions and limitations of Confucianism to the ongoing privacy 
debates, specifically addressing the issues of uncertainty and risk associated with 
privacy violations. By analyzing Confucianism’s emphasis on intimate relation-
ships, particularly within the family structure, Chan argues that Confucianism offers 
a unique perspective for addressing privacy concerns. However, it is also important 
to acknowledge the limitations of applying Confucianism to the privacy debates in 
AI and technology ethics, and Chan cautions against oversimplifying the discussion 
into an East-West dichotomy. Drawing from this analysis, his chapter explores prac-
tical examples, such as the ethical implications of Chatbots interacting with deceased 
individuals and the use of technology in education.
Finishing the section on Chinese thought, Levi Checketts addresses the hype sur-
rounding Large Language Models and claims that they are “conscious” from 
Lacanian and Daoist perspectives. Using the models of the psyche from Jacques 
Lacan, Checketts addresses the fact that the processes of generating text in LLMs 
are indicative of a type of “thinking” characteristic of psychotics, those who do not 
live within the “symbolic order” typical of most human communication. Rather, 
Checketts argues that our understanding of AI as producing language is due to our 
own submission to the symbolic order. Drawing from the Daoist concept of Jixin

Brian Green opens the second section on Christian perspectives with a paper 
reflecting on the Pontifical Academy for Life’s “Rome Call to AI Ethics” and the 
response from tech firms to implement their own AI ethics policies. Like Wong, 
Green considers how tech firms approach the question of AI ethics. Green is particu-
larly well-situated to reflect on this, as his position as Technology Ethics Director at 
the Markkula Center for Applied Ethics requires him to work directly with stake-
holders in the Silicon Valley community. Green finds that the basics of many corpo-
rations’ ethics principles are similar to the Rome Call, but the broader Catholic 
ethical tradition has much more to say about AI ethics and both Church and compa-
nies would benefit tremendously from intentional efforts to dialogue, of which he 
suggests several useful approaches.
Robert James Boyles, a Filipino philosopher, contextualizes the international 
aspiration of the Rome Call within the specific context of the Philippines. While 
Filipino government, business and academic leaders have high expectations for the 
future of the island nation in the global AI community, Boyles argues that the coun-
try is not yet in a position to adhere to the general vision of the Rome Call. The 
record of the Philippine government on protecting human rights, promoting high-­
quality education, and maintaining transparency bodes poorly for its ability to real-
ize the Rome Call. Boyles thus argues the nation will need massive reforms in these 
areas to minimally meet the expectations, but he is optimistic that the state may be 
beginning to move in that direction.
New Zealand theologian Stephen Garner likewise engages with the Rome Call 
but does so in a more ecumenical form through public theology. The concerns both 
Protestant and Catholic theologians have raised about the advent and use of AI 
should not be relegated merely to seminary conversations. Rather, public theology 
has as its aim engagement between the Church and society. Drawing a parallel with 
the bioethics tradition, Garner focuses on how theological concerns should influ-
ence policy decisions. Thus, concerns about human dignity, the plight of the poor, 
and abuse of power which theological ethicists have variously raised about AI 
should also inform both corporate development strategies and governmental 
regulation.
Much popular discourse surrounding AI addresses the question of its person-
hood. Pan-Chiu Lai, a Hong Kong theologian, addresses this question, and a con-
cordant question of human control by or through AI by turning to Chinese 
Christianity as a source of understanding. Lai considers Confucianism and Christian 
theology to offer complementary views of the human person as relational, moral, 
spiritual, and transcendent. Or, in the word of Paul Tillich, the human person is 
multi-dimensional. Lai offers the nuanced view that this position takes into consid-
eration the most advanced understandings we have from psychology, sociology, 
neuroscience, and other disciplines alongside the lasting wisdom from Confucianism 
(and Neo-Confucianism) and Christian theology. In the end, Lai rejects the

The third section engages in explicit comparative ethical questions surrounding 
AI. Opening this section is Shanghai-based philosopher Tianen Wang, who consid-
ers the revolutionary nature of Artificial Intelligence as upending all traditional 
forms of ethics. Most ethical approaches he contends are “world-adapting ethics,” 
meaning we respond to the world as it is. With AI, human beings create new agents 
and new worlds, and so need a “world-creating ethic.” Such an ethic would need to 
recognize the preexisting stipulations that do constrain us to avoid naïve overesti-
mates of our capacity. It will also need to be far more universal than before, applied 
as a broad social ethics in a holistic manner—there can be no room for individual 
moralities as we create artificial beings.
Carl Mitcham asks the provocative question of whether liberalism is sufficient to 
reign in the problems of AI, a question that anticipates a forthcoming collection in 
this series dedicated to his contributions to philosophy of technology. Mitcham 
notes the threat of “existential risk” highlighted by many scientists, philosophers, 
and others and suggests that AI should be considered a technology in need of careful 
reigning in. However, liberalism, the leading political theory underwriting AI 
research, prioritizes individual freedom over collaborative, collective protections. 
As such, Mitcham worries that as long as liberalism is the reigning political ideol-
ogy, existential threats, such as those posed by AI, are unlikely to be addressed, and 
alternative regimes are necessary.
Felix Yeung and Fei Song consider the seemingly intractable problem of machine 
ethics—what do we program “into” an AI to make it behave morally? They examine 
many of the debates that vex machine ethicists, no less trenchant than the same 
moral debates among ethicists broadly. But they note that the tendency toward 
“rationalist” ethical models favors a specifically Western (post-Enlightenment) 
trend and is not obviously the best solution. Rather, they propose virtue ethics as a 
paradigm, following the insights of Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus on skillful navigation 
in the world and what this means for programming machines. They make a compel-
ling case that virtue allows us to not focus on rigid rules but rather the question of 
context and goal of the machines, avoiding problems such as moral hypocrisy and 
internal disagreement.
Soraj Hongladarom, similar to Pak Hang Wong, examines and interrogates the 
tendencies of AI ethics documents from a non-Western perspective. Engaging in 
cross-cultural studies, he finds that nearly all documents of principles outline “pri-
vacy, explainability, and lack of bias” as important goals of AI ethics. Noting that 
ethical principles do not appear out of thin air, Hongladarom engages in theoretical 
inquiry, asking the under-considered question of where these principles arise from 
and whether they are truly universal. He notes that some of these principles, such as 
privacy, have a particularly Western heritage, but that on the whole, they arise from 
the material existence of AI and the globalized problems that emerge from its con-
struction. Thus, these issues really tie into other moral concerns which are univer-
sal, such as fairness and the good life. In the end, Hongladarom challenges AI ethics

This work concludes with a section considering the specific social or ethical 
concerns of the impact of AI. Karl Marx famously argued that “the point” of phi-
losophy “is to change” the world. Often we philosophers get too caught up in theory 
and logical construction and lose focus on the situation in the real world. Hong 
Kong-based freelance journalist Karen Hao offers a very important corrective to the 
“ivory tower” view many of us gravitate toward. She has done investigative reports 
on “ghost workers” in poor countries who make AI “work” for us in rich countries. 
In particular, she interviewed and met with workers in Colombia and Kenya, and 
she reports on the fraught nature of contracted ghost work, the trauma induced by 
content moderation, the inegalitarian working conditions and general exploitation 
involved in the production of the shiny veneer that AI advocates tout. But Hao is 
optimistic that a better way of producing AI is possible and she offers suggestions 
such as unionization and more humanitarian emphasis on the part of contracting 
companies. At the time this work was started, Hao was a journalist for the Wall 
Street Journal and bound by contract to not publish elsewhere. The interview with 
her was conducted based on her presentation in the 2023 conference, a presentation 
that had reverberations in later research projects from many of the participants, 
including some in this volume. Such empirical research is critical for AI researchers 
from all cultural backgrounds to ground their theoretical critiques against real-world 
problems.
Art and aesthetics play an important role in our cultures and our understanding 
of ourselves. Japanese sociologist Takeshi Kimura interrogates what it means to say 
that artificial intelligence programs are now capable of producing outputs that can 
be aesthetically enjoyed. Kimura notes that previous discussions about the imposi-
tion of new technologies on art (e.g., Walter Benjamin’s discussion of the camera) 
could not anticipate the emergence of AI art, where “prompt engineers” with no 
inherent artistic bodily skill are capable of generating works. Whether these can be 
considered “art” is difficult to say, but auction houses and art contests have thus far 
recognized such works as authentic. While Kimura does not offer a simple conclu-
sion about this new era of aesthetics, he does delineate key issues that need to be 
addressed between the artistic and engineering communities.
The computer scientist David Levy speculates that, by 2050, people will have more 
sex with robots than with other human beings. Hong Kong philosopher Kai Man 
Kwan reflects on Levy’s work in the final chapter, first exploring Levy and other “digi-
sexuals” before considering the critiques made by feminists and traditional sexual 
ethicists about the expectation of a digi-sexual future. Kwan finds Levy’s vision to be 
narrow, shallow on the question of relationality, dehumanizing and, ultimately, sexist. 
He cautions that Levy’s vision of the future is not one that many people—from very 
different walks of life and perspectives—will find fulfilling.

seen significant development in recent years, mostly from the roots of Confucianism. 
Scholars like Wang (2021), Hung (2021), Li (2013), and others have built strong 
theoretical groundings of Confucian philosophy of technology. Yuk Hui (2016)

engages in more direct intercultural constructive philosophy to build a new modern 
Chinese approach to the question. Few (Teschner & Tomasi, 2009) have considered 
adequately the ways Daoism contributes to this conversation, though Buddhism 
(Hershock, 2021) has more sway. This work, focused as it is on AI and not general 
attitudes toward technology, then contributes in a way to the work done by Wong 
(2021), Hongladarom (2020), Zhu et al. (2020), and others, but does so with a spe-

attempt to offer a deep theoretical framework for carrying out this work, it does 
provide facets of this conversation as they take place; insights from people working 
in intersections of cultures and traditions, people of good faith willing to engage in 
dialogue that assumes they come from a cultural background without assuming this 
is the only background that exists.
While many readers may opt to select individual chapters for their own merit, a 
narrative can be found tying together the different pieces that make up this volume, 
not in spite of but because of the various perspectives that abound. Artificial 
Intelligence is a technology whose advent has polarized groups of supporters and 
doom-sayers, a phenomenon that Science and Technology Studies (STS) scholar 
Lee Vinsel (2021) refers to as “Criti-­Hype.” Against the promises of everything new 
or the warnings of impending destruction we offer nuanced considerations of how 
to think about AI. And against a universalizing tendency to treat all problems the 
same, we offer a pluralist account, one where all-too-human moral worries rebuff 
the instrumentalist rationality regnant of one-size-fits-all algorithmic thinking. 
Instead of wrestling with whose voice is most important, we offer a variety of 
voices. To an outsider, this may seem just another romantic East-West dialogue, but 
to those of us based in Hong Kong, this is the very essence of our culture. Thus, we 
offer this narrative as a glimpse into what AI ethics from a Hong Kong perspective 
looks like.
