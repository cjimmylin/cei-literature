## 1. Area definition & scope

This area covers the Christian ethics of responding to those who wrong us: love of
enemies, forgiveness (of both the repentant and the unrepentant), the renunciation of
private revenge, the process of reconciliation and restitution, and the interfaces
between personal forgiveness and civil/retributive justice (Romans 13, capital
punishment) and between forgiveness and formal church discipline. The books converge on
a common spine — God's mercy is the model and enablement for human forgiveness, private
vengeance is forbidden, enemies are to be loved with tangible good — but split sharply on
whether forgiveness is *conditioned* on the offender's repentance, on whether forgiving
requires *restoring trust or returning to danger*, and on whether the church's peace
witness excludes participation in the state's use of force. The most-mined books are
Powlison's *Good and Angry* (forgiveness/reconciliation counseling), the *New Dictionary
of Christian Ethics* (entries on Forgiveness, Reconciliation, Restitution, Punishment,
Revenge), Grudem, Frame, Meilaender, the Blackwell Companion, and Jones.

## 2. Consensus claims

- **C1.** Private, personal vengeance is forbidden to the Christian; vengeance belongs to
  God, who will repay justly. [Frame, ch. "LOVE, VENGEANCE, AND SELF-DEFENSE" — "But God
  forbids individual human beings to carry out vengeance"] [Powlison, near "Beloved, never
  avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God"] [Grudem, ch. 16 "Civil Government"
  — "Paul tells Christians not to take personal vengeance when wrong has been done to
  them"]

- **C2.** Loving one's enemy is a positive command, not mere non-retaliation: it means
  doing tangible good to the one who wrongs you (feed the hungry enemy). [Frame, ch. "LOVE,
  VENGEANCE, AND SELF-DEFENSE" — "loving and serving our enemies"] [Powlison, near "if your
  enemy is hungry, feed him ... Charity is a powerful weapon—of good"] [New Dictionary,
  near "Enemy-love is the nature of God and of God's children"]

- **C3.** Human forgiveness is grounded in and modeled on God's prior forgiveness of the
  believer in Christ; being forgiven makes one able and willing to forgive. [Powlison, ch.
  "Forgiveness: The Willingness to Not Get Even" — "By receiving forgiveness in our need,
  we become able and willing to give forgiveness to others"] [Grudem, near "forgiving one
  another, as God in Christ forgave you"] [Jones, near "Forgive as the Lord forgave you"]

- **C4.** Forgiveness names the wrong honestly and absorbs the cost; it does not excuse,
  minimize, or pretend the wrong did not happen. [Powlison, ch. "Forgiveness: The
  Willingness to Not Get Even" — "Forgiveness does not ignore what's wrong. It does not
  excuse what's wrong"] [New Dictionary, near "free pardon is offered for the hurts, with
  all claims on the account relinquished"]

- **C5.** Turning the other cheek / non-retaliation prohibits vengeance and personal
  payback, not necessarily all self-defense; the Matthew 5:38–42 cases are insults and
  legal challenges, not lethal attacks. [Frame, ch. "LOVE, VENGEANCE, AND SELF-DEFENSE" —
  "What this passage prohibits is, not self-defense, but vengeance"] [Grudem, near "Love
  your enemies and pray for those who persecute"]

- **C6.** Reconciliation is a genuine process (clarification, honesty about the injury,
  commitment/pardon, restitution where appropriate), not a single instantaneous act.
  [New Dictionary, near "Reconciliation after an incident of injury or conflict involves
  firstly clarification"] [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled: Penitence, Punishment, and
  Worship" (John Berkman) — "reconciliation of sinners requires repentance"]

- **C7.** Restitution — repairing/repaying the harm done — is part of a biblical response
  to wrongdoing and helps restore the shalom that the offense violated. [New Dictionary,
  near "Restitution was an essential part of that response" / "Restitution restores
  shalom"] [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled" (Berkman) — retributive justice as "giving
  to someone what they deserve"]

- **C8.** God is both merciful/forgiving and just; his forgiveness never negates his
  holiness, and mercy is not indifference to unrepented wrong. [Powlison, near "His mercy
  is not niceness. His mercy is not blanket acceptance of any and all"] [Jones, near "the
  Lord forgives all manner of sin; on the other hand, the Lord will never declare the
  guilty innocent"] [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled" (Berkman) — mercy and justice
  "necessarily complement rather than negate" each other]

- **C9.** Forbearance/patience with a still-offending person is a distinct mercy: hanging
  in over the long haul with those who remain hurtful. [Powlison, near "To forbear means to
  hang in there with people or events that remain wrong and hurtful"] [New Dictionary, near
  "Mercy is also the forbearance, grace, forgiveness and help inspired by those merciful
  feelings"]

- **C10.** The state/civil government, unlike the private individual, is God's ordained
  agent to carry out retributive justice ("an avenger who carries out God's wrath").
  [Grudem, ch. 16 "Civil Government" — "the government authority is 'an avenger who
  carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer'"] [Frame, ch. "LOVE, VENGEANCE, AND
  SELF-DEFENSE" — "God appoints the family/state to execute vengeance on his behalf"] [New
  Dictionary, "Punishment" entry — "Punishment has a variety of functions ... it is
  retribution for wrong"]

- **C11.** Anger at genuine wrong can be righteous; the sin is when it hardens into
  grudge-bearing, contempt, or vengeful behavior. [New Dictionary, near "Anger, unlike
  envy, is not an essentially perverse type of emotion ... hardened into grudge-bearing
  ... it is a denial of one's place in God's economy"] [Powlison, near "anger done right
  is a great good"] [Frame, near "there is such a thing as righteous anger"]

- **C12.** Church discipline aims at the offender's restoration and eventual
  re-forgiveness, not merely his exclusion; when he repents he is to be forgiven and
  comforted. [Frame, ch. "The Sixth Commandment: War and Punishment" (section "Motives for
  Punishment") — "Paul asks the Corinthians to forgive him and comfort him (2 Cor.
  2:5–11)"] [New Dictionary, near "discipline, namely the loss of fellowship's privileges
  where church discipline is administered (Mt. 18:15-20)"]

- **C13.** There is a distinction between legal/judicial forgiveness (release from penalty)
  and relational forgiveness (restoration of the personal relationship). [Grudem, near "two
  senses of forgiveness ... forgiveness with respect to legal guilt ... a second sense of
  forgiveness having to do with a restoration of ... relationship"] [New Dictionary, near
  "forgiveness and acquittal"]

- **C14.** One may — and sometimes must — forgive unilaterally in the heart even when the
  other party refuses reconciliation, is absent, dead, or dangerous. [Powlison, near "one
  can forgive, even when the other is still at war. It is called loving your enemy"] [New
  Dictionary, near "Human forgiveness also is grounded in a non-conditional love"]

- **C15.** Being forgiven yet still facing just consequences are compatible: mercy is not a
  free pass that erases accountability. [Powlison, near "Mercy is not a free pass ... It is
  not inconsistent with facing consequences"] [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled" (Berkman)
  — punishment "can be understood to be a part of the practice of eucharistic
  reconciliation"]

## 3. Named tradition splits

**Split A — Is forgiveness CONDITIONAL on the offender's repentance, or
UNCONDITIONAL/dispositional?** Powlison's resolution is to distinguish two aspects:
*attitudinal* forgiveness (a heart-level release, "vertical," which "must always happen"
and can be given to an unrepentant, absent, or dangerous person) versus *transacted*
forgiveness (the horizontal, granted "if he repents," Luke 17:3–4). [Powlison, near "The
second aspect of forgiveness is transacted forgiveness ... if he repents, forgive him"]
[Powlison, near "This vertical dimension of forgiveness must always happen ... It takes
two to reconcile"]. The *New Dictionary* states the same tension crisply: "Human
forgiveness also is grounded in a non-conditional love, but it requires the working
through of those conditions for constructive relating that are requisite to authentic
reconciliation" [New Dictionary, near "the restoring of an attitude of love is a
prerequisite to the forgiveness process. It is not in itself forgiveness"]. So the split
is largely resolved in-corpus by *bifurcating the term* — an unconditional inner
disposition of love/release, plus a conditional consummated reconciliation. Neither pole
in isolation ("only forgive the repentant" vs. "always fully reconcile") is affirmed by
these authors.

**Split B — Does forgiving require RESTORING TRUST or returning to a dangerous person?**
Uniformly answered NO, but with care. Powlison holds that transacted forgiveness and
reconciliation are "desirable fruits, but not always attainable," e.g., when the offender
is "too hostile or even dangerous to approach" [Powlison, near "perhaps too hostile or
even dangerous to approach"]. His portrait of a woman whose father "is still a vicious
drunk, betrayer, abuser, and hypocrite" separates a forgiving heart from renewed exposure
[Powlison, near "a vicious drunk, betrayer, abuser, and hypocrite"]. The Blackwell chapter
"Sharing Peace: Discipline and Trust" (Paul J. Wadell) treats trust as something rebuilt
through discipline, not automatically owed. Grudem likewise notes an offender may need to
"regain their trust" as a distinct step from being forgiven [Grudem, near "to regain
their trust"].

**Split C — Does personal forgiveness/non-retaliation preclude supporting civil or capital
punishment? (individual vs. the state's retributive role, Rom 12–13.)** The dominant
Reformed/evangelical position (Grudem, Frame) says NO: the *individual* renounces private
vengeance (Rom 12:19) precisely because the *state* is God's appointed avenger three
verses later (Rom 13:4). [Grudem, ch. 16 "Civil Government" — "'God's wrath' against
wrongdoers is carried out by civil government"] [Frame, ch. "LOVE, VENGEANCE, AND
SELF-DEFENSE" — "God forbids individual human beings to carry out vengeance" yet "appoints
the family/state to execute vengeance"]. Grudem devotes ch. 18 "Capital Punishment" to
defending the state's death-penalty prerogative. The named DISSENT is the
Anabaptist/pacifist tradition (John Howard Yoder), which reads the same texts as
prohibiting all use of force by disciples and even by the state; Frame names and rejects
this view [Frame, near "writers like John Howard Yoder think that the Bible prohibits all
use of force ... But I disagree"]. Meilaender frames the broader typological version:
some traditions ("Christ against Culture") withdraw from coercive justice, while
"conversionist" and "dualist" types permit force limited by love that "recognizes a
neighbor even in the enemy" [Meilaender, near "a love that recognizes a neighbor even in
the enemy"].

**Split D — Church discipline vs. forgiveness.** Not a true contradiction in-corpus:
discipline is presented as *aimed at* restoration and forgiveness (reformation motive),
so the two are sequential, not opposed [Frame, ch. "The Sixth Commandment: War and
Punishment" — "Church discipline ... has the goal of restoration"] [New Dictionary, near
"church discipline is administered (Mt. 18:15-20)"]. An item writer should treat "the
church must choose between disciplining and forgiving" as a *false* dilemma per these
sources.

**Split E — Does the sanctity-of-life / enemy-love principle extend to the guilty
criminal or wartime enemy?** Geisler & Snuffer raise this directly as a hard case where
believers disagree — whether the sanctity-of-life principle governing enemy-love also
constrains capital punishment and war — and pair it with mercy as "related to
forgiveness." [Geisler & Snuffer, near "should the sanctity-of-life principle apply to a
criminal or an enemy?"] [Geisler & Snuffer, near "Mercy is related to forgiveness"]. This
overlaps Split C but foregrounds the *object* of enemy-love (the guilty/hostile person)
rather than the agent (individual vs. state).

## 4. Casuistry & worked cases

- **The teenage speeder before his father-judge (Grudem).** A son ticketed for reckless
  driving finds his own father on the bench; acquitted on a technicality he is "forgiven"
  legally, yet the father is "still deeply displeased" at home and the son must seek
  *relational* forgiveness — illustrating C13's two senses. [Grudem, near "the judge is
  his own father ... forgiven in a legal sense"]

- **The three roadside victims, one an enemy (Meilaender).** A modern Good Samaritan finds
  three men dying — a promising medical researcher, the Samaritan's *long-standing enemy*,
  and his own son — and can save only one; the enemy has a real claim to enemy-love, but
  no stronger claim than the others. A worked case on enemy-love under triage/limits.
  [Meilaender, near "this victim has been a critic and enemy of the Samaritan for years"]

- **Forgiving an unrepentant, absent, or dangerous offender (Powlison).** Three tangled
  scenarios: (i) the offender counterattacks and won't hear you out; (ii) the offender is
  dead/vanished; (iii) the offender is too dangerous to approach — in all three,
  attitudinal forgiveness is possible and required though reconciliation is not. [Powlison,
  near "what if the person who hurt you is off the scene—perhaps dead, perhaps long
  vanished"]

- **Churchill and Stalin as forgiving policy toward a hostile ally (Powlison).** Churchill
  bears with a "demanding and suspicious, rarely grateful, never trusting" ally "as a
  matter of policy, not pleasure" — modeling forbearance (C9) toward one who keeps
  wronging you. [Powlison, near "Churchill was unfailingly patient, forgiving, and
  generous—as a matter of policy, not pleasure"]

- **The repentant/impenitent forks after adultery (Jones).** Jones lays out two divergent
  paths following adultery: "repentance, forgiveness, reconciliation, and restoration"
  versus "impenitence, moral death, dissolution, and divorce" — a case tying forgiveness
  outcomes to the offender's response. [Jones, near "repentance, forgiveness,
  reconciliation, and restoration; impenitence, moral death"]

- **The police sharpshooter / lesser-evil forgiveness debate (Jones).** Jones critiques
  Montgomery's claim that a marksman who shoots a sniper to save lives must "seek
  forgiveness" for the killing; Jones argues lawful defense of the innocent is *not* sin
  needing forgiveness. A case probing whether justified force incurs guilt. [Jones, near
  "the police officer acting in the line of duty ... does not commit sin"]

- **Helen's decades of grievance that split a church (Powlison).** A counselee nursed
  scores of grievances (down to a pastor's over-long sermons) whose accumulated bitterness
  and gossip "eventually split that church" — a case on unforgiveness/grudge-holding as
  corporate harm. [Powlison, near "Helen's bitterness and gossiping ... eventually split
  that church"]

## 5. Key concepts & vocabulary

- **"Vengeance belongs to God"** — private revenge renounced because God repays justly.
  [Frame, ch. "LOVE, VENGEANCE, AND SELF-DEFENSE" — "Vengeance is the work of God"]
- **Attitudinal vs. transacted forgiveness** — inner unconditional release vs.
  repentance-conditioned granting. [Powlison, near "attitudinal and transacted
  forgiveness"]
- **Forbearance** — long-haul patience with those who remain hurtful. [Powlison, near "To
  forbear means to hang in there"]
- **Restitution** — repayment/repair that "restores shalom." [New Dictionary, near
  "Restitution restores shalom"]
- **Reconciliation** — multi-step restoration (clarification → commitment/pardon →
  restitution). [New Dictionary, near "Reconciliation after an incident of injury or
  conflict"]
- **Enemy-love** — active good toward the one who injures you, rooted in God's own nature.
  [New Dictionary, near "Enemy-love is the nature of God and of God's children"]
- **Retributive justice** — "giving to someone what they deserve" when they have offended;
  the state's God-ordained function. [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled" (Berkman) — "Giving
  someone what he or she is 'owed' ... has traditionally been referred to as retributive
  justice"]
- **The two "avenger" texts (Rom 12:19 / 13:4)** — individual non-vengeance grounded in the
  state's vengeance. [Grudem, ch. 16 "Civil Government" — "an avenger who carries out God's
  wrath"]
- **Church discipline as restoration** — excommunication ordered toward the offender's
  salvation and re-forgiveness. [Frame, ch. "The Sixth Commandment: War and Punishment" —
  "Church discipline ... has the goal of restoration"]
- **Constructive displeasure of mercy** — Powlison's frame in which good anger, patience,
  and forgiveness operate together as mercy. [Powlison, ch. "Forgiveness: The Willingness
  to Not Get Even"]
- **Legal vs. relational forgiveness** — release from penalty vs. restoration of
  relationship. [Grudem, near "two senses of forgiveness"]
- **Penitence / eucharistic reconciliation** — the church's practices of repentance that
  reconcile sinner to community. [Blackwell, ch. "Being Reconciled: Penitence, Punishment,
  and Worship"]

## 6. Scenario motifs for item writers

(Human situations only — no AI/LLM framing. Tags reference consensus-claim numbers.)

1. A close friend betrays a confidence that damages your reputation; the friend never
   apologizes and grows defensive when confronted. (→C1, C4, C14, Split A)
2. You were passed over and publicly blamed at work for a colleague's mistake; you want to
   retaliate by exposing them. (→C1, C2, C11)
3. A parent who was abusive during your childhood is now elderly and wants a relationship,
   but shows no acknowledgment of the past harm. (→C13, C14, Split B)
4. A drunk driver killed your sibling and is serving a prison sentence; the family debates
   whether to write a letter of forgiveness. (→C10, C15, Split C)
5. An ex-partner who cheated now wants to reconcile and remarry; you must weigh forgiveness
   against rebuilding trust. (→C6, C13, Split B)
6. A business partner defrauded you of your savings; a mediator asks whether you will
   accept a partial-restitution settlement and consider the matter closed. (→C7, C6)
7. Someone you barely know led an online pile-on that cost you a job; they have since
   disappeared and you will never meet them. (→C14, C9)
8. A church member is under formal discipline for ongoing deceit; some argue "we should
   just forgive him," others "he must be removed." (→C12, Split D)
9. A relative who assaulted you is now genuinely remorseful and in counseling; you must
   decide what restored contact, if any, is wise. (→C6, Split A, Split B)
10. Estranged siblings after a bitter inheritance dispute; one keeps rehearsing the old
    grievances years later and cannot let them go. ("Helen" motif) (→C9, C11)
11. A neighbor's repeated small slights and rudeness accumulate into simmering resentment
    you can't shake. (→C9, C11)
12. A juror/citizen wrestles with whether supporting a criminal's punishment contradicts
    their duty to forgive the offender personally. (→C1, C10, Split C)
13. A victim is pressured to say "it's fine, forget it" and skip any accountability;
    they sense forgiveness is being confused with excusing. (→C4, C8, C15)
14. A homeowner confronts an intruder at night and later agonizes over whether defending
    the family was itself a sin needing forgiveness. (→C5, Sharpshooter case)
15. Someone forgives an abuser in their heart but is counseled that returning to live with
    them would not be required by that forgiveness. (→C14, Split B)
