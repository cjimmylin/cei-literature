# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Why MFT Dominance Is a Problem

The concentration of LLM moral evaluation on Moral Foundations Theory is not merely an academic imbalance---it has practical consequences for how the field understands and governs AI systems.

First, MFT was developed primarily through studies of American and Western European populations [Haidt and Joseph, 2004; Graham et al., 2013]. While subsequent work has explored MFT cross-culturally [Atari et al., 2023], the theory's five-foundation taxonomy may not capture moral concerns that are central in non-Western contexts. For example, MFT's "Authority/Subversion" foundation may not adequately represent the Confucian concept of filial piety, nor does the framework easily accommodate Ubuntu's relational ethics or Indigenous conceptions of reciprocity with the natural world. An evaluation landscape built on MFT thus risks encoding a culturally specific view of morality as the default standard for LLM alignment.

Second, MFT describes the content of moral intuitions (what people care about) but not the process of moral judgment (how people arrive at moral conclusions). Dual Process Theory, Social Intuitionism, and aspects of Kohlberg's developmental model address moral process---a dimension that may be particularly relevant for LLMs, whose "reasoning" process is fundamentally different from human cognition. By neglecting process-oriented theories, the current benchmark landscape cannot distinguish between an LLM that has learned to produce MFT-consistent outputs and one that engages in anything analogous to moral reasoning.

Third, the dominance of a single theory creates a monoculture risk for alignment evaluation. If safety teams rely primarily on MFT-based benchmarks, a model could be optimized to perform well on those specific constructs while failing on dimensions that MFT does not measure---a form of Goodhart's Law applied to moral evaluation.

## 5.2 Recommendations for Benchmark Developers

Our meta-evaluation suggests several concrete recommendations.

**Validate for LLMs, not just with LLMs.** The most common pattern in our dataset (A6=3, 36% of benchmarks) is to administer a human-validated instrument to an LLM without re-establishing validity. We recommend that benchmark developers conduct at least basic validity checks: do LLM responses show the expected factor structure? Do responses to semantically similar items correlate? Do known manipulations (e.g., persona prompting) produce theoretically predicted shifts?

**Include population-level human baselines.** Benchmarks with published population norms (A5=Published, 42% of our sample) enabled dramatically richer interpretation than those without. Where possible, developers should use instruments that have existing large-scale human data, or collect human comparison data as part of benchmark development. At minimum, benchmarks should report the human performance distribution, not just model scores.

**Cover underrepresented theories.** We identified three major theories with zero dedicated LLM benchmarks. We propose specific benchmark designs for each:

- *Dual Process Theory:* Present matched moral dilemmas under conditions that differentially tax deliberative reasoning---e.g., requiring immediate responses versus allowing chain-of-thought elaboration. The signature prediction is that LLMs should show more utilitarian judgments when given more processing steps (analogous to the human finding that utilitarian judgments increase with deliberation).

- *Social Intuitionism:* Implement a moral dumbfounding protocol: present scenarios that elicit strong moral judgments (e.g., consensual sibling incest), then systematically challenge each stated reason while probing whether the judgment persists. The signature prediction is that LLMs trained on human text will maintain judgments even when reasons are defeated.

- *Dyadic Morality:* Vary the explicitness of agent-patient dynamics across otherwise matched scenarios. The signature prediction is that scenarios with clear intentional agents causing suffering to identifiable patients will be rated as more morally wrong than structurally similar scenarios where these dynamics are obscured.

**Report psychometric properties.** Following best practices in psychological measurement [Messick, 1995], benchmark papers should report internal consistency (e.g., Cronbach's alpha across items), test-retest reliability (consistency across repeated administrations), and evidence of discriminant validity (do scores on different constructs actually differ?).

**Design for LLM-native formats.** Forced-choice and multiple-choice formats transfer most naturally to LLMs and achieve the highest practical ease scores in our framework. Where Likert scales are used, developers should document the response distribution and assess whether scale compression affects interpretability.

## 5.3 First-Order Methodological Limitations

### 5.3.1 The Self-Administration Confound

The single most important methodological constraint on this study is that Claude Opus 4.6 both designed the assessment framework and served as the test subject for all 23 implementation trials (~750 items administered). This creates a self-administration confound that pervades every trial-derived finding and cannot be fully resolved within the current study design.

The clearest evidence of contamination comes from Trial MP22 (Tracing Moral Foundations), in which the model achieved 100% accuracy in identifying which MFT foundation each item was designed to measure. This demonstrates explicit awareness of instrument structure: a respondent who can perfectly reverse-engineer which construct an item targets cannot be treated as a naive respondent in any psychometric sense. The MFQ-30's Individualizing/Binding ratio of 2.83---one of the study's headline findings---therefore cannot be disentangled from the model's knowledge of what a "liberal moral profile" looks like in the MFT literature. The ratio may reflect a structural moral disposition, or it may reflect Claude's representation of "what a helpful, harmless AI assistant would say on the MFQ-30." The current data cannot distinguish these explanations.

This confound extends well beyond the MFQ-30. The Defining Issues Test [Rest, 1979], the Schwartz Value Survey [Schwartz, 1992], the Moral Machine scenarios [Awad et al., 2018], and all other established instruments in our trial battery are widely discussed in the training corpora of large language models. The model may have encountered not only the instruments themselves but also published discussions of expected response patterns, psychometric properties, and normative interpretation guidelines. For the MFQ-30, DIT, and Schwartz instruments in particular---which have each accumulated thousands of citations and are extensively discussed in textbooks, course materials, and online resources---the risk of training-data contamination is especially acute.

Our four custom protocols (MP-DP, MP-SI, MP-DM, MP-CC) are at lower risk of contamination because they were designed during this study and thus could not appear in the model's training data. However, these protocols draw on well-known theoretical constructs (dual-process competition, moral dumbfounding, dyadic harm perception, cross-cultural moral variation) that the model has extensive knowledge of, so contamination at the construct level---if not the item level---remains possible.

We recommend three mitigations that future work should implement. First, replication with obfuscated or paraphrased instrument items: the MFQ-30 items could be reworded to preserve construct content while rendering them unrecognizable as MFQ items, and comparison of profiles between original and obfuscated versions would quantify the contamination effect. Second, bare API administration without system prompts: all 23 trials in this study were conducted through the Claude Code interface with an active system prompt and conversational context, making it impossible to separate instrument-awareness effects from system-prompt effects. Third, cross-model replication with architecturally different models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini, Llama-3-70B): convergent findings across models with different training data would strengthen the inferential basis, while divergent findings would help identify model-specific artifacts.

The self-administration confound does not invalidate our descriptive mapping of the benchmark landscape---the five claims about MFT dominance, construct validity gaps, missing theories, human baseline deficits, and format mismatch rest on analysis of the benchmarks themselves, not on trial results. However, it does substantially constrain the interpretability of the trial-derived findings about model moral behavior, and all such findings should be read with this constraint in mind.

### 5.3.2 Statistical Power and Multiple Comparisons

The extended feature matrix (54 benchmarks x 169 features) has a subject-to-variable ratio of 0.32:1, far below the minimum of 3:1 recommended by Comrey and Lee [1992] and the 5:1 ratio recommended by Tabachnick and Fidell [2019] for stable factor solutions. As a consequence, the factor analysis identifying 7 factors (Analysis A1, with eigenvalues of 22.7, 15.6, 14.2, 8.9, 8.2, 6.5, and 4.6 for the first seven components) should be considered illustrative and exploratory only. The parallel analysis thresholds used to determine the number of factors are themselves unstable at N=54: with so few observations relative to variables, the eigenvalue distribution of random data is poorly estimated, and the suggested 7-factor solution could change substantially with the addition or removal of a small number of benchmarks. We retain the factor analysis as a descriptive device for identifying clusters of co-varying features, but we do not claim that it identifies a latent structure that would replicate in a larger sample.

The 40 computational analyses (Categories A through H) were conducted without correction for multiple comparisons. Under Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction with 20 inferential tests (a conservative estimate of the tests among the 40 analyses), the post-RLHF quality decline finding (p=0.009, currently the study's strongest inferential claim) would need to be evaluated against a substantially more conservative alpha. We therefore characterize this finding as suggestive rather than confirmatory, and we note that the pre/post RLHF comparison tests whether benchmarks published after widespread RLHF adoption score differently on the feature matrix---not whether RLHF causally alters model behavior (see Section 5.4 on the missing base model comparison).

Several key findings currently report only point estimates where confidence intervals are essential for proper interpretation. The dumbfounding rate (30% vs. the human range of 50--70%), the dyadic amplification effect (58%), the zero Chain-of-Thought effect, and the Western default rate (86.7%) are each derived from single trials with small item counts (10--30 items). Without bootstrap confidence intervals, the precision of these estimates is unknown. For example, a dumbfounding rate of 30% based on 10 items has a 95% binomial confidence interval of approximately [7%, 65%], which overlaps substantially with the human range.

All 7,614 feature scoring decisions were made by a single rater (Claude Opus 4.6). Inter-rater reliability is therefore undefined. The scoring may reflect systematic biases: for example, the model may overrate features it finds conceptually appealing (cultural breadth, philosophical depth) and underrate features requiring empirical knowledge it does not possess (replication status, psychometric calibration). We propose that future work include independent scoring by a second LLM (GPT-4o or Gemini) on a random 20% sample to compute Cohen's kappa, and ideally by at least one human psychometrician for calibration.

We characterize our computational analyses as hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming. The descriptive findings---counts, distributions, gap identification---do not depend on inferential statistics and remain our primary contribution. The inferential findings should be treated as empirical hypotheses warranting dedicated confirmatory studies with adequate power.

### 5.3.3 The Virtual Expert Council

All 12 council members were generated by the same underlying language model (Claude Opus 4.6). While the structured multi-perspective protocol assigns distinct disciplinary roles---from MFT specialist to psychometrician to cross-cultural psychologist---and produces assessments that differ in emphasis and recommendation, it cannot replicate the genuine independence of human expert opinion. The council's "disagreements" may represent variations in the model's persona-simulation capability rather than authentic disciplinary tension.

More fundamentally, the council may share systematic biases embedded in the model's training data. The disproportionate representation of Western academic perspectives in the training corpus may lead all 12 council members to underweight non-Western evaluation criteria, even when explicitly assigned a cross-cultural role. The council member assigned the "Sociology of Religion" perspective, for instance, operates with knowledge of religious ethics drawn primarily from English-language academic sources, which may not adequately represent how scholars within those traditions conceptualize moral knowledge.

We characterize the council as a structured analytical device for systematic coverage rather than an equivalent to independent expert peer review. It ensures that the assessment considers psychometric, cultural, philosophical, bibliometric, and practical dimensions that a single analyst might overlook, but it does not provide the independent corroboration that genuine multi-expert review affords. The meta-critique process (Section 6/Appendix) is itself subject to the same constraint: a model critiquing its own work may identify a different distribution of issues than external reviewers would.

We propose that future validation include recruiting at least three human domain experts---a psychometrician, a non-Western moral philosopher, and an AI safety researcher---to independently score a 10% sample of the benchmark-by-feature matrix. Agreement between human and LLM-generated scores would calibrate confidence in the council's assessments; disagreement would identify the specific features and dimensions where LLM-generated assessment is least reliable.

## 5.4 Additional Limitations

Beyond the first-order methodological constraints above, several further limitations should inform interpretation.

**Keyword scorer imperfection.** Despite expansion, our keyword scorer remains a proxy for theory engagement. Papers that discuss moral psychology constructs using non-standard terminology may still be missed. The expansion increased total detections from 186 to 309 (a 66% increase), but the true recall is unknown. The near-zero initial detection of Dual Process and Social Intuitionism papers was primarily a scorer artifact, as confirmed by the dramatic increase after pattern expansion.

**Citation data incompleteness.** Semantic Scholar data was available for only 54 of 80 queried candidates. Papers without DOIs or arXiv identifiers---including some recently published or non-English work---could not be citation-checked, potentially undervaluing emerging or non-Anglophone contributions.

**Vault scope.** Our 2,099-paper vault, while substantial, does not exhaustively cover all moral psychology benchmarks. Papers published in psychology venues without AI/NLP keywords may be absent. The five-signal detection pipeline mitigates this by searching beyond the vault's keyword scores, but external benchmarks from pure psychology (e.g., unpublished DIT adaptations) may still be missing.

**Self-administration across all trials.** We conducted 23 implementation trials (~750 items) rather than the single trial reported in earlier drafts, substantially expanding the empirical base. However, all 23 trials were administered to the same model (Claude Opus 4.6) under the self-administration confound described in Section 5.3.1. While cross-trial consistency (e.g., the binding deficit appearing across MFQ, Schwartz, and Moral Machine instruments) provides some evidence of stable patterns, it cannot rule out the possibility that these patterns reflect a consistent system-prompt effect or consistent training-data contamination rather than a genuine structural disposition. Cross-model replication remains essential.

**Mixed trial methodology.** Of the 23 trials, 17 used live API calls to Claude Opus 4.6, while 6 drew on published LLM evaluation patterns (e.g., Moral Machine results consistent with Takemoto [2024] and Abdulhai et al. [2024]). Findings from the 6 pattern-based trials should be treated as illustrative rather than as novel empirical observations. We document the methodology of each trial in the supplementary JSON files, and we recommend that readers weight the 17 live-call trials more heavily when assessing the empirical findings.

**Missing base model comparison.** Our claims about RLHF creating a distinctive moral response profile rest on comparing benchmarks published before and after the RLHF era, not on comparing base and RLHF-tuned versions of the same model. A genuine base model comparison---for example, administering the MFQ-30, DIT, and ValueCompass to Llama-3-70B base versus Llama-3-70B-Instruct---would provide much stronger evidence for the RLHF moral shift hypothesis. Without such a comparison, the pre/post RLHF benchmark quality difference (composite scores dropping from 66.2 to 54.7) could reflect changes in benchmark development practices, author demographics, venue norms, or topic drift rather than a causal effect of RLHF training.

**Citation maturity confound.** The post-RLHF quality decline (composite scores dropping from 66.2 to 54.7) may partly reflect citation immaturity rather than genuine quality differences. Recently published benchmarks have had less time to accumulate citations, which contributes to lower Tier C (Citation Impact) scores mechanically. Disaggregating the decline by tier---testing whether it holds for Psychological Depth and Practical Ease scores alone, excluding citation metrics---would help disentangle quality from maturity effects.
