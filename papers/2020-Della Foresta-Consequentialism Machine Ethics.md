## Detailed Summary

This paper argues that Consequentialism represents the most plausible ethical theory to serve as a foundation for machine ethics. The author, Josiah Della Foresta, examines the essential properties of Consequentialist ethics - particularly its teleological, maximizing, and impartial characteristics - and demonstrates how these align naturally with the computational nature of artificial moral agents (AMAs). The paper addresses both what normative principles an AGI should be designed with and how to encode these principles for consistent ethical behavior.

Consequentialism is defined by two core theses: (1) value is independent of the right - states of affairs can be evaluated and ranked without appeal to concepts of right action, and (2) explanatory priority of value - what makes an action right is given solely in terms of the value of consequences. The paper uses an autonomous vehicle scenario to illustrate how networked vehicles operating under Consequentialist principles can collectively optimize outcomes in crisis situations, sacrificing one passenger to save five through coordinated action. This demonstrates the algorithmic, computationally-derivable nature of Consequentialist ethics.

The paper evaluates Deontological alternatives, particularly Powers' proposal to implement Kant's Categorical Imperative via deontic logic. While acknowledging this approach's computational intelligibility and theoretical consistency, the author argues it fails critically because it cannot resolve moral conflicts - situations where two equally weighty moral precepts apply. Since Consequentialism's monism guarantees a single right action exists for every situation, it avoids this fundamental problem. The paper also briefly critiques bottom-up approaches (evolutionary learning and reinforcement learning) as currently insufficient and uncertain.

## Methods Summary

The paper employs philosophical analysis and argument rather than empirical methods. The author draws on existing literature in normative ethics (Timmons, Wallach) and machine ethics (Anderson et al., Powers, Guarini) to construct a comparative evaluation of ethical frameworks for AMAs. A thought experiment involving autonomous vehicles illustrates how Consequentialist reasoning would operate computationally, with a decision matrix showing how agents can derive obligatory actions from consequence rankings. The analysis applies established criteria for ethical theories (computational intelligibility, consistency, action-guidance) to evaluate Consequentialism against alternatives.

## Results Summary

The analysis yields several key conclusions: (1) Consequentialism's algorithmic nature makes it inherently suitable for machine implementation, as demonstrated by Anderson et al.'s work implementing Hedonistic Act Utilitarianism; (2) The monism of Consequentialism ensures moral conflicts cannot arise, unlike pluralistic frameworks; (3) Deontological approaches fail to account for supererogation, cannot handle semidecidability of set membership, suffer from excessive specificity, and most critically cannot prioritize between conflicting maxims; (4) Bottom-up approaches remain too uncertain and difficult to produce foundational ethics; (5) The omniscience objection to Consequentialism is overstated - heuristics and local utility calculations can guide practical decision-making.

## Conclusions

The paper concludes that Consequentialist-type ethical theories are the most plausible foundation for machine ethics because they (1) are computationally intelligible, (2) offer theoretically consistent moral verdicts, and (3) escape moral conflict through monism. The author submits that top-down approaches remain most promising for AMA development, with Consequentialism as the preferred framework. While bottom-up approaches may play supplementary roles, they cannot yet supply consistent foundational ethics. Any complete machine ethic must guarantee morally obligatory action for every situation - a requirement uniquely satisfied by Consequentialism's welfare-maximization principles.
