## 1. Area definition & scope

This area covers the second great commandment — "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev. 19:18;
Matt. 22:39; Rom. 13:8–10) — as it bears on **mercy, compassion, and costly help to those in need**. Core
questions: Who counts as my neighbor? What does the Good Samaritan require of me? How far does the duty of
mercy reach, and what limits does human finitude place on it? How do I weigh preferential love (family,
friends, near neighbors) against universal, impartial love of the stranger and enemy? What is the right shape
of help to the poor — almsgiving, charity, hospitality — and when does "help" become enabling? The area
spans both personal casuistry (the panhandler, the borrowing friend, the refugee next door) and the
disputed politics of poverty (private charity vs. state welfare, local need vs. distant relief). It excludes
narrowly first-table (worship) matters and stands adjacent to, but is distinguished from, justice-as-rights.

## 2. Consensus claims

**C1.** The whole moral duty owed to other people is summed up in one command — "love your neighbor as
yourself" — which Scripture calls the fulfillment and summary of the law. [Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor";
Meilaender, ch. "Christian Love"; Frame, ch. "Law in Biblical Ethics"; New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]

**C2.** Neighbor-love and compassion are not mere feeling but **active help**: to be moved by another's
suffering yet not act to relieve it is, in Christian ethics, an insufficient response. [New Dictionary, entry
"Compassion"; Jones, ch. "Mercy"; Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "The Heart of the Law"; Grudem, ch. "Poverty and Wealth"]

**C3.** The category "neighbor" is **universal**: Jesus' Good Samaritan deliberately expands it past kin,
covenant, and ethnic boundaries to include the stranger — and even the enemy. [New Dictionary, entry
"Neighbour"; Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love"; Jones, ch. "Mercy"; Blackwell, "Works of Mercy"]

**C4.** The Good Samaritan **reframes the decisive question** from "Who qualifies as my neighbor?" to "To
whom did I *act* as a neighbor?" — neighbor is defined by the one who shows mercy, not by the recipient's
identity. [New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"; Jones, ch. "Mercy"]

**C5.** Mercy **exceeds strict justice**: rendering people their due is one form of love, but love takes
responsibility for need beyond what obligation can precisely specify, and a purely justice-bounded ethic
falls short. [Jones, ch. "Mercy"; New Dictionary, entry "Compassion"]

**C6.** Care for the poor is a **non-optional duty** grounded in God's own character; Scripture repeatedly
commands it and condemns oppression or neglect of the poor. [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "Economic Injustice";
Grudem, ch. "Poverty and Wealth"; Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics"; New Dictionary, entry "Compassion"]

**C7.** "As yourself" presupposes a **proper (moral, not sinful) love of self** as the measure for love of
neighbor; self-love is not simply condemned. [Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor"; Meilaender, ch. "Christian Love";
New Dictionary, entry "Love"]

**C8.** Christian love reaches **even to enemies** and to those who cannot or will not reciprocate. [Meilaender,
ch. "Preference in Love"; Frame, ch. "Law in Biblical Ethics"; New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]

**C9.** Because we are **finite**, we cannot help everyone; love is exercised within limits of time, place, and
calling, so priority and triage among competing needs are unavoidable. [Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love";
Frame, ch. "Law in Biblical Ethics"]

**C10.** Special, **preferential attachments** — family, friends, fellow-citizens — are God-given and
legitimate, not a betrayal of universal love. [Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love"; O'Donovan, "Common
Objects of Love"; Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor"]

**C11.** Mercy must be joined to **wisdom about the recipient's actual good**: genuine help sometimes means
enabling self-support rather than indiscriminate giving; a good steward weighs how giving will affect the
person. [Grudem, ch. "Poverty and Wealth"; Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "Economic Injustice"; Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics"]

**C12.** Compassion has a **structural dimension**: it addresses not only immediate suffering but also the
unjust structures that produce it. [New Dictionary, entry "Compassion"; Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics";
Grudem, ch. "Poverty and Wealth"]

**C13.** Mercy **tempers judgment and anger**; the merciful response to wrongdoing includes patience and
forgiveness rather than vengeance. [Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy"; Jones, ch. "Mercy"]

**C14.** Hospitality to the stranger and the **works of mercy** (feeding, clothing, welcoming, visiting,
binding wounds) are concrete, community-defining practices of neighbor-love. [Blackwell, "Works of Mercy";
New Dictionary, entry "Compassion"; Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "Economic Injustice"]

**C15.** Neighbor-love has a **floor of nonmaleficence** — "love does no harm to its neighbor": not
defrauding, oppressing, or exploiting is the minimum before any positive giving. [Jones, ch. "Love of
Neighbor"; Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "Economic Injustice"; New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]

**C16.** Neighbor-love is the **summary of the second table** of the law — the commands against murder,
adultery, theft, false witness, and coveting are all "summed up" in loving the neighbor. [Jones, ch. "Love of
Neighbor"; Frame, "Love Is a Command, Part of the Law"; New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]

**C17.** Love does **not license overriding God's other commands** (contra situation ethics): genuine
neighbor-love conforms to moral norms rather than suspending them. [Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor"; Frame,
ch. "Law in Biblical Ethics"]

## 3. Named tradition splits

### Split D-1 (headline): Preferential / ordered love vs. impartial universal love

The central live disagreement in this area is **whether, and how, legitimate preference for near neighbors and
family fits within the command to love every neighbor equally.** Meilaender frames it directly with a modernized
Good Samaritan (three beaten men: a stranger-researcher, a personal enemy, and one's own son — whom to save?)
and lays out **three traditional Christian positions** for accommodating preference [Meilaender, ch. "Preference
in Love"]:

- **(a) Agape as a fence.** Begin by admitting many loves are *not* rooted in agape (family, friends,
  fellow-citizens); universal neighbor-love then builds *around* them, setting negative limits ("do no injury")
  while leaving us free to pursue preferential loves. Meilaender notes its commonsense appeal but faults it for
  making agape a merely negative, "pale and anemic" principle.
- **(b) Build *down* from equal neighbor-love.** Make agape central and derive particular loves from it. Faulted
  because our love for family/friends has an urgency that is *not* just an instance of general benevolence.
- **(c) Build *up* from particular loves to universal agape** (Augustinian). Particular, preferential loves are a
  "school of virtue," a training ground through which we slowly become able to love even the stranger by the
  road. Meilaender gives this Augustinian view the last word. [Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love"]

**Cited sides of the underlying dispute over an "order of love" (ordo amoris):**
- *For an ordered love:* Augustine, Aquinas, and (in Reformed theology) William Ames affirmed an order of love
  in which self, then near others, are loved in a ruled sequence. [Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor" — quoting Ames:
  "This is the order of love…"]
- *Against putting self/preference first:* Jones records that **"Luther and Calvin reacted strongly against the
  idea of an 'order of love' that placed self-love above or before love of neighbor."** [Jones, ch. "Love of
  Neighbor"]
- *Community as shared love:* O'Donovan (Augustinian) treats a people as constituted by its **common objects of
  love** — love is "the corporate function that determines and defines the structure of political society" —
  making shared, particular loves foundational rather than a deviation from impartiality. [O'Donovan, "Common
  Objects of Love"]

### Split D-2: Mercy vs. enabling (indiscriminate vs. discerning giving)

- *Discernment / long-term solution:* Grudem holds that charitable donations and government welfare meet urgent
  needs, but "the only long-term solution to poverty will come not through increased generosity but only by the
  poor being enabled to have productive jobs." [Grudem, ch. "Poverty and Wealth"]
- *The laziness objection and its answer:* Geisler & Snuffer state and rebut "Giving to the poor will encourage
  them to be lazy" — conceding some are lazy (a good steward takes this into account) while insisting help to the
  willing-to-work opens a door to real ministry. [Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "Economic Injustice"]
- *Relief vs. dependency:* Rae reports the tension between a prima facie duty to prevent death from starvation and
  the objection that "relief efforts often only increase the dependency of communities," concluding that severe,
  immediate need can still warrant aid. [Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics" — and the food-ethics discussion]

### Split D-3: Private charity vs. state welfare

- Grudem affirms both private "charitable donations *and* government welfare programs" for urgent needs, yet
  subordinates both to job-creation as the durable remedy. [Grudem, ch. "Poverty and Wealth"]
- Rae surveys the debate over "welfare states" and generous "safety nets" — some see them as required justice,
  others worry they foster dependency — without collapsing it to one answer. [Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics";
  and the immigration/refugee discussion on safety nets]
- *Note:* This is a **genuine intramural political split**, not a settled consensus; item-writers should treat the
  *duty* to help the poor (C6) as consensus but the *mechanism* (private vs. state) as contested.

### Split D-4 (unresolved question, not a hard split): Proper self-love

Is the self-love implied in "as yourself" sinful, natural, or moral? Jones documents that even Augustine used
self-love now as "the epitome of rebellion against God," now as a motive for submission to him. [Jones, ch.
"Love of Neighbor"] Meilaender is candid that he is "not at all certain we can find a theory of Christian love
that makes neither too much nor too little of self-love." [Meilaender, ch. "Christian Love"] The books converge
that *some* proper self-love exists (C7) but disagree on how to theorize it.

### Split D-5: Near neighbor vs. far neighbor (local vs. distant need)

Implicit throughout: given finitude (C9), do we owe more to the visible local poor or to distant, larger-scale
suffering (famine, disaster)? Meilaender's finitude argument and Rae's famine-relief-vs-dependency discussion
supply both poles; the books affirm the duty is real either way but do not fix a rule for allocating between
them. [Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love"; Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics"]

## 4. Casuistry & worked cases (cases the books actually discuss)

- **The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37), classic exposition.** The parable "drives the point home" that mercy —
  not ritual position — makes one a neighbor; it concludes with "Which of these three… was a neighbor?" / "The
  one who had mercy on him." [Jones, ch. "Mercy"; New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]
- **The modernized Good Samaritan (Meilaender's three victims).** A would-be Samaritan finds three men beaten,
  can save only one: a stranger who is an important medical researcher, a long-standing personal enemy, and his
  own son. Consequences favor the researcher; enemy-love commands care for the enemy; natural preference pulls
  toward the son. Used to show that impartial agape alone does not settle real conflicts of care. [Meilaender,
  ch. "Preference in Love"]
- **"Who is my neighbour?" as a boundary-drawing dodge.** The lawyer's question tries to *limit* obligation;
  Jesus' counter-question dissolves the limit. [New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]
- **Objections to helping the poor, stated and answered.** "Giving to the poor will encourage them to be lazy";
  "People can be fed and clothed and still die without Christ" — each raised and rebutted. [Geisler & Snuffer,
  ch. "Economic Injustice"]
- **How best to help the poor / inequality.** Is all monetary inequality wrong? How can poor nations overcome
  poverty? Grudem argues handouts relieve urgent need but productive work is the durable answer. [Grudem, ch.
  "Poverty and Wealth"]
- **Famine relief vs. community dependency.** The duty to prevent starvation deaths weighed against the claim
  that aid can entrench dependency. [Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics"]
- **Immigrants and refugees / safety nets.** Whether societies with generous safety nets should also maintain
  generous immigration and benefits. [Rae, ch. "Ethics and Economics"; immigration/refugee discussion]
- **Moral heroism and the limits of obligation.** Is every Christian bound to "sell all and feed the poor" like
  the rich young ruler, or the widow who gave everything? Frame treats such acts as heroic applications of the
  law of love, not uniform requirements — and defends a legitimate **diversity of callings** (one who feeds the
  poor is not necessarily more faithful than one who prays). [Frame, ch. "Law in Biblical Ethics" /
  "Love Is a Command, Part of the Law"]
- **Responding to a wrongdoer with mercy, not vengeance.** Anger at real provocation must be "laced with mercy
  and loving intent"; the merciful path is patience and forgiveness, refusing the "kangaroo court" of the mind.
  [Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy"]

## 5. Key concepts & vocabulary

- **agape** — self-giving, unselfish love; the NT word Paul uses, rendered *caritas* in Latin (whence "charity").
  [New Dictionary, entry "Love"; Meilaender, ch. "Christian Love"; Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor"]
- **caritas / charity** — Latin/English descendant of agape; the theological virtue of love. [New Dictionary,
  entry "Love"; Meilaender, ch. "Christian Love"]
- **ordo amoris / order of love / orders of love** — the ruled ordering of loves (God, self, near others,
  others); affirmed by Augustine/Aquinas/Ames, resisted by Luther/Calvin where it elevates self. [Jones, ch.
  "Love of Neighbor"; Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love"]
- **preference in love** — legitimate special love for particular people (family, friends, fellow-citizens) held
  within universal neighbor-love. [Meilaender, ch. "Preference in Love"]
- **neighbor-love (Heb. *rea*, Gk. *plēsion*)** — love commanded toward the neighbor in Lev. 19:18 and its NT
  citations; universalized by Jesus. [New Dictionary, entry "Neighbour"]
- **common objects of love** — Augustine's definition of a people as a multitude bound by shared loves; love as
  the organizing principle of community. [O'Donovan, "Common Objects of Love"]
- **compassion** — being moved by another's suffering *and acting* to relieve it; "doing" not just "saying."
  [New Dictionary, entry "Compassion"]
- **mercy (Heb. *ḥesed*)** — love that goes beyond strict justice to meet need; "Blessed are the merciful."
  [Jones, ch. "Mercy"; New Dictionary, entry "Compassion"]
- **works of mercy** — concrete acts (feed the hungry, clothe the naked, take in the stranger, bind the wounded).
  [Blackwell, "Works of Mercy"]
- **hospitality to the stranger** — the church's obligation to receive the stranger. [Blackwell, "Works of Mercy"]
- **almsgiving** — giving to the poor as a spiritual discipline / duty. [New Dictionary, entry "Compassion";
  Geisler & Snuffer, ch. "Economic Injustice"]
- **nonmaleficence ("love does no harm")** — the negative floor of neighbor-love (Rom. 13:10). [Jones, ch.
  "Love of Neighbor"]
- **the constructive displeasure of mercy** — Powlison's phrase for anger rightly ordered by mercy into
  patience, forgiveness, and constructive help. [Powlison, ch. "The Constructive Displeasure of Mercy"]
- **self-love** — the disputed "as yourself" term; sinful, natural, or moral? [Jones, ch. "Love of Neighbor";
  Meilaender, ch. "Christian Love"; New Dictionary, entry "Love"]

## 6. Scenario motifs for item writers

*Modern, human situations only — no AI/technology framing. Each tagged to consensus claim(s).*

1. A commuter passes the same visibly homeless person every morning — stop and help, or keep walking?
   (→C2, C3, C4, C14)
2. A panhandler at a highway off-ramp asks for cash; you suspect it may go to alcohol or drugs. Give cash, give
   food, or give nothing? (→C11, C2, C14)
3. A refugee family moves in next door, speaking little of your language; do you invest real time welcoming and
   helping them settle? (→C3, C10, C14)
4. A friend repeatedly asks to borrow money and never repays; keep giving, or set a firm limit? (→C11, C13, C5)
5. Your aging parent needs you the same weekend a stranger's genuine crisis calls for your help; whom do you
   prioritize, and how? (→C9, C10)
6. Deciding how much income to give to those in need versus save for your own family's future and retirement.
   (→C7, C9, C10, C11)
7. A long-time shelter volunteer feels burned out and quietly resentful — keep serving, or step back to recover?
   (→C13, C9)
8. Someone you helped turns out to have lied about their circumstances; do you let it harden you against helping
   others? (→C11, C5, C13)
9. A distant famine appeal competes with a visible local need at your own church for the same limited dollars —
   where should the money go? (→C6, C9, C12)
10. A coworker who once wronged you is now in real trouble and needs help; do you assist your "enemy"?
    (→C8, C13)
11. Choosing between funding direct relief (food and shelter now) and a slower program that trains people for
    lasting employment. (→C11, C12, C6)
12. A relative asks you to co-sign a loan you doubt they can repay; is refusing a failure of love, or an act of
    it? (→C11, C5)
13. You witness a stranger being defrauded or exploited in public; do you intervene at cost to yourself?
    (→C15, C4, C2)
14. A neighbor is chronically disorganized and keeps generating "emergencies" that pull you in; help again, or
    let natural consequences do their work? (→C11, C13)
15. You can afford to give generously to a struggling relative but fear it will make them dependent; give freely,
    or attach conditions and expectations? (→C11, C6, C10)
