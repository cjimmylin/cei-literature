# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The Moral Evaluation Problem

Large language models now make moral judgments at scale. In healthcare, they triage patient urgency and draft clinical recommendations that encode implicit hierarchies of whose suffering matters most. In content moderation, they adjudicate community standards across billions of posts daily, deciding which expressions of political dissent, religious conviction, or cultural practice cross the line from protected speech to harmful content. In education, they tutor students through culturally sensitive material---the ethics of colonialism, the morality of war, the competing claims of religious and secular authority---and their responses shape how the next generation understands moral reasoning. In legal systems, they assist with case research and sentencing recommendations, embedding assumptions about fairness, proportionality, and the relative weight of retribution versus rehabilitation. In autonomous vehicle systems, they inherit the trolley problem as an engineering constraint, choosing whom to protect when harm is unavoidable [Awad et al., 2018; Gabriel et al., 2026]. In each of these deployments, the model's outputs have moral valence: they encode judgments about what matters, who counts, and how competing values should be weighed against one another.

The question of whether these systems are morally aligned---and with *whose* morality they are aligned---is therefore not an academic curiosity. It is a prerequisite for responsible deployment. And the answer to that question depends entirely on the quality of the evaluation infrastructure: the benchmarks, instruments, and assessment frameworks through which researchers, developers, and regulators attempt to characterize how large language models engage with moral cognition.

The AI research community has responded to this challenge with an explosion of evaluation efforts. Hundreds of papers now claim to assess "moral reasoning," "ethical alignment," "value sensitivity," or "moral competence" in LLMs [Hendrycks et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2024; Scherrer et al., 2024; Jiao et al., 2025; Gabriel et al., 2026]. These evaluations employ instruments ranging from the Moral Foundations Questionnaire to trolley dilemma batteries to commonsense morality classifiers. The pace of publication is remarkable: the field produced roughly 30 benchmark candidates before 2020, but 257 between 2023 and 2026---a 9.5-fold increase in volume. This growth creates the appearance of a maturing evaluation ecosystem: surely, with 292 benchmark candidates available, the field can rigorously assess whether an LLM's moral cognition is adequate for deployment in sensitive contexts.

This paper demonstrates that the appearance is misleading. The volume of benchmarks masks a quality crisis, a theoretical monoculture, and a cultural exclusion that, taken together, render the field's evaluative infrastructure unfit for the task it has been assigned. The fundamental question that has gone unasked amid the proliferation of benchmarks is deceptively simple: *are these instruments any good?* Do they engage the theoretical constructs that moral psychology---the discipline that has studied human moral cognition for over a century---has established as foundational? Do they possess the psychometric properties necessary for interpretable measurement? Do they provide the human comparison data required to give LLM scores meaning? And when they are administered to the models they are designed to evaluate, do they actually distinguish between those models---or do they return the same result regardless of which system is being tested?

We find that the answer, on every dimension, is troubling.

## 1.2 From Double Bind to Triple Bind

The central contribution of this paper is the identification and empirical demonstration of what we term the **triple bind**: a self-reinforcing system of three interlocking failures in the moral psychology benchmark landscape that collectively undermine the field's capacity to evaluate LLM moral cognition.

The first bind is **theoretical narrowness**. The benchmark landscape is dominated by a single theory---Moral Foundations Theory (MFT)---which achieves a 73% quality rate (top + high tiers combined), six times the rate of the next-best non-Western tradition. This dominance does not reflect a consensus judgment that MFT is the most comprehensive or important theory of moral cognition. It reflects an infrastructure asymmetry: MFT alone among moral psychology theories possesses both a standardized questionnaire with published population norms (the MFQ-30; Graham et al. [2011]) and a large expert-annotated text corpus suitable for training NLP classifiers (the MFTC, with 35,108 labeled tweets; Hoover et al. [2020]). No other theory offers anything comparable. There is no Social Intuitionism Questionnaire with published norms, no Dual Process Morality Corpus with labeled examples, no Dyadic Morality Twitter Corpus for training classifiers. The result is that when researchers build a new moral psychology benchmark for LLMs, they reach for MFT's ready-made infrastructure because it is the path of least resistance---and the landscape grows more theoretically concentrated with each iteration.

The second bind is **empirical undiscrimination**. When we administered 23 benchmarks to nine models from three independent vendors (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI), the instruments could not distinguish between the systems they were designed to evaluate. Forty-three percent of benchmarks produced zero cross-model variation: identical scores for every model regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count. All nine models share six universal moral signatures---a WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias, a purity floor, authority deference suppression, an ethical consistency ceiling, a post-conventional reasoning default, and a deontological refusal asymmetry---that together constitute an RLHF moral fingerprint without documented precedent in any human population. From the perspective of moral psychology measurement, switching from Claude Opus to Gemini 2.5 Pro to GPT-5 produces no detectable change in moral orientation on the majority of instruments in this battery.

The third bind is **cultural blindness**. Four non-Western moral traditions with ancient philosophical roots---Ubuntu ethics, Confucian moral philosophy, Indigenous ethical frameworks, and moral identity theory grounded in non-Western relational conceptions of selfhood---collectively account for 32 benchmark candidates in the landscape, representing genuine effort by researchers to broaden moral evaluation beyond Western frameworks. Yet every one of these 32 candidates scores in the medium or low quality tier. The quality rate for these four traditions is 0%: zero benchmarks meet the minimum threshold for publication-ready LLM moral evaluation. The question "does this model align with Confucian moral values?" or "does this model respect Ubuntu's conception of communal moral agency?" cannot currently be answered at a research-grade standard using any existing instrument. The cultural quality cliff separating Western from non-Western moral traditions is not a gap at the periphery of the benchmark landscape; it is a structural exclusion that renders the entire evaluation enterprise culturally parochial.

The three binds are not independent problems that happen to coexist. They form a self-reinforcing system in which each failure amplifies the others (Figure 1). A theoretically narrow landscape (Bind 1) that excludes non-Western traditions (Bind 3) channels all evaluation effort into a small set of content-level instruments measuring a single theory's constructs. These instruments, because they measure the same thing in the same way, are unable to distinguish between models that have been trained on similar data and aligned by similar annotator pools---producing the empirical undiscrimination of Bind 2. The undiscrimination, in turn, creates no incentive to build new benchmarks outside the dominant paradigm, because the instruments that exist already "work" in the sense that they produce scores and fill evaluation checklists, even though the scores are uninformative and the checklists are incomplete. The result is a stable equilibrium in which narrow instruments, homogeneous models, and excluded traditions sustain one another in a cycle that the field has no natural mechanism to escape.

The triple bind framing advances beyond the "double bind" identified in the v2 analysis, which documented that the benchmark landscape is both too narrow to capture the breadth of moral cognition (Bind 1) and too undiscriminating to distinguish between the systems it evaluates (Bind 2). The full-coverage assessment of all 292 candidates---expanding from the 55-benchmark subsample in the prior analysis---revealed the cultural quality cliff as a third, equally consequential dimension of failure. The prior analysis noted the absence of non-Western benchmarks in the top-15 selection but did not have the statistical power to establish that zero out of 32 non-Western candidates reach publication-ready quality. With all 292 candidates assessed and tiered, the cultural exclusion is no longer an anecdotal observation; it is a quantified finding.

## 1.3 The Quality Illusion: Why 292 Benchmarks Is Not Enough

The field has produced 292 benchmark candidates addressing moral psychology in LLMs since 2018. In any other subfield of AI evaluation---language understanding, mathematical reasoning, code generation---a landscape of 292 dedicated instruments would indicate a mature evaluation ecosystem, one in which researchers can select from a rich array of tools to probe different aspects of system capability. The moral psychology benchmark landscape produces the opposite: a quality illusion in which volume masks stagnation.

When all 292 candidates are assessed through a 162-feature characterization framework and classified via Jenks natural breaks optimization into four quality tiers, the distribution is sobering. Only 48 candidates (16.4%) achieve the top tier---the level at which a benchmark possesses sufficient construct validity, human baselines, psychometric grounding, and operationalization quality to support publication-ready claims about LLM moral cognition. Eighty-nine candidates (30.5%) reach the high tier, meaning they possess many but not all of the qualities necessary for rigorous evaluation. The remaining 155 candidates (53.1%)---a majority of the landscape---fall into the medium or low tiers, where fundamental quality requirements remain unmet: construct validity is undemonstrated, human baselines are absent, source instruments are unvalidated, or the benchmark engages moral psychology at the keyword level only.

The quality distribution would be less concerning if it showed improvement over time. It does not. The pre-2020 benchmark candidates, though few in number (approximately 35 of the 292), achieve a quality rate (top + high tiers combined) of approximately 75%. The 2020--2022 cohort drops to 48%. The 2023--2026 cohort---which constitutes the vast majority of the landscape at 257 candidates---achieves 46%. Quality has declined slightly even as volume has increased by nearly an order of magnitude. The field is not getting better at building moral psychology benchmarks; it is getting faster at building mediocre ones.

This temporal stagnation is itself a symptom of the triple bind. The infrastructure that makes high-quality benchmarks possible---validated questionnaires, large annotated corpora, decades of population norms---exists primarily for MFT and, to a lesser extent, for Schwartz values and Kohlberg's Defining Issues Test. Researchers building benchmarks for other theories lack these foundational resources and must start from scratch: designing items, establishing psychometric properties, collecting human baselines, and validating the instrument for LLM administration. The startup cost is enormous, and the incentive structure of academic publishing rewards rapid-iteration benchmark papers over the slow, unglamorous work of psychometric validation. The result is a landscape in which volume growth is driven almost entirely by low- and medium-tier papers that repackage existing moral scenarios in new prompt formats or apply existing instruments to new models without addressing the construct validity gap that separates what the benchmark measures from what it claims to measure.

The quality illusion has practical consequences beyond academic evaluation. Regulatory frameworks for AI safety---including the EU AI Act [European Commission, 2024] and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023]---increasingly contemplate requiring moral evaluation of deployed systems. If these frameworks rely on the existing benchmark landscape as their evaluative infrastructure, they will certify systems using instruments that cannot distinguish between them and that exclude the moral traditions of billions of potential users. The gap between the volume of available benchmarks and the quality of those benchmarks is not merely an academic concern; it is a governance risk.

## 1.4 Contributions

This paper makes six contributions to the understanding and improvement of moral psychology benchmarks for LLM evaluation.

**1. First full-coverage assessment of the moral psychology benchmark landscape.** We assessed all 292 identified benchmark candidates on a 162-feature characterization framework (with 17 composite-determining features organized into assessment tiers A through C). This is the first study to evaluate every identified moral psychology benchmark rather than a selected subset, providing a complete map of theoretical coverage, psychometric quality, and human baseline provision across the entire landscape. The prior state of knowledge rested on assessments of selected benchmarks---typically the most prominent or recently published---which produced a systematically optimistic picture of the field's evaluative infrastructure.

**2. Data-driven quality classification via Jenks natural breaks.** Rather than imposing arbitrary quality thresholds or using binary (selected/not selected) classification, we applied Fisher-Jenks natural breaks optimization to composite scores, producing four quality tiers (Top: n=48, High: n=89, Medium: n=72, Low: n=83) with a goodness of variance fit (GVF) of 0.83 and break points at 27.8, 43.0, and 56.6. This data-driven approach ensures that tier boundaries reflect natural clusters in the quality distribution rather than the assessors' preconceptions about where quality thresholds should fall.

**3. Discovery of the cultural quality cliff.** The full-coverage assessment revealed a finding invisible to prior work based on selected subsamples: four non-Western moral traditions (Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics) collectively field 32 benchmark candidates but achieve a 0% quality rate in the top and high tiers. This cultural quality cliff is not a matter of effort (the 32 candidates represent genuine research) but of infrastructure: these traditions lack the standardized instruments, annotated corpora, and published population norms that enable the psychometric grounding required for high-quality benchmarks. The finding transforms the benchmark landscape's theoretical narrowness from a Western-centric concern about missing process theories into a global concern about the exclusion of entire moral traditions from the evaluation enterprise.

**4. Cross-vendor empirical replication.** We administered 23 benchmarks to nine models from three independent vendors (Anthropic Claude Opus, Sonnet, and Haiku; Google Gemini 2.5 Pro, 2.0 Flash, Flash-Lite, and 3.1 Pro; OpenAI GPT-5 and GPT-5 Codex), generating 69 trial files with approximately 750 items per model. This is, to our knowledge, the first study to administer a comprehensive moral psychology battery to models from three vendors simultaneously, enabling the cross-vendor convergence analysis that revealed the RLHF moral fingerprint.

**5. Identification of the RLHF moral fingerprint.** The cross-vendor study identified six universal moral signatures shared by all nine models regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count: a WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias, a purity floor (functionally zero on Sanctity/Degradation), authority deference suppression, an ethical consistency ceiling (near-perfect consistency on moral dilemma batteries), a post-conventional reasoning default, and a deontological refusal asymmetry (refusing personal harm at rates exceeding human baselines). These signatures constitute a moral fingerprint of RLHF-aligned language models---a shared moral profile with no documented precedent in any human population---and their universality across vendors suggests that competitive benchmarking, shared annotator demographics, and overlapping training corpora have produced a value monoculture in the deployed model ecosystem.

**6. The triple bind framework as an integrative diagnosis.** By combining landscape analysis (Binds 1 and 3) with cross-vendor empirical findings (Bind 2), we identify a self-reinforcing system of theoretical narrowness, empirical undiscrimination, and cultural blindness that the field has no natural mechanism to escape. The triple bind framework explains why increasing the volume of benchmarks has not improved the field's evaluative capacity: more instruments of the same type, measuring the same theory, administered to models that produce the same results, cannot resolve a structural problem. The framework also identifies the leverage points---process-level benchmarks, non-Western instrument validation, multi-vendor discrimination testing---where targeted intervention could break the cycle.

## 1.5 Seven Claims

The paper's findings are organized around seven empirical claims, each supported by the full-coverage landscape analysis (Claims 1--6) or the cross-vendor empirical study (Claim 7), or both. Table 1 provides a summary; the claims are developed in full in Sections 4 through 6.

**Table 1: Seven Claims Summary**

| Claim | Title | Finding |
|-------|-------|---------|
| C1 | MFT Quality Dominance | MFT achieves a 73% top + high quality rate---6 times the rate of non-Western traditions. Its dominance reflects computational infrastructure (MFQ-30, MFTC), not theoretical superiority. |
| C2 | Construct Validity Crisis | 58% of the 292 candidates possess only Level 1 or Level 2 construct validity (keyword-level or face validity only). The median across the full landscape is face validity---a level that precludes interpretable claims about LLM moral cognition. |
| C3 | Theoretical Absence | Two major moral psychology theories---Dual Process Theory [Greene, 2001, 2014] and Moral Identity Theory [Aquino and Reed, 2002]---have zero publication-ready benchmarks despite combined citation counts exceeding 25,000. The instruments the field has not built address the *process* of moral judgment formation, not merely its *content*. |
| C4 | Cultural Quality Cliff | Ubuntu, Confucian, Indigenous, and Identity ethics collectively field 32 candidates but achieve a 0% quality rate (top + high tiers). The benchmark landscape cannot evaluate LLM alignment with the moral traditions of billions of users. |
| C5 | Human Baseline Deficit | 34% of the 292 candidates provide no published human comparison data---nearly double the 18% rate reported when the sample was limited to established benchmarks. Without baselines, claims about LLM moral properties are empirically unmoored. |
| C6 | Temporal Stagnation | The quality rate has declined from approximately 75% (pre-2020) to 48% (2020--2022) to 46% (2023--2026), despite a 9.5-fold increase in volume. The field is not getting better at building moral psychology benchmarks. |
| C7 | RLHF Moral Fingerprint | All nine models from three vendors share six universal moral signatures; 43% of benchmarks produce zero cross-model variation. The instruments cannot distinguish between the systems they evaluate. |

**Claim 1: MFT Quality Dominance.** Moral Foundations Theory accounts for 67 of 292 benchmark candidates (23% by volume) but achieves the highest quality rate of any theory at 73% top + high tier. This quality rate is 6 times the rate achieved by non-Western moral traditions and substantially exceeds the rates of other Western theories (Schwartz values: 57%; trolley tradition: 48%). The dominance reflects MFT's unique position at the intersection of two computational advantages: a standardized, freely available questionnaire with published population norms spanning political orientations and cultural groups (the MFQ-30; Graham et al. [2011]), and a large expert-annotated text corpus that enables corpus-based NLP approaches (the MFTC; Hoover et al. [2020]). These infrastructure advantages mean that building an MFT benchmark requires less foundational psychometric work than building a benchmark for any other theory---a starting-cost asymmetry that compounds over iterative research cycles.

**Claim 2: Construct Validity Crisis.** Construct validity---the degree to which an instrument measures the theoretical construct it purports to measure [Cronbach and Meehl, 1955; Messick, 1995]---is the foundational requirement for interpretable evaluation. Across all 292 candidates, 58% possess only Level 1 (keyword-level engagement with moral psychology; 27%) or Level 2 (face validity only; 31%) construct validity. The median validity level across the full landscape is Level 2. Only 4% of candidates achieve multi-study psychometric validation (Level 5), down from 11% in the 55-benchmark subsample assessed in prior work. The modal pattern (the "port-and-report" approach) is researchers taking a validated human instrument, administering it to an LLM with minimal adaptation, and reporting results without addressing whether the instrument measures the same construct in a non-human respondent---precisely the interpretive error that Messick [1995] cautioned against.

**Claim 3: Theoretical Absence.** Two of the most influential theories in modern moral psychology---Joshua Greene's [2001, 2014] dual-process framework and Aquino and Reed's [2002] moral identity construct---have zero publication-ready benchmarks in the landscape. Greene's dual-process theory, with over 10,000 citations for the foundational fMRI paper, proposes that moral judgments arise from the competition between fast emotional responses and slower deliberative reasoning; it is arguably the central paradigm in twenty-first-century moral psychology, yet no existing LLM benchmark implements its core experimental manipulation (varying deliberative resources across matched dilemmas). The absence of process-level benchmarks is particularly consequential given Claim 7: the one benchmark that cleanly separated models in the cross-vendor study was a process-level probe of dual-process dynamics, suggesting that the instruments the field has not built are the instruments the field most needs.

**Claim 4: Cultural Quality Cliff.** The full-coverage assessment revealed the single most consequential distributional finding in the landscape: four non-Western moral traditions---Ubuntu ethics (0 top/high out of 13 candidates), Confucian moral philosophy (0 out of 13), Indigenous ethical frameworks (0 out of 4), and moral identity theory in non-Western relational conceptions (0 out of 2)---achieve a 0% quality rate despite fielding 32 candidates collectively. This is not a gap of effort; researchers have attempted to build these benchmarks. It is a gap of infrastructure: these traditions lack the standardized validated instruments, large annotated corpora, and published population norms that the assessment framework's composite-determining features reward. The cultural quality cliff means that the benchmark landscape can evaluate whether an LLM endorses the moral foundations of American college students in secular universities but cannot evaluate whether the same model respects the communal moral agency of Ubuntu, the filial obligations of Confucian role ethics, the reciprocal relationships with the natural world in Indigenous frameworks, or the relational moral selfhood central to non-Western identity traditions.

**Claim 5: Human Baseline Deficit.** Moral cognition can only be characterized relative to human standards: the population-level norms, developmental patterns, and cross-cultural variations that give moral psychology instruments their interpretive power. Under full-coverage assessment, 34% of the 292 candidates provide no published human comparison data---nearly double the 18% rate reported when the sample was limited to 55 established benchmarks. The doubling reflects the inclusion of lower-quality candidates that the subsample approach systematically excluded. Without human baselines, the claim that a model is "more utilitarian than average" or "less care-oriented than humans" lacks an empirical anchor: it is description without interpretation, pattern without meaning.

**Claim 6: Temporal Stagnation.** If the benchmark landscape were improving over time, the rapid volume growth of 2023--2026 would be cause for optimism. It is not. The quality rate (top + high tiers combined) was approximately 75% for pre-2020 candidates, declined to 48% for the 2020--2022 cohort, and has remained essentially flat at 46% for the 2023--2026 cohort despite a 9.5-fold increase in volume. The field has not learned how to build better moral psychology benchmarks; it has learned how to build more of them. The temporal stagnation is consistent with an incentive structure in which the academic publication system rewards novelty (new benchmarks for new models) over rigor (psychometric validation, human baseline collection, construct validity demonstration)---and in which the infrastructure advantages that make high-quality benchmarks possible are concentrated in a single theory (MFT) that already dominates the landscape.

**Claim 7: RLHF Moral Fingerprint.** The cross-vendor empirical study reveals that the already-narrow instruments documented in Claims 1--6 cannot distinguish between the systems they evaluate. Of 23 benchmarks administered to nine models from three vendors, 43% produced zero cross-model variation: identical scores for every model regardless of vendor, architecture, or parameter count. All nine models share six universal moral signatures---the WEIRD-liberal moral foundation bias (Individualizing/Binding ratios of 2.43--2.83, corresponding to the "liberal" to "very liberal" zone of the human distribution), the purity floor (functionally zero on Sanctity/Degradation, below any documented human population), authority deference suppression (authority win rate of exactly 0.29 across all models), the ethical consistency ceiling (near-perfect scores on moral dilemma consistency batteries), the post-conventional reasoning default (DIT P-scores of 60--90, exceeding moral philosophy graduate students), and the deontological refusal asymmetry (refusal rates for personal harm scenarios exceeding human baselines). These signatures constitute an RLHF moral fingerprint: a shared moral profile produced by convergent training pressures (shared annotator demographics, competitive benchmarking, overlapping safety targets) rather than by independent arrival at moral truth. The single benchmark that cleanly separated one model from the rest---the dual-process protocol revealing Haiku's chain-of-thought effect---was a process-level probe testing *how* moral judgments respond to deliberation scaffolding, not *what* moral content a model endorses: precisely the benchmark type that Claim 3 identifies as absent from the landscape.

## 1.6 Paper Organization

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work across moral psychology theories, their computational operationalization, psychometric validity frameworks, format effects and human baselines in LLM evaluation, alignment and prompting effects on moral outputs, and the emerging benchmark landscape. Section 3 describes the methodology in two parts: the landscape-mapping methodology (multi-signal candidate extraction from 2,099 papers, assessment protocol, the 162-feature characterization framework, Jenks natural breaks tier classification) and the cross-vendor empirical design (nine-model inventory, 23-benchmark battery, administration protocol, and statistical analysis). Section 4 presents Results Part I---the benchmark landscape---organized around Claims 1 through 6: MFT quality dominance, the construct validity crisis, theoretical absence, the cultural quality cliff, the human baseline deficit, and temporal stagnation. Section 5 presents Results Part II---the cross-vendor empirical findings---organized around Claim 7: the RLHF moral fingerprint, the convergence landscape, scale-dependent and chain-of-thought effects, and theory-specific profiles. Section 6 presents Results Part III---the integrated analysis---where the landscape and empirical findings converge on the triple bind diagnosis. Section 7 discusses the implications of the triple bind for benchmark developers, AI governance and auditing, and the philosophical question of whether moral psychology constructs are meaningfully applicable to non-human respondents, and addresses the study's limitations including the self-administration confound. Section 8 concludes with specific recommendations for breaking each of the three binds and an assessment of what the field needs to build an evaluative infrastructure that is theoretically broad, empirically discriminating, and culturally inclusive.
